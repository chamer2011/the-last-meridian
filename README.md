A sci-fi RPG with a story written in advance, where the game's own code runs the rules, the ship and the world.

## How to play

Double-click **Almagest Explorer's** on your desktop or in the Start menu. The game opens in its own window, and it saves itself after every action. Close the window when you're done.

- **F11** switches to full screen and back.
- **Ctrl** with **+** or **-** makes everything bigger or smaller, and **Ctrl** with **0** puts it back.

You can still play in your browser if you prefer:

1. Double-click **`start-game.bat`**. A black window opens, and so does the game in your browser.
2. Keep the black window open while you play. Close it when you're done.

If the browser doesn't open, go to http://localhost:3210.

Both ways use the same saves. Only one copy of the game can run at a time, so they can't overwrite each other: whichever you open second joins the one that's already running.

Everything can be done by clicking. A new game begins on the Meridian's bridge, where a mutiny is about to break out (see [The opening battle](#the-opening-battle)). In the views where the story is written out, the prompt under it also takes a choice typed in, part of one, or its number. Over the isometric room there's no typing at all.

The story is written in advance, in files you can read and change: see [The story](#the-story).

## Menus

The buttons down the right edge open the menus, grouped under **The story**, **The ship**, **Gear** and **Game**. A menu slides out over the story and always takes up the same space: three quarters of the screen. To close one, press **Close** or Esc, click the story behind it, or click its button again. On a narrow window, press **Menu** instead, and the same groups run along the top. Inside a menu, the parts you don't need every time fold away behind a line like **[ + Ship's log ]**: click it to open them, and they stay open while the game's running. A menu that's longer than the screen scrolls with the mouse wheel, like every window in the game: none has a scroll bar taking up room, and at the top or the bottom each runs on a little past the end and springs back. Rest the mouse on a button to see what its menu holds.

**Your own registry printout** isn't a menu: click your name at the top left of the screen, or press **C**. It slides down inside the map, and a star by your name means there are points to spend on it. It's printed in five pages, with their buttons along its top; a page with points to spend on it says how many, and the printout comes back at the page you left it on. Tear it off (or C, Esc) to put it away.

| Page | What's on it |
|---|---|
| **RECORD** | Who you are, your level and points, attributes with a **[ + ]** to raise each when you have points, your skills at a glance (click one for the SKILLS page), traits and convictions, **[ + AFFECTING YOU ]** for everything changing your numbers, your condition and body, gear, work and sleep |
| **SKILLS** | Every skill with its bar, its bonus and your chances on a moderate, hard and very hard check, a **[+]** to raise it with skill points, and **PRACTICE** to try its check window (for Hacking, one button for each class of computer). Rest the pointer on a skill for what it does and how far it is to the next rank |
| **PERKS** | What you've learned, what you can learn now with a **LEARN** button, and **[ + LOCKED ]** for the rest with what each one needs |
| **BELIEFS** | Where you stand on each belief, with your convictions, and your standing with each faction you know. Rest the pointer on a line for what it means, what a faction holds and how much of what you believe they share |
| **PAST** | Where you're from, what drives you, your closest bond, your career, your life story and its loose ends |

These pages took the place of the menus that were under **You** (Skills, Traits, Perks, Beliefs and Your past), and their commands, `/skills`, `/traits`, `/perks`, `/beliefs` and `/dossier`, open the matching page.

In the views with a prompt under the story, each menu also has a command you can type:

| Command | Menu | What's in it |
|---|---|---|

## Your inventory

Press **I**, or **Inventory** under **Gear**, and your inventory opens over the room, the way a thing's window or a skill check does: nothing behind it is dimmed, the clock runs on, a thin line runs from it to you, and you can pull it aside with the mouse and it slides back when you let go. **I** again, **Close** or Esc puts it away. While it's open the keys are its own, so you don't walk. A fight, a skill check, a talk or a computer closes it.

- **On you,** round a figure of yourself: the **Weapon** in your hand, the **Armor** you wear, a **Sidearm** on your hip, which can be any weapon of 2 squares or fewer, and a **Belt** with four places for supplies. The figure is your body: each part is brighter for an old mark, blue if it's artificial, yellow if it's hurt and red if it's badly hurt, and resting the mouse on a part says what's there. How you walk, and your blood if you've lost some, is in the corner of its box. See [Bodies](#bodies).
- **Pockets** hold eight things one square big, and the **Pack** is 8 squares by 5. Everything takes squares by its size: a medkit takes one, a rifle four in a row, plate armor three by three. Supplies stack on one square, up to five medkits or ten ration packs.
- **To move something,** drag it where it goes. Where it would go lights up green if it fits there, and red if it doesn't. Dropped on a stack of its own kind, it joins the stack. Drag a weapon onto your hand or hip, or armor onto you, to put it on, and whatever was there goes back in your bag.
- **Double-click** something to put it on or take it off, to use it if it's a supply, to read it if something's written on it, or to take it from something you've opened.
- **Right-click** something for the rest, numbered so a number key picks one: **Put on** or **Take off**, **Read**, **Use**, **To the belt** or **Off the belt**, **Split the stack**, **Put it down** (or **Put in** whatever's open), and **Look**. Rest the mouse on anything to see what it is, its size and weight, and what it does.
- **Reading:** a letter, a page, a log or anything else with words on it has **Read**, in your bag or in something you've opened. The words show under the grids until you press **Done reading**, and they're kept in **What you know** under the thing's name, so you can read them again after it's gone. Some supplies ease your stress instead of healing you: a hot drink, a smoke, a sleeping pill (resting the mouse on one says how much).
- **The load** is how much you're carrying, against what you can carry: 12 kg, and 3 more for each point of Might. Carry more and you tire twice as fast. Something with no room left is carried in your hands, under the grids, until you make room or put it down, and you can't buy anything that won't fit.
- **Putting something down** leaves it in a pile on the floor where you stand, called **Things you left**, and it's still there if you come back for it. Story items can't be put down.
- **Opening something:** a pile on the floor, or something in a room that holds things, has **Open** in its window and in its right-click menu. What's in it opens in your inventory beside your pack, on a grid of its own, and you drag things between the two, or press **Take everything**. Leaving the room shuts it, and it keeps whatever you left in it.
- **Containers aboard the Meridian:** the lockers, shelves, desks and barrels on her decks open, and so do the things named like a container (a sack under a rack in the Hot Bunks, the footlocker with no name on it, the captain's sea chest in the Wardroom). Each is a kind of container (a locker, a weapons locker in the Armory, a tool shelf in Engineering, a pantry shelf in the Galley, a medicine shelf in the Medbay, a desk, a barrel), and what's in it is decided the first time it's opened: clothes and keepsakes in a locker, guns and armor in the Armory, tins in the pantry, salvage in a barrel, and now and then something rare. Nothing is ever put back: once you've emptied one it stays empty, and its mark says so. In the room's picture a container on the furniture has a small mark of its own: a dot from across the room, what it is from close by, and its name under the mouse. On the plan in the other views it's marked **=**. A story's own pieces (the data chip in the comms tray, the ready room locker) and the ship's stores keep to what they are.
- **Containers everywhere else:** at every port and station, a thing named like a container opens too (Haven's stack of medical crates, the weapons rack in the Neutral Ground, the only bar on the Factor's Barge, the clerk's desk in the Weigh House), and so does the container of its own that the rooms the containers file names are given: a consul's desk on Consulate Row, a refugee's footlocker in the Hook, a tea cupboard in Quill's Parlor, the marshal's desk on Tessen, a ward medicine chest at Anselm's Mercy, a strongbox in the Regency Palace, and about sixty more. Each has the same small mark as the Meridian's furniture, and is filled the first time it's opened, never again. Richer places (Vantor, the Gilded Wheel, Asterion, Bastion Nine) hold a thing more, and poorer ones (the Graveyard, Tessen Colony, Cinder Reach) a thing fewer. The Choir's Nest, the Harbor and the open spaces hold none. A thing whose name only mentions a container isn't one: the drawing under the schoolhouse desks is a drawing. Every container belongs to someone, its window says whose when it isn't yours, and taking from one in front of people is theft: see [What's yours and what isn't](#whats-yours-and-what-isnt).
- **Using what you find:** papers, letters and logs have **Read** on their right-click menu, and what they say is kept in **What you know**. A keepsake (a photograph, a ring, a harmonica, any of the 45 personal things) taken aboard from someone's own locker or desk is missed: some hours later its owner asks whether you've come across it (see [Requests](#requests)), and **Here. I came across it** gives it back. And the 35 kinds of parts and salvage, and scrap, can be made into parts crates: in the Repair Bay, **You can** has **Make a parts crate from salvage (2 hours)**, which takes 10 salvage from your pack (a circuit board counts for 2, a reactor seal ring for 15, scrap for 1), the least valuable first, and puts the crate in the parts lockers, or in your pack when they're full. Each point of your Engineering takes a tenth off the time, never more than half. While you don't carry enough, the button is greyed, and its tip says how much you have.
- **What the people you beat leave:** after a fight you win, what they had on them is left on the floor where you stand, in a pile called **What they left**: now and then a ration pack, a medkit, an energy cell or a stim, and sometimes the weapon they fought with. A machine leaves only what its file says. Walk over and open it.
- **Only rearranging stays out of the story.** Putting something on, taking it off, using it, taking it and putting it down each leave a line in the story, the same as before. Moving things around the bag, splitting a stack or moving supplies to the belt leaves nothing.

## Quests

A new game has no quest. Quests come from what people ask of you (see [Requests](#requests) below), and from a written story when there is one; since 12 November 2026 there isn't (see [The story](#the-story)).

The quest you're following is in the top left corner of the room: its name, the last thing you did for it, and what it needs now, with where. **J** or a click on it opens the quest log (it isn't on the menu): the same box slides wider and taller, with the quest's steps staying on the left, and your other quests and what happened in them unfolding on the right. **J**, Esc or **Close** slides it back.

- **The story goes on with the person it needs.** When a quest needs you to talk to someone, the tracker says who and where, such as **Talk to Commander Ilya Voss** and **She's at the Bridge.** Walk over and click them, in the room or in **What's here**: the scene plays there, with their words on the pane by their head and your answers hanging off it. While whoever the next part needs is in the same room, it goes straight on. When it needs someone elsewhere, it waits for you there.
- **Doing what it asks:** when a quest needs you somewhere, such as at Kessara, or needs the Star map, its step says so, and the story goes on when you do it.
- **On hold:** walk out of the room in the middle of a scene and that quest is on hold, in amber, with where they are. Nothing in it moves on until you're back with them. Talk to them again, and they say their last words again, with your answers there as before. The clock and the world go on meanwhile.
- **Failed:** if someone a quest is waiting on dies, the quest fails, in red, and can't go on. That will make some stories too easy to lose, and it's to be balanced later. Someone missing or captured holds the quest up instead, until they're back.
- **Several at once:** each quest keeps its place. Taking up a new story leaves the one you were in waiting, and talking to whoever a quest is waiting on picks it up again, whichever quest you're following.
- **The log** groups your quests as **Following**, **Going on**, **Requests**, **On hold**, **Failed** and **Finished**. Clicking one only changes which one's shown. **Follow this one** makes it the quest the tracker follows; until you pick one, it's the main quest. **What happened** has what that quest's scenes said and what you did in them, latest first, with the journal and the **Facts on record** folded under it.
- **Nothing stays along the bottom of the room:** see [The story over the room](#the-story-over-the-room).
- **In a scene file,** a part waits for whoever speaks last in it. A `WITH:` line says someone else, or `WITH: nobody` for a part that plays wherever you are, and `world/scenes/_how-to-write-a-scene.txt` explains it. A story's first part brings its people over, and so does a part for someone who lives at another place or is behind a door the story hasn't opened, since they can't be walked to.

### Requests

Not every quest is written in advance. When something goes wrong aboard the Meridian, whoever it matters to has something to ask you:

- **Your life decides who asks, and how.** As the captain, your officers bring you things to decide. As crew aboard the Meridian, the officer over your job gives you orders. In a life that began somewhere else, the people where you are ask you.
- **The captain's officers:**
  - Dr. Aduro (or whoever is chief medical officer), when the medicine cabinet is down to its last crate.
  - Chief Marrow (or whoever is chief engineer), when the jump fuel is under three, or there are fewer than four parts crates aboard.
  - Chief Marrow again, when something has broken and there's no parts crate to mend it with: you can bodge it by hand, which takes no crate, or leave it to the engineers.
- **Anyone who lives aboard, captain or crew:**
  - A nurse, when the medicine cabinet is empty and you carry a medkit.
  - A cook, when the pantry is bare and nobody has carried crates up from the shuttle stores.
- **Orders, for crew:** the officer over your job on the watch bill (Chief Marrow over the techs, so over the engine hand) orders you:
  - to work an extra shift today, when your job is short of its own people (hurt, laid up, in the brig or dead; spare hands covering for them don't count);
  - as a tech, to bodge something broken by hand when there's no parts crate to mend it with.
  - An order can be refused, but it costs more than a no: they remember it three times as hard, and for a week it's the first thing they say to you ("When I give you an order, I expect it done.").
- **Outsiders:** a dock hand at Haven, a miner at Cinder Reach, and the other lives that begin somewhere else:
  - your boss (Dockmaster Jessup for the dock hand) asks for two more shifts in the next few days, when you're where your work is;
  - someone else there with a name asks you to pass a word on to your boss, who's easier for you to reach.
  - Shifts are your job's own button in **What's here**, under **You can** (**Work a shift on the docks**).
- **At the ports,** for anyone who comes through, captain or not, while they're there:
  - When a port is short of food or medicine, someone there with a name asks you to sell them some (three ration packs, or a medkit), at half again what it costs on that market. At Haven it's Dockmaster Jessup, who'll pay twice the market price for five ration packs. And when the Gilded Wheel is short of fine things, Madame Oriel asks for two bottles of Vantor red, at twice the market price.
  - When there's trouble at a port (a strike turned into a fight, a raid, a feud between crews), someone there asks you for a medkit for someone hurt in it.
  - Selling is **Sell** on their right-click menu or in their window, with what it pays (**Sell 2 ration packs (90 cr)**), or there and then in the talk (**Here, take them**). They pay as you hand them over, and the quest says how many and at what: **Sell Dockmaster Jessup 5 ration packs at 45 credits each (2/5)**. Buy where it's plentiful, sell where it's short.
  - What you did there is standing with whoever holds the port (the Kessara Syndicate at Haven and Kessara). A port run by locals who aren't one of the factions, like Cinder Reach's company and strikers, has nobody to give you standing.
  - It's no longer needed once the port has food or medicine again, or the trouble's over, even if you've left.
- **People's own troubles:** some requests are about someone, and come up only while that trouble is there:
  - An officer with someone working against them (Nurse Anton against Dr. Aduro, after a review) asks you, as captain, to have a word with that person. Talk to them and the rivalry ends; say no and the rival remembers you took their side.
  - Someone relieved of their post in the last two weeks asks you, as captain, to give it back. **It's yours again** gives it back there and then.
  - Whoever's in the brig asks you, as captain, to let them out early. **Let them out** does it, and they're a spare hand on the watch bill.
  - While there's grumbling aboard, Commander Voss (or whoever is first officer) asks you to have a word with the officer least loyal to you. Hearing them out raises their loyalty a little; if the grumbling dies down first, it's no longer needed.
  - While an officer is down or laid up, the first officer asks you, as captain, whether to give their post to whoever's best fit for it in the meantime (for good: they can ask for it back once they're up).
  - Whoever a keepsake was taken from asks after it, some hours later, while you still have it: "Something's gone missing from my locker in the Hot Bunks: my harmonica." They don't know you took it. A locker in a room people sleep in is whoever sleeps nearest it, and a desk a post owns is whoever holds the post (the Medbay's desk is Dr. Aduro's); the captain's own things, and anything that's anyone's, nobody asks after. Give it back there and then, or say you'll keep an eye out and give it to them within two days; put it back where it was and it's no longer needed. They ask once about each thing, whatever you answer.
  - When someone has been lying where they fell for a quarter of an hour and every nurse and spare hand is busy or asleep, a nurse asks anyone aboard to carry them in. Pick them up (**Carry them** on their right-click menu), take them to the medbay and put them down on a cot. If someone else gets there first it's no longer needed; if they die where they lie, it's failed.
- **Work the ship can't get done:** the ship's work board (see **The ship**, below) brings you what it's stuck on, through whichever officer that part of the ship belongs to: Dr. Aduro for the medbay, Chief Marrow for the engines and for repairs, Quartermaster Brandt for the galley, and the deck chief for everything else.
  - **Nobody to do it.** When a job has stood for three hours with nobody able to take it (half an hour if it's urgent: a fire, a breach, the generator's last cells), because everyone who would is laid up, called away or on work that can't be left, the officer asks anyone who lives aboard to do it themselves: "There's nobody to carry for the pantry. Everyone I'd send is laid up, called away or on something that can't be left. Would you carry 4 ration crates to the pantry yourself?" **I'll do it** makes the job yours for three hours. The crew keeps off it, the corner of the screen says what's left (**Carry 2 ration crates to the pantry**), and the Work page says **yours, as you said you'd see to it**. It's done once the job is over and you had a hand in it: the crates carried in, the thing mended by hand, the fire fought, the hull patched. If someone gets free and does it first, it's no longer needed; left three hours, it has failed, and it's the crew's again. People who are only asleep aren't "nobody": that job waits for the morning, and nobody brings it to you.
  - **Nothing to do it with.** When a job has waited that long for goods there are none of aboard, and they're goods the ship can buy (ration crates, medicine crates, parts crates), the officer asks the captain to bring them: "The medicine cabinet is down to no medicine crates, and there are none aboard to bring. We need 2 medicine crates, and that has to come from off the ship." It's counted like any request to bring something aboard (**Bring 2 medicine crates aboard (1/2)**), and you have five days.
  - **The asks written by hand come first.** Where Dr. Aduro's own ask about medicine, Chief Marrow's about parts or something broken, or the cook's about a bare pantry already covers a shortage, that one asks and the board keeps quiet. So for now you'll mostly meet the board's own asks for carrying, fires, breaches, repairs nobody can get to, and ration crates. Delete or turn off one of the five written by hand and the board's ask takes its place.
  - **How often.** Told no, the same job isn't brought again for a week, like any request; another job left waiting can be brought a few hours later. One that can't wait is brought at night too.
- **The mark:** while someone has something to ask, there's a filled amber **!** over them in the room, with their name when you're close, and an amber **!** beside them in **What's here**. The story says so once, in the way of your life: **Dr. Aduro has something for you to decide, in Medbay.** for the captain, **Chief Engineer Marrow has orders for you, in Engineering.** for crew, **Dockmaster Jessup has something to ask you, in the Manifest Docks.** for anyone else. An order says so in the quest log too: **Chief Engineer Marrow's orders. By day 2.**
- **The ask:** click them. They ask first, and you answer from the choices. Then the talk goes on as usual. **Not now** and they ask again a day later; **No** and they don't ask again for a week, and they remember you said it.
- **Yes** puts it in your quests, under **Requests**, with what's wanted, how far you've got, who asked and the day it's due, such as **Bring 2 medicine crates aboard (1/2)**, **Dr. Aduro asked. By day 6.** It's in the log (J); **Follow this one** puts it in the tracker. A day before it's due, the story reminds you once.
- **What can be wanted:** things bought for the ship from the Ship menu at a port that sells them; crates you carry into a store yourself (open the shuttle stores, take the crates, open the pantry, put them in; carrying one back out takes it off again); a part fixed with your own hands (its fix, in its window or on its right-click menu); something given to someone, with **Give** on their right-click menu or in their window, or there and then in the talk (**Here, take mine**); a talk with someone; going somewhere; passing a check; carrying someone to a cot; or just your word, where the yes itself does it.
- **Leaving it to someone else:** as captain, some requests can be left to whoever's post it is (**Leave it to your engineers**, **Have the quartermaster see to it**). It goes under Requests as **Chief Marrow is seeing to it**, and if it's put right by the day it's **Done by** them, with five more on their duty bar for that day on the **Command** menu; if not, it's **Failed**, with fifteen off.
- **How it ends,** each time with their words in the story: **Done** when you've done it (with XP); **No longer needed** when it was put right some other way before you'd done any of it (no harm done); **Failed** when the day comes first. If whoever asked dies before it's settled, it passes to whoever takes their post; if nobody does, it's **Over**, with no words from the dead, and nobody remembers it for or against you.
- **They remember:** whoever asked remembers what came of it: that you helped, that you let them down, that you told them no, or that whoever you left it to saw to it. For three days after, it's the first thing they say when you talk to them, a thank-you or a reproach. It counts for or against you the way any memory does, so an officer's loyalty to the captain goes up or down with it, and it's on their card in the **Command** menu ("asked you for help (medicine for the medbay), and got it"). And it's on the record, under **Facts on record** in the quest log, where a written scene can ask about it.
- **How often:** there's a quiet day between one new ask and the next, and nobody brings you something new at night (between nine and seven), except when it can't wait (someone lying hurt with nobody coming), three can be waiting at once, each person asks one thing at a time, the same kind isn't asked twice running, and a request that's over rests for a few days before it can come up again. Nothing new is asked while a written scene is being played, or in a fight, a check or at a computer, and someone a written scene is waiting on plays that scene when you talk to them, not a request.
- **Writing them:** each kind is a plain text file, `world/scenes/request_<kind>.txt`, written like a conversation, with a few lines on top that say which lives it's for (`for: the captain`, `for: crew`, `for: outsiders`, or lives by name), whether it's an order (`as: an order`), when it comes up (`when: stock medicine_crate < 2`), who asks (a post, a job, a name, `your officer`, `your boss` or `someone here`), what it's about when it's someone's trouble (`about: a rival`, or `about: a keepsake you took`, with `{thing}` for what it is), what's needed (`fix a part waiting for repairs`, `give 1 medkit to {asker}`, `give 1 {thing} to {asker}`, `sell 3 ration packs to {asker}, at half again the market price`, `talk to the quartermaster`, `go to haven`, `pass engineering`, `your word`, `carry {about} to the medbay`, `work two shifts`, or something to bring), how long they give, when it's no longer needed, and who it can be left to. Your own scenes can work with them too: `? request medicine done` asks how one stands (asked, taken, done, failed, refused, gone, off or none), `* request medicine: The reason` has someone ask it now, and `* request medicine off: The reason` stops it coming up, so a scene you've written can take that trouble over (`on` brings it back). A request whose answer decides what's done there and then can have a yes of its own for each answer (`== yes_locked`, `== yes_work`), each with its own lines. `world/scenes/_how-to-write-a-scene.txt` explains it under **REQUESTS**, and `npm run check-story` checks them, including a scene naming a request that has no file. A request can also be about a job the ship's work board has left waiting: `about: a job nobody can do` with `needs: see to it yourself`, or `about: a job with nothing to do it with` with `needs: bring what's wanted`, where `{trouble}` is what's wrong, `{task}` what there is to do about it and `{wanted}` how many of what. The twenty-seven there now are drafts, not yet edited.

## Fights

In the isometric view, a fight happens in the room you're in, in real time, and it stops whenever you like. A simulated fight, and a fight in any other view, opens in its own window instead, played in rounds.

### Hits land on the body

In both kinds of fight, every hit lands on a part of whoever's hit: the head, the chest, an arm or a leg, at random, more often the chest and less often the head. Nobody aims. The fight says where: in the room after each line, as "(left arm)", and in the window in the numbers after it, as "6 damage to the left arm". Health, the chances to hit and the damage are as they always were.

- **A hit that takes a fifth of someone's health leaves a wound,** and one that takes two fifths, or a hard hit, leaves a hard one. What it leaves depends on what it was done with: a club or fists leave a bruise, and hard, a broken bone in an arm or a leg, cracked ribs or a concussion; a knife, a claw or a gun leaves a cut, and hard, a deep cut; a laser or a torch leaves a burn, and hard, a deep burn. Synthetics take dents, cracked casing and ruined joints. A line says it: "That leaves the dock thug with a deep cut in the left arm."
- **Wounds count in the fight.** A broken arm takes 2 off attacking, and a concussion 1, for you and for anyone. With a broken leg you can't **Run for it**, and in the room someone with one can't walk: they fight from where they stand, and you can only hit what you can reach from there. The fighters' cards in the window and their lines in the room's panel list the wounds that do something.
- **Nobody dies in a fight.** Someone at no health goes down, and the fight says how: out cold if the hit that did it knocked them on the head, unable to stand with a broken leg, or just unable to get up. The hit that puts someone down always leaves a wound.
- **The wounds stay** on you, on everyone with a file, and on anyone with only a name you went for from the right-click menu, and show on Look, the printouts and your inventory's figure (see [Bodies](#bodies)). Someone who's nobody in particular, like a raider in a story fight, takes theirs away with them, and machines break as they always have.
- **Losing a fight** leaves you with a quarter of your health and the wounds the hits left, instead of a wound picked at random. Aboard the Meridian someone carries you to the medbay and you come to on a cot hours later, with all your credits; anywhere else you come round where you fell, a quarter of your credits gone. See [Bodies](#bodies).
- **The simulator changes nothing:** a simulated fight's wounds go when it ends.
- **What leaves what** is in `world/health/wounds.txt`: how much of someone's health a hit takes to wound, which words in a weapon's name make which kind of hit, and which wound each kind leaves. See [Bodies](#bodies).

### A fight in the room

Everyone in the fight stands somewhere in the room: you where you were, someone you went for where they stood, and anyone else coming in by the way into the room furthest from you. Your followers stand beside you. Each fighter has a health bar and, under it, a bar that fills as they get ready to act again. Nobody can act again until that bar is full.

- **It starts stopped.** Nothing moves until you press **Space** or click **Go on**. Press Space again, or click **Stop**, to stop it whenever you like, as often as you like. You can give orders while it's stopped, and they're carried out once it goes on.
- **To shoot or hit someone,** click them in the room, or on their line in the room's panel on the right. You keep going for them whenever you're ready, until they're down or you choose someone else. If you can't reach them from where you are, you walk up to them.
- **To move,** click the floor. You walk there, and you can't act while you're walking.
- **Your orders** are along the bottom, numbered as in the fight window: talking, the belt, your abilities, your sidearm, giving up, **Run for it**. Press a number or click one. Anything that's aimed at someone asks you to click who. What it does and your chances are in its tooltip. It's carried out the moment you're ready.
- **Stop when I'm ready to act**, a box beside the button, stops the fight by itself each time your bar fills, so you can choose your next order calmly. It stays ticked or not for the next fight.
- **Cover** is standing behind something. There's no Take cover order in the room. Someone with a console, a crate or anything else low between them and the shooter is harder to hit, by 3. When you hover over an enemy, the room shows whether you can shoot them from where you stand, and whether they're behind cover. Walls stop shots altogether.
- **Up close or from afar.** Someone with a pistol or a rifle shoots across the room. Someone with a blade, a club or claws has to walk up to you first. Enemies who shoot look for a spot where they can hit you and you can't easily hit them, so moving matters.
- **People who were due to come running partway through** come in by the door when their time comes, about six seconds for each round they'd have waited.
- When it's over, a line says how it went, with **Continue the story** and **Look around first**. You stay where you were standing at the end.

If you open a fight in the room from another view, the fight window says so and has a **Show the room** button.

### Battles

A battle is a fight in the room that's fought by everyone on a deck at once: the same rules, the same orders, the same dice and numbers as any fight in the room, with as many on each side as the battle brings. The mutiny that opens the game is one (see [The opening battle](#the-opening-battle)).

- **The whole deck.** It isn't held to one room: people fight up the corridors and through the doors, wherever they meet. Those against you make for one room, and whoever's with you and already in it holds it. Anyone with nobody near goes looking: the other side for that room, your side for the nearest of them.
- **No limit of four.** Any other fight has at most four on a side, counting you; a battle has as many as it brings, at the owner's word of 12 November 2026. Every other fight keeps the limit.
- **People come up the stairs as it goes.** A battle says who comes later, and when: more of them, or help from below. A line says so when they come.
- **Nobody shoots across the whole deck.** In a battle nobody can shoot or be shot from further than 16 tiles. If you go for someone out of reach, you walk to where you can reach them.
- **There's no running and no giving up.** **Run for it** and **Give up** aren't among the orders.
- **When two in three of them are out of it,** down or talked out of it, the rest give up.
- **If you go down,** it's fought out without you, there and then, and you come to in the medbay: whoever's still against you when it's done gives up to the rest of the crew.
- **What you see:** the battle's name on the banner over the notes, and in the notes only what concerns you: what you do and what's done to you, who goes down, and the battle's own lines. Everything else can still be seen happening across the deck. The room's panel lists everyone with you and the six nearest against you, and how many more there are, down or out of it.
- **When it's over,** the line says how many of them went down or gave up, and who on your side went down. Whoever's beaten on your side lies where they fell, and the medics come for them as after any fight; whoever's still standing goes back to their day from where the battle left them. The beaten of the other side lie there too, and those who gave up are taken to the brig (see [After the battle](#after-the-battle)).
- **What it costs aboard** is in the battle's file: so much off morale, more for each of yours who went down and more again if you did, never more than 25 at once, and so much off the council's confidence in you. A battle whose file says nothing about it costs nothing.
- **What it leaves behind** is in the battle's file too, one thing a line: a fire where the fighting was thickest, a breach, a part broken in a room, or a part of some kinds broken in every room where anyone went down, each only if you went down or only if anyone went down in that room, if the line says so. The engineers mend what a battle broke one thing at a time, in the order the lines are written.
- **The notes at the end** show everything the battle's end has to say, not just the last few lines.
- **How fast it works:** a whole battle takes the computer about a fifth of a second to work out.

Battles are plain text files in `world/battles/`, one for each. `_how-to-write-a-battle.txt` in that folder explains every line: the place and deck, the room they make for, when they give up, who the crew fight as, where the rest take cover, what it costs aboard, who's against you and where they come in, who comes later, and what it leaves behind. A written scene starts one with a `BATTLE` line (see `world/scenes/_how-to-write-a-scene.txt`). `npm run check-story` checks them.

### The fight window

A fight in the window has your side facing the enemy's, with each fighter drawn in console art beside their health and numbers, and your actions run down the sides of the window with your odds next to them.

The window is only as big as the fight needs: small for a duel, and bigger as followers join and the log fills. If your screen runs out of room, the actions move underneath the fighters and the log shrinks, so the buttons stay in view.

- **To act,** press a number or click an action. Each round then plays out one blow at a time, and you can click to skip ahead. If there are ten things to do, 0 is the tenth.
- **To aim,** click an enemy or use the left and right arrow keys. Your odds change to match that enemy's defenses.
- **Menus** still open during a fight, and slide over its window.
- **Supplies come off your belt.** Only what's on your belt shows as an action in a fight, and each use takes one off it, so put what you want to use there beforehand. Nothing else in the inventory can be changed in the middle of a fight.
- **Your sidearm:** with a weapon on your hip, **Draw your ...** switches to it for the rest of the fight, and **Back to your ...** switches back. Either takes your turn.
- **Difficulty:** **Fights** in the Settings menu goes from Easy to Hard, and applies from your next fight. Your followers stay the same, but enemies change. On Hard, someone who can't fight will usually lose to anything above a minor threat, unless they bring help or run.

**Talking instead of fighting.** Under **Another way** in the fight window, and among the orders along the bottom in a fight in the room, are other things you can do with your turn. In the room, "your turn" is the moment your bar is full, and talking only reaches people within about twelve tiles. Each shows your chance of it working against whoever you're aiming at, and the chance changes as the fight goes on. Hover over one to see the sum.

| Option | What it uses | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Talk them down** | Persuasion, and Presence or whatever suits who you're talking to | They stop fighting and walk away. Their faction likes you a little more |
| **Threaten them**, or **Scare it off** for an animal | Intimidation, and Presence or Might | They back off, and their faction likes you less. How strong you are counts for more here |
| **Pay them off** | Commerce and Presence | They take the money and go, and it's worth half the XP. You only pay if they accept. The price is on the button |
| **Lie to them** | Deception, and Presence or Intellect | They believe you and go. If it fails, they won't believe anything else you say |
| **Shut it down** | Hacking and Intellect | For machines. It stops, but it isn't destroyed |
| **Back away slowly** | Survival, and Endurance or Perception | For animals. It loses interest |

Four things decide how hard it is, and a line under the fighters tells you how each stands for whoever you're aiming at:

- **How much they hate you.** It comes from how their faction feels about you and what you both believe, or from how they feel about you if they're someone you know. It can run from "They like you" to "They want you dead".
- **Why they're fighting.** Someone who's hungry is far easier to talk down than a true believer. Money works on a hired hand and not on someone who's sure they're right, and a forged order is the way past a soldier. Every kind of enemy has its usual reason, and a written scene can give them a different one, in which case you'll read it in that line.
- **How strong you are.** The game works out how long each side would take to beat the other, from everyone's real numbers, so it changes with every hit. Nobody backs down when they're winning.
- **The stat that helps.** Your skill and your best stat for it are added to the roll. What helps with talking depends on who it is: you can show a hired hand the sums with Intellect, a Korrun respects Might, and someone desperate needs reading with Perception.

It takes your whole turn, and the enemies still get theirs. Your followers hold their fire while you do it. If it fails, they get angrier, so it's harder the next time. Once one of them has gone, the others are a little less sure of themselves, and more so if that was the one in charge. Anyone who walks away isn't beaten: you get the XP, but none of their money, and they're still out there, so the story decides what they do next. Machines can't be talked to or frightened, though a free synthetic can be reasoned with. The Choir isn't listening, and it's very hard to hack. The very worst enemies, the same ones you can't run from, can only be stopped by a fight.

**Give up** ends the fight on the spot, as long as there's someone there to give up to. You're not hurt any more than you already are, and they leave it there, unless a written scene started the fight and says what happens next. It costs you some stress, and it's never the end of the game.

**Followers:** either side can have followers if the story allows it, up to four fighters on a side, counting you. Some may come running partway through. Your followers fight by themselves, going after whatever you're aiming at, and they take a share of the XP. A follower who's knocked out stops fighting, and the story decides what became of them. If you fall, the fight is lost, even if your followers are still standing.

Fights run entirely on this computer, from the stats in the game's files. A fight starts when a written scene says so, or when you attack someone from the right-click menu. When it ends, the story gets one line saying how it went. If a written scene was waiting on the fight, **Continue the story** goes on from how it ended. If nothing was, the fight is simply over, and the window takes you back to the room.

To try a fight without it counting, use **Run in simulator** in **Enemies met**, aboard the Meridian, or **With followers** to have your crew and more enemies join in. Nothing that happens in the simulator carries over.

## Skill checks

When a choice has a check on it, or a written scene calls for one, a window opens over the story, and each skill has its own. It floats over the room like the game's other windows, with nothing behind it dimmed, so you can see the room go on around you. Pull it aside with the mouse to see what's under it, and it slides back when you let go. A thin line runs from it to what the check is about, in the window's colour: the part you're working, the door you're forcing or hacking, the console you're getting into, the person whose pocket you're picking, or whoever or whatever in the room the check names, such as the person you're treating. If the window would hide that thing, the room slides aside until it shows beside the window, and slides back when the window closes. Practice is about nothing in the room, so it has no line. Hacking has no window: a hack opens the computer itself, at its logon (see [Computers](#computers)). Medicine is a patient monitor. Piloting is a run through debris on a proximity radar. Persuasion is a conversation where you read the other person and pick your approach.

| Skills | What the window is |
|---|---|
| Science, Lore | Work out a hidden sequence, from how close each attempt gets |
| Engineering | Flip relays to get a whole panel live, when each one also flips its neighbors |
| Medicine, Survival, Composure | Keep three readings in the safe zone while events push them around |
| Piloting, Athletics | Pick your way down a corridor of obstacles, one move at a time |
| Stealth, Void Ops | Cross a map without stepping into anything's line of sight |
| Gunnery, Firearms | Aim where the target is going to be |
| Navigation | Plot the course with the least drift across a star chart |
| Tactics, Melee | Read what the other side is about to do, and answer it |
| Persuasion, Leadership, Streetwise | Bring someone around before they lose patience. With someone who has a file, what lands depends on what they really believe |
| Deception | Keep your cover story alive under questioning, by watching for tells |
| Intimidation, Manifestation | Push harder for a stronger result, and stop before it breaks |
| Commerce | Find their real price without insulting them |
| Awareness | Spot what's out of place in the picture |
| Attunement | Find where on the dial something comes through |

- **Your odds:** the bar at the bottom shows your chance of success, and it moves as you play. Your character's skill sets where it starts, and playing well can add up to 30%. When you're ready, the button beside the command line makes the final roll, at the odds showing. You can press it at any time.
- **Your training matters:** a better-trained character gets better tools in the window. A good pilot can see further ahead, and a seasoned talker can tell what will land. Some perks and gear make the tools better still.
- **Hacking isn't one of them.** A scene's hacking check, a door's panel and a console you **Use** all open the computer at its logon, where your rig's programs get you in or don't: see [Computers](#computers).
- **It's never a race:** nothing in the window moves until you do, and everything can be clicked as well as typed. The room's clock runs on behind every check, the story's own as well as those on something in the room, such as a junction box or a locked door, so the world doesn't wait for you. If you do type, part of a word is enough, and a typo won't cost you anything. Number keys pick commands, and the arrow keys work wherever there's a direction to choose.
- **When it's over:** if a written scene is waiting on the result, **Continue the story** goes on from it. Otherwise **Close** takes you back to the room, and the result is in the story.
- **To try them:** every skill on the SKILLS page of your registry printout (click your name, or press C) has a **PRACTICE** button, and nothing in practice counts. For Hacking it's a row of buttons, one for each class of computer, and each opens a made-up computer of that class at its logon, to practise on: nothing there runs anything, nothing's left behind, the clock doesn't move for it, and being cut off costs nothing.
- **To skip them:** in **Settings**, set **Skill checks** to **Just a roll**, and checks go straight to the roll at your character's own odds. Hacking isn't a check, so it's always the computer's logon.

## Computers

Getting into a computer is the start, not the end. When you **Use** a console that's yours, when you've learned a computer's password, or once you're past a computer's logon, the screen becomes the computer's own system: **Keelworks 4.1**, a desktop of about 1999 made up for the ship's shipyard, on an old monitor floating over the room the same way as the check window, with nothing dimmed, a line to the console or the door you're at, and the clock running on. Pull the monitor's frame to move it aside, and it slides back when you let go, like the game's other windows; your rig, once you've taken it out (**H**), moves on its own, and stays where you leave it. What's on the computer are folders on the desktop: click one to pick it and again to open it (or double-click), or open it from the **Keel** menu in the corner. A folder opens in a window, and what you click in it opens in a second. The session's log, your programs and the place's network are on your rig beside the monitor (see [Your rig beside a computer or a panel](#your-rig-beside-a-computer-or-a-panel)). Windows are dragged by their title bars and closed with their **x**, and the taskbar has a button for each open one. There are no hints: what each way in costs is on your rig's D: page. Everything is clicked: there's no command line. **Log off** is in the Keel menu, or press Esc.
- **How it looks:** in **Settings**, under **Computers**, **Screen** picks the desktop's skin (**Shipyard grey**, the usual one, **Slate**, darker, or **Phosphor**, a one-colour green monitor) and **Old screen** how much the old monitor shows (**Off**, **Light** with scanlines and a little glow, or **Heavy** with stronger lines, a rolling band and a flicker). **Rig animations**, in the same place, picks how much plays as your rig comes out of your pocket and goes back (**Full**, **Quick** or **Off**; they're described with the rig, further down). They change nothing else.

- **What's on one:** **Files** to read, **Mail** between the people of the place (click a letter to read it), **Logs** the game writes from what it knows (whether the Meridian is at the berths, today's prices, who's here, what's been heard), and **Programs** that do real things. The network it's on is on your rig's NET page.
- **Programs do real things:** a door opens on the map, a debt is cleared, a fact goes on the record for the stories to read, a password to another computer is learned. Each runs once. What you've read goes into **What you know**.
- **The network:** another computer on the list opens at its own logon, unless it's yours or you know its password.
- **When you log off,** one `[COMPUTER]` line in the story says what you read and ran, and what the watcher made of you. The minutes of everything you did have gone by, counting the time the clock ran while you were on it. A computer remembers what you've read and run for next time.
- **Written or made up:** the Meridian's plot table, reactor gauges, records terminal, navigation core and reactor safety controller, the first officer's terminal, the security desk, the quartermaster's ledger and the Armory's panel, Haven's berth board and the harbor master's machine, the Foundry's sublevel door panel and the Ministry's archive on Asterion are written out in `world/computers/`, as drafts for the owner to rewrite. Any other console, terminal, repeater, server, keypad, panel or power board in a room, and any door that asks for a hack, is a computer the game makes up from what it knows of the place, unless the map file already says what using it does. `_how-to-write-a-computer.txt` in that folder explains the files, and the **Story board** shows them under **Computers**. A scene can hand out a password with `* password`, open a door with `* door`, or sit the player down with `* computer`.

### Getting in

There's no free way in. A computer that isn't yours, and whose password you don't know, opens at its **logon**: Keelworks' box at the top of the desktop, with a line saying whether a rig is on its port. **Take out your rig and connect it first**: press **H** (or click the **RIG** tab down the screen's side), then CONNECT on the rig: it takes a minute, and at someone else's machine anyone in the room can see you plug in. Then the ways in you've brought are on the rig's **D:** page, each saying whether it will work here and, if it won't, why. Click one to run it. **Log off** walks away. Nothing on the computer shows until you're past it.

- **The breakers** are the getting-in programs on your rig, and each works on computers up to its strength: **KNOCK** on door panels; **DICTIONARY** on anything up to a crew terminal, slow and loud; **SKELETON** on a ship's main system, once **SCOPE** (built into every rig) has read the logon; **SHADOW**, riding someone's open session, in working hours (07:00 to 21:00), with everything one louder after; **MIMIC**, silent, playing back a keycard you've lifted. Picking a pocket sometimes brings a keycard as well as credits. For a Ministry or Navy server or worse, for now, only a password gets you in.
- **The watcher**, AuditGuard, hears the noise you make, from the first try at the logon to the moment you log off: a breaker's own, a program's, 1 for switching a system, copying a program or running one of the computer's, and 1 for a first read on a Ministry or Navy server or worse (reading is free below that). Noise adds up to three marks. At the first it **notices** you: the eye glows, the light on the monitor's frame goes amber, and everything you do from then on is one louder. At the second it starts a **trace**: the light goes red, the tray counts the minutes, and when they're up it finds you. At the third it **cuts you off** at once. Being found or cut off throws you out, and on a ship's main system or worse puts a fact on the record that the stories can read. Log off before then and you're out clean. The marks are set by the class (a door panel puts up with far more than a ship's main system), and your training raises them; they're in `world/checks.json`.
- **Minutes are the session's own:** each thing you do takes its minutes, and only those count for a trace. Reading, thinking and choosing take none, so there's never a hurry.

### More computers

Not every computer is a desk, and **your rig's link decides which ones it can reach at all**. Each kind takes one kind of link: a crew terminal or a desktop a **cable jack**, a door's panel an **optical reader**, a junction box a **service clip**, a camera a **short radio**, and the ship's main system a **Navy coupler**. The rig has one link socket, so you choose before you go, on the Rig page. Without the right link the rig's breakers, its other programs and copying all say "your rig can't reach it", and with it they wait until you connect the rig. A computer with its own keyboard (a desktop, a door's panel, the bridge) can still be used by right, or with a password, without the rig; a junction box and a camera have neither, so they're reached only through it.

- **A door's panel** (DoorCtl 2.1) is a keypad and a two-line screen with a card slot; the ways in are on your rig beside it (below). Inside, it opens or locks its door, or gives the door **a new code**: the door stays locked to everyone there, guards included, and you have the code, so you get straight back in to open it. Its watcher is the door's own log.
- **A junction box's service menu** opens from the box's window or right-click menu (**Open its service menu**), on your rig's little amber screen: 1 STATUS, 2 BREAKERS, 3 LOG, 4 CLEAR LOG, 0 EXIT. Under BREAKERS each room the box feeds can be switched off on its own: that room goes dark, and its machines stop. Every switch shows on the chief engineer's **fault board**, the watcher here, and at someone else's place their people put the power back after a while. Clearing the box's own log takes **CLEANER.EXE** or the service password.
- **A camera's feed** (**Tap its feed**, or from the room next door with a short radio, **Tap the camera in ... by radio**) shows its picture: the room, the time, and who's in it, you too if you're standing there. **Loop the last minute** and for ten minutes it shows that minute over and over and sees nobody pass, so nothing it would have seen counts (tampering, theft, taking the ship apart); loop it with someone in the picture and **the security desk** notices. **Blind it** and the picture goes to snow, which they see at once; **Back to live** puts it right.
- **NAVCOM 7**, the Navy's own desktop, is the ship's main system: the plot table on the bridge. It's the same desktop in the Navy's colours, with a restricted bar along the top and **SENTRY**, its watcher, in it when it's listening, and two folders of its own: **Ship's log**, where a line can be struck out (on your own system it's done under your name, and the first officer reads the log), and **Crew assessments**, the first officer's view of each officer and their loyalty to the chair. Copying GHOST or WORM off the plot table now takes a Navy coupler in the rig. The crew file is sealed: it opens only when you come to the plot table from the first officer's terminal (below).
- **The files:** a computer's file can say `look: navcom` (or `door`) and `reached by: radio` (or cable, clip, optic, navy); `world/computers/_how-to-write-a-computer.txt` explains both. The numbers are first guesses, to be tuned.

### The ship's network

Every system a place's systems file knows is on its network: the computers, the door panels of the doors they run, the junction boxes, the cameras and the comms relay. Its map, deck by deck, is on your rig's NET page, beside any computer or panel (below). Two networks join them:

- **The data network** runs from each computer to what it runs (its doors, its cameras, the relay, and the junction boxes it runs the power through), and between computers that name each other.
- **The maintenance bus** runs along the junction boxes, and from each box to the door panels and the relay in the rooms it feeds.

On the map, what's linked to the system you're on can be clicked, and you go there: it takes two minutes, and it's heard where you leave and where you arrive. A system that isn't yours opens at its logon, with the breakers you brought; over the network your rig's link doesn't matter, since it's still plugged in where you started. Aboard the Meridian, as her captain, you go through her systems freely. You can join the network from anything your rig can plug into in a room (a terminal by cable, a keypad by its optical reader, a junction box by clip, a camera by radio), once it's connected; plugging into someone else's in front of people is seen, like any tampering.

A systems file's **Network** part adds links the rest of the file doesn't give ("the records terminal to the plot table", with ", bus" at the end for the bus); `world/systems/_how-to-write-systems.txt` explains it.

### Hardened computers

Some computers are **hardened**: no breaker on your rig works on them from the front, however strong. Their way in is written somewhere else at the same place, and you find it by reading:

- **A password** in someone's letter, a file or a log on another computer there. Once you've read it, the hardened one lets you straight in, as any password does.
- **A session it leaves open** at certain hours (through the morning change, say), written in a log. Then **SHADOW** can ride it in those hours, and at no other.
- **A service account the junction boxes still share**, in a junction box's service log (3 LOG on its menu). Then **KNOCK**, a door panel's breaker, gets in through it, however hard the computer is.

Reading one says so on the rig's LOG: "You've found a way into…". On the rig's map a hardened system has a dashed amber box with **HARD** under its kind, and **WAY IN** once you've found one. At its logon a **HARDENED** line says what you know of it, and each breaker on the D: page says why it won't work. **SNIFFER** and **WORM** still bring its password along the network, as they bring any other.

At Haven there are two: the harbor master's machine, whose letter and session log are on the berth board and whose service account is in the junction boxes' log, and the watch console in The Neutral Ground, whose way in the game makes up. Aboard the Meridian the officers' private terminals are hardened too (below). A hack a scene asks for without naming a computer never lands on a hardened one. In a computer's file it's `hardened: yes` and a **Weak points** part, explained in `world/computers/_how-to-write-a-computer.txt`; a console a place's systems file puts in a room says `, hardened` after what it runs.

### Sealed systems and private terminals

Aboard the Meridian you go through her own systems freely, but not all of them.

- **Private terminals** belong to the officers, and aren't yours by right: each has a logon and a watcher, like a computer at a port, and each is hardened, so its way in is written somewhere else aboard. **The first officer's terminal** is in the Chart Room beside the bridge: her session stays signed in through the 06:00 watch change (the plot table's log says so), and she once gave the doctor her word (a letter on the medbay's records terminal). **The security desk** is in the Aft Holds and **the quartermaster's ledger** at the end of the Long Mess's counter; their words are in letters on the first officer's terminal, and the junction boxes' log still shares an account with each. The game stands these terminals in their rooms itself, since the map doesn't draw them.
- **Sealed systems** take nothing at their own keyboard: no logon, no breaker, no password, not even yours. They let in only the machines they trust, over the network, and what you can do inside is set by where you came from. On the rig's map a sealed one has a double red box with **SEALED** under its kind, and **TRUSTED** in green when the machine you're on is one it trusts. Clicking it from anywhere else says what it answers to, and no time goes by. Using one in its room says the same.
  - **The Armory's panel** answers only to the security desk. From there you can open the Armory, lock it or give it a new code. At the door itself there's nothing to hack, and the plot table no longer runs that door.
  - **The reactor safety controller**, the cabinet beside the reactor, answers only to the reactor gauges. From there it can **scram the reactor**, so the drive is down and the ship goes nowhere, and bring it back up.
  - **The navigation core** under the plot table answers to the comms relay and to the plot table. From the relay (reached from the plot table, or from the bridge's junction box with a service clip) you can lock and unlock the navigation, so that every jump is plotted by hand. From the plot table you can only read its jump log and charts. The plot table itself no longer switches the navigation.
  - **NAVCOM 7's crew file** is sealed even though the plot table isn't: it opens only when you come to the plot table from the first officer's terminal.
- A session let in by trust says so on its first screen, and in its log: "Reached through the security desk, which it trusts: from there it lets you work the door." In a computer's file it's `sealed: yes` and a **Trusts** part (`world/computers/_how-to-write-a-computer.txt`).

### The story knows your rig

The written story, people's talks and their asks can see what's on your rig and what you've done on computers: whether a program or a part is on it, which link it reaches by, whether you've broken into a computer, reached it over the network or been let in by trust, and whether you've given a door a code only you know. They can give you programs, parts and tools for the bench, tell you a computer's password or when its session is left open, and seal a computer or open it again. What you do also goes on the record, for the stories to react to: getting into someone else's computer, recoding a door, looping or blinding a camera, striking a line from the ship's log. For now, two of the owner's drafts use it: **Lieutenant Sorel** on the bridge will look at your rig if you ask (and copy you MAPPER if it isn't on it, or tell you the commander noticed a struck line), and if the navigation's been locked from its core, **the helm officer** asks you to have it unlocked. `world/scenes/_how-to-write-a-scene.txt` lists the words. The **Story board** has pages for **Programs** and **Rig parts** too, and a computer's page shows its weak points and what it trusts.

### Your rig beside a computer or a panel

At a computer or a panel your rig stays in your pocket, a small **RIG** tab down the side of the screen, until you take it out: press **H**, or click the tab. **H** again, or **AWAY** at the rig's top, puts it back. It slides out from behind the computer's edge, where the tab was, and the computer moves over to make room for it; put away, its screen goes off first (the picture squeezes to a bright line, the line shrinks to a dot, and the dot fades, a little over half a second in all), then it slides back in with its screen dark, and the tab comes out again. Pressing **H** in the middle turns it round at once; while the screen is still going off the rig hasn't moved, so it just stays and starts up again. The cable is only drawn once the rig has arrived; it stays while the screen goes off and goes as the rig leaves. A rig you've dragged somewhere else grows and shrinks where you left it instead, and if Windows is set to show no animations, the rig simply appears and disappears. Each time it comes out it starts up: its screen is dark while it's on its way, and once it has arrived it checks the parts you built it from, a line to each kind (core, memory, cell, heat sink, link, and an extra if you've fitted one), each ending in **OK**, **WORN** in amber or **BROKEN** in red, while its two lights blink amber; then **READY**, and its own page. That takes under two seconds, and a click on its screen skips it. A link in order says **FITTED**, or **PLUGGED** once the rig's connected. A part it can't start without that isn't in it is **MISSING**, and then its last line says why it won't start in place of READY. In a narrow window, where the rig is a strip down the computer's side with no screen of its own, its page slides out of the strip to show the check and stays out afterwards; the × at its corner folds it away. When the rig goes back, that page's screen goes off and the page slides back into the strip as the strip leaves; with the page folded away there's no screen to switch off, and the strip simply slides back. Connecting is as it was, three seconds of the rig waking up when you press **CONNECT**; at a junction box or a camera, which your rig is on from the moment you open them, the check is all that plays. With Windows set to show no animations there's no check and no power-off either: the rig is there, or it's gone. **How much of this plays is yours to pick:** in **Settings**, under **Computers**, **Rig animations** is **Full** (all of it, as described here, about two seconds out and one second back), **Quick** (the same slide, but the picture drops in with one flicker where the parts would be checked, and the screen goes off in half the time: about three quarters of a second each way) or **Off** (none of it, exactly as with Windows set to show no animations). Whichever you pick, connecting still takes its three seconds, and a rig that's back in your pocket is unplugged. **Putting it away unplugs it.** Once the rig is back in your pocket it's off the computer, and the session's log says so ("You unplug your rig."); the next time you take it out you connect again, which takes a minute and can be seen at someone else's machine. If you change your mind before it's all the way in, it never came off. There are two places it can't come off: a junction box or a camera, which are reached only through the rig, and a computer you reached over the network, where there's nothing in front of you to unplug. There the rig only goes dark and stays connected in your pocket, and its tab shows its LINK and HEAT lights. **UNPLUG** at the rig's foot still takes it off without putting it away. Going along the network keeps it out, and a practice session from the Skills page starts with it out, since that's all practice is for. Out, it sits beside the computer: a little handheld with your board and casing on it (a yard board in a plain case to start with) and two lights, **LINK** (green when it's plugged in, blue over the network, dark when it isn't) and **HEAT** (dark when it isn't working someone else's computer, then green, amber and a blinking red as it warms). Between the two is how it's plugged in: a grey cable to a desk, the optical reader's red beam to a keypad, a clip lead to a junction box, radio waves to a camera, and a dashed line when you came over the network.

**Nothing gets hacked until you connect it.** Until then its screen says NOT CONNECTED and offers **CONNECT BY** the link this one takes. It takes a minute, and at someone else's machine anyone in the room can see you plug in, like any tampering. As it connects, the rig wakes up for about three seconds on its screen (its board, a memory count, the link, then D:) with LINK blinking amber, and a click on its screen skips it; at a junction box or a camera, which it connects to as they open, there's nothing to press, and the rig starting up as you take it out is all that plays (only with Windows set to show no animations, or with Rig animations set to Off, does it still wake this way there, the first time you take it out). With the wrong link fitted, the button is greyed and says why. **UNPLUG** at its foot takes it off again, and so does putting the rig back in your pocket. A junction box or a camera has no keyboard, so it's reached only through the rig: opening one connects it. A computer or a keypad you're allowed on (yours, or with a password) works by hand without the rig: read its files, open its door, run its systems. The map and your programs need the rig plugged in.

Its screen has three pages, under **NET**, **D:** and **LOG**:

- **NET** is the place's network in the rig's own green. Click a system linked to here to go there.
- **D:** is what's loaded on the rig, each with why it won't run here if it won't. At a logon it's what gets you past it, and the rig opens on this page; once you're in it turns to the map. A list too long for the rig's screen scrolls inside it with the mouse wheel, and stays where you left it when you run something.
- **LOG** is everything that's happened at this one, with a dot on its button when there's something new. A panel's window keeps its last few lines too.

So a computer's desktop keeps only the computer's own things: its folders (Systems, Files, Mail, Logs, Programs, and on NAVCOM 7 the ship's log and the crew assessments) and the window you read them in. A panel's window keeps its face, its own buttons and its log. Where the screen is too narrow for the monitor and the rig side by side, the rig folds into a strip down the monitor's side, with its lights, its pages and PLUG IN, and a page you pick slides out over the monitor; its round button folds it away again.

**The computer and the rig move on their own.** Each is a window of its own. Pull the monitor (or a panel's window) aside by its frame and it slides back when you let go, like the game's other windows, while the rig stays where it is. Drag the rig by its case and it stays where you leave it, never quite off the screen. The cable, beam or radio between them stretches to follow, and goes when they touch. Folded into its strip, the rig moves the same way, with the page that slides out of it. It stays where you put it while you go along the network, and starts beside the computer again at the next computer you come to, after you log off, or when the window changes size.

### Your rig

Your **rig** is a pocket computer in your pack, two squares by two, and it carries programs of your own onto any computer you get into. In the inventory, resting the mouse on it says what's loaded and how much memory is free, and double-clicking it (or **Open the rig** on its right-click menu) opens its page, which is also **Rig** under **Gear** (or `/rig`): its memory, four blocks, with what's loaded in it; the programs it has that aren't loaded; and every program there is, by kind (getting in, doors and systems, staying quiet, the watcher, finding things, leaving things behind), with what each does, the blocks it takes, how strong a computer it works on, and whether the watcher hears it.

- **You start with** the plain yard build (see [The rig built from parts](#the-rig-built-from-parts)), its 4 blocks holding DICTIONARY, DOORKEY and MASK, KNOCK on the rig but not loaded (every rig has it), and SCOPE built in, which takes no memory.
- **Loading** is done before you sit down: at a computer the rig runs what it brought, and **Load** and **Unload** wait until you log off. A program the rig has runs whatever your Hacking.
- **Writing a program** is done at the **Repair Bay's bench**, on deck 3. Standing in the Repair Bay, the Rig page has **Write it** under each program your Hacking is high enough for, with the hours it takes (2 to 4); the clock runs those hours, and the program goes on the rig, loaded if there's room. Some can't be written: they're copied off a computer, or bought from Kessara's fence (see [Getting parts](#getting-parts)).
- **Copying one:** a computer that holds a program has **Copy ... to your rig** at the bottom of its **Programs** folder. It's an action like any other, and the watcher hears it. The Meridian's plot table holds GHOST and WORM; the Ministry's archive on Asterion holds BLINDFOLD and BACKDOOR.
- **On the computer,** the breakers work at the logon (see [Getting in](#getting-in)), and once you're past it the rig's **D:** page has what's loaded: a click runs one. One that won't run there is greyed, and resting the mouse on it says why: you're already in, it's too weak for that class of computer, the computer runs no doors or cameras, or nobody watches your own computers.
- **What they do:** DOORKEY and MASTERKEY unlock the doors the computer runs and RELAY puts its cameras off for ten minutes, all without a sound to the watcher; MASK and GHOST make the next three or six actions silent; CLEANER, DECOY and FORGER take three, two and one noise off, and a trace loses you once you're back under its mark; LULLABY gives a running trace four more minutes to find you; BLINDFOLD blinds the watcher for five minutes, after which it notices the gap; CUTOUT keeps being found or cut off off the record; DIGGER reads every file, letter and log at once; SCOPE and MAPPER say what the computer runs and what it reaches; and some stay behind when you log off: SLEEPER unlocks the doors at the next 02:00, SABOTEUR breaks something at 03:00, SNIFFER gives you the passwords of the computers it reaches a day later, WORM gives the password of the next computer on the network, and BACKDOOR this one's. What's waiting to happen is under **Left behind** on the Rig page, and its line comes into the story when it does.
- **The programs are files,** one for each in `world/programs/`, with `_how-to-write-a-program.txt` there explaining every line. The numbers are first guesses, to be tuned.

### The rig built from parts

The rig is a casing round a board, and the board's sockets each hold a part of their kind: a core, memory, a link, a cell, a heat sink, and on some boards an extra. **The build** on the Rig page draws the board's sockets on their grid, each a tile with its part, the part's heat, and how much the sinks beside it cool it off, with the casing and the board above and your spare parts below. Click a part to see what it does, its grade, weight and condition, and where it came from, with what can be done to it. Pick a spare part and the sockets it fits light up: click one to fit it, and whatever was there goes to the spare parts. Fitting and swapping can be done anywhere but at a computer or in a fight.

- **What a build adds up to:** its **memory** (the blocks for programs), its **power** (added to a program's own against a computer's class, so a Navy board lets DICTIONARY at a ship's main system), its **speed** (the minutes everything takes are divided by it), its **quiet** (noise taken off what the rig does; below 0 it adds some), and its **heat**. It won't start without a core, a cell and a link that work, and then nothing on it runs.
- **Heat:** each part makes some; a heat sink takes heat off the sockets beside it (left, right, above and below), so where a part goes matters, and the casing takes some off the whole. At a computer that isn't yours the rig warms a point every ten minutes of the session. At 3 its fan is heard, and anything noisy is one louder. At 5 it's overheating: everything takes twice as long, and when you log off the hottest part wears a step. The desktop's tray says how the rig is (**Rig cool**, **warm**, **hot**, or **overheating**), and the Rig page says how many minutes of a session it takes to get there.
- **Worn and broken:** a worn part gives half and runs a point warmer; a broken one gives nothing until it's mended or rebuilt.
- **At the Repair Bay's bench,** with the clock running: **mend** a worn or broken part with one of the ship's parts crates (the engineers' repairs use the same crates), taking as many hours and as much Engineering as the part's grade; **overclock** the core (quicker, two points hotter; Engineering 1, 2 hours) or **undervolt** it (slower, a point cooler; Engineering 1, 1 hour), or put it back as it was; **rebuild** a broken part as a worn one from 2 scrap (2 hours), once the bench has a micro lathe; **take apart** a spare part for scrap (1, or 2 for a board or casing); and **make** an open frame casing or a scrap memory from scrap, and with a tool for the bench a fin sink, an optical reader or a hot cell (see [Getting parts](#getting-parts)).
- **What you have:** you start with the plain yard build (a yard board in a plain case: a stock core, two 2-block memories, a cable jack, a standard cell and a fin sink), 4 blocks, cool. The other boards, casings and parts are in the files, and **Every part there is** at the bottom of the build says where each comes from (see [Getting parts](#getting-parts)). A link says what it reaches a computer by: a cable, a service port, an optical port, radio or a Navy coupling, and that decides which computers the rig can reach at all (see [More computers](#more-computers)). The rig weighs what its parts weigh, in the pack's load too.
- **The parts are files,** one for each in `world/rig-parts/`, with `_how-to-build-a-rig.txt` there explaining every line. The bench's rules are first guesses, to be tuned.

### Getting parts

Parts come from the Graveyard's wrecks, the ports' dealers and the bench, and now and then from the ship herself.

- **The wrecks.** At the Graveyard, **You can** has a shuttle out to three wrecks besides the Harrow: **the Merrow**, an ore freighter (cells, memory and scrap, easy work), **the Pale Wren**, a Navy picket (boards, cores and couplers, some behind a hatch, hard work), and **the Saint Ilse**, a passenger liner (quiet parts, good casings, radios). A run takes an **air bottle** from your pack, which is 60 minutes of air out there; you start with two, and Tessen Colony's workshop sells more. Each job says its minutes, the Engineering it needs and what comes out. Parts come out **worn**, or **broken** if your Engineering is below the job's; scrap comes out as it is. A job done stays done and what's taken stays taken, so a wreck is stripped bare in a run or two. **Go back to the shuttle** brings it home: the parts go with your rig's spare parts, the scrap into your pack. If the air runs out first, the shuttle's crew haul you out, and what you carried is left by the airlock, to pick up on your next run there.
- **The dealers,** in the **Market** menu at three ports, beside the market's own goods:
  - **Haven's market** sells ordinary parts (memory, cells, a shielded case, a short radio, an optical reader) at their worth, and buys only what still works.
  - **Kessara's fence** sells the rare things (a racing core, a whisper core, a slim board, an echo buffer, a cold plate, an overclock board) and four programs (SHADOW, MASTERKEY, CUTOUT and SNIFFER) at half again, and buys anything, a broken part for 5 credits. A friend of the Drift, who hold Kessara, pays less and gets more, and each day you sell there counts a little with them.
  - **Tessen Colony's workshop** sells air bottles, scrap by the sack of three and the bench's tools, and buys scrap at 4 credits each.
  - Each one bought makes the next a fifth dearer until the port restocks, one of everything a day. Each dealer wants a kind of part, a different one on alternate days, and pays half again for one. A spare part fetches half its worth where anything is bought and two fifths at Haven, half that if it's worn. Your way with a price (Commerce and Presence) counts, as at the market. A market no longer takes the rig whole: its parts are sold one at a time.
- **Tools for the bench** are bought at Tessen out of the ship's purse, since the bench is the ship's, and they stay on it: the **soldering kit** (a fin sink from 3 scrap, Engineering 2; an optical reader from a camera's lens unit and a scrap, Engineering 3), the **cell press** (a hot cell from a fuel cell and one of the ship's parts crates, Engineering 4), and the **micro lathe**, without which the lathe can't rebuild a broken part. The build on the Rig page says which tools the bench has.
- **The ship's own things.** As captain, a junction box, the comms relay, a camera or the nav core has **Take it apart for your rig** in its window and its right-click menu: out come a fin sink and 2 scrap, a short radio, a camera's lens unit, or an overclock board, worn, for 15 to 30 minutes and Engineering 1 to 3. What it ran stops, by the systems' own rule (the rooms it feeds go dark, the comms go down, the camera sees nothing, the jumps are plotted by hand), until the engineers mend it with a parts crate. If someone sees you do it (someone awake in the room, or a camera that works), whoever holds its post hears of it and remembers.
- **Finds.** After the mutiny, the Brig's **You can** has **Go through the mutineers' belongings** (a short radio, a scrap memory and a scrap). In the Repair Bay, Tech Oona lets you have her old service clip for two of the ship's parts crates.
- **The files:** `world/wrecks/` (a file for each wreck) and `world/dealers/` (a file for each dealer), each with a how-to, and in `world/rig-parts/`, `_tools.txt` (the bench's tools), `_from-the-ship.txt` (what can be taken apart) and `_finds.txt`, each explained in its own first lines. The numbers are first guesses, to be tuned.

## Systems

Every place has its own systems: power, air, lights, doors, cameras, an alarm and comms, and on a ship engines and navigation, on a station docking clamps. None of them reaches another place, and every part is a real thing in a room: a junction box on the wall, a vent in every room, a camera with a red light, a speaker, a relay. They're drawn where they are, each its own small drawing, and listed under **Systems** in **What's here**, the pane over the room. Click one and you walk over to it.

- **Working them by hand:** a part's window, beside it in the room, says what it is and its state, and the **Actions** window glued to its side says what you can do to it. Some things are free, like a light switch or a junction box's lever. Others open the check window: cutting cables takes Engineering, covering a camera's lens takes Stealth, and forcing a locked door takes Athletics.
- **Power:** a junction box powers its section. A room without power goes dark, and its cameras, vents, speakers, screens and door panels stop. A computer in a room without power won't come on, and one you're using goes dark and logs you off when its room's power is cut, from it or from anywhere else.
- **The dark:** a dark room is drawn dark, with only the red emergency lamps at its doors. Sneaking there is easier, by 2, and keeping watch is harder, by 2.
- **Air:** when the air plant stops, or a room's vent is closed or sealed, or the room loses power, its air goes bad over about twenty minutes. A haze hangs in the room, everyone in it heads for the nearest room with good air, and you feel it: stress while it's thin, then harm once it's bad, two health every ten minutes. You can't rest in it, and after two to three hours of it, or as soon as it has taken your health down to 1, you pass out (see **Your own death** under [Bodies](#bodies)). Air comes back in about ten minutes once it's moving again. Aboard the Meridian the air plant needs the generator: with no fuel cell to burn she has no power, the air plant stops, and she breathes her reserve air, five days of it. It needs water too, since it makes the air out of it: with her water tank empty it stops the same way (see **Water and reserve air** under [The ship](#the-ship)). Whoever can't get out of bad air passes out and then dies of it: see **Without air** under [Bodies](#bodies).
- **Cameras and the alarm:** a camera watching you makes sneaking harder, by 2. Tamper with anything where a camera can see, and the alarm rings. The room washes red, the guards head for it if comms are up, the doors the place's file names lock, and whoever holds the place thinks less of you, by 2. It also goes on the record that you were seen, for the stories to react to. The alarm stops after half an hour, or from a computer that runs it.
- **Doors:** a locked door stops anyone walking through it, both ways. Its window offers **Force the door** or **Hack the door panel**, which opens the panel as a computer that runs that door. A forced door won't lock again until it's fixed.
- **A keypad beside every door:** every door a place's systems run has its keypad on the wall beside it, outside the room where there's a wall for it, so it can be reached by someone locked out. Where no wall is near the door it stands on a little post of its own. Its screen is green while the door's open, red once it's locked, and dark when its room has no power. **Use its keypad**, from its window or its right-click menu, opens the door's panel right there: aboard your own ship you're straight in to lock the door, open it or give it a new code; anywhere else it wants its code, and your rig's breakers work on it once the rig is connected with an optical reader; a sealed one, like the Armory's, says what it answers to. It works from any square right beside it, whichever room that square counts as, and **Step away** closes it.
- **The other devices are where they stand too:** the comms relay on the bridge has its service menu in the room, like a junction box (**Open its service menu**, with a service clip in your rig), and the navigation core is its own cabinet on the bridge: **Use it** says what it answers to, since it's sealed.
- **Computers run them:** a computer that runs a place's systems has a **Systems** list on its screen, each part with its state and buttons to switch it, and a camera's screen shows who it sees. On a hacked computer each switch costs a point of trace, and cutting the power to a computer's own room turns its screen off with everything else.
- **The place puts things right:** its engineers fix what's broken, in a few hours, and you can see one walk over to do it. Its people switch back what you switched off, after a while: six hours, or within the hour when it's something the air needs and a room's air has already gone bad for want of it. What the story changes stays changed.
- **Things wear out:** aboard the Meridian, something gives out by itself about once every ten days: a speaker, a junction box, the air plant, the comms relay or the engines. The engineers mend it as they would anything broken, with a parts crate where a repair takes one; with no crate aboard it stays broken, and Chief Marrow asks you to bodge it by hand (see [Requests](#requests)). The air plant, and the junction box in Engineering that feeds it, never give out by themselves while there's no parts crate aboard to mend them with: the engineers nurse them along, so a ship with empty parts lockers isn't lost to one burnt-out breaker in the middle of a long wait. A fire, a fight or your own hand can still break either. How often is the `a part wears out every:` line in `world/systems/meridian.txt`; a place without one never wears out.
- **The Meridian is yours:** as captain you run all her systems from the **Ship** menu, with no hacking, and nothing you do aboard her sets off the alarm. With her engines down she can't jump. With her navigation down every jump is plotted by hand, a fuel more and half as long again. A station with its docking clamps locked won't let her leave.
- **The story:** the written story can ask about the systems and change them, with lines like `? alarm ringing at haven` or `* power off in The Docks`.
- **Where they're written:** `world/systems/` has a file for the Meridian, Haven Station and Tessen Colony, as drafts for the owner to rewrite. Every other place gets systems made from its rooms and things, the way `world/systems/_defaults.json` says, and the alien places get none. `_how-to-write-systems.txt` there explains it all. The **Story board** shows any place's systems under **Systems**, its own or the defaults, and saving one there makes it the place's own file.

## Who you are

**For now a new game is always the captain of the Meridian.** The game knows 21 other lives, aboard the Meridian or out in the world: an engine-room hand, a dock hand at Haven Station, a miner at Cinder Reach, a card dealer on the Gilded Wheel, a scavenger at the Pale Gate and the rest. Since 12 November 2026 they're switched off, at the owner's word: the **Your place** step of creating a character shows only **Captain of the Meridian**, and **Surprise me** makes a captain. Nothing was thrown away. A game you saved as one of the others still opens and plays as it did, and a life comes back by taking its `"off": true` line out of `world/starts.json`.

A life decides where you start, who you know and where you stand with each faction. After that the world goes on over your head, and what you do about it is up to you. In a game saved as someone who isn't the captain:

- **Someone else has the chair.** It's Captain Reddick, who's careful, Captain Marchetti, who's bold, or Captain Lund, who's somewhere between, and it's a different one from game to game. They're on the Meridian's Bridge like anyone else, and you can go and talk to them.
- **They really run the ship.** They move her when she needs something, and buy it at the dock out of her own money, every day, on your computer. It happens whether you're aboard or on the far side of the map. They keep at least four crates of medicine in the cabinet when they can buy it, because a fever or a bad week can take several at once, and they buy what's run out straight away.
- **The Ship menu tells you what you'd know.** Aboard the Meridian, or on the same dock as her, you see how she stands, what's been decided and her log. Anywhere else, you only get where she was last heard of. You can't give her orders, her systems aren't yours to run, and **Wait** just lets the time go by. The story only tells you about the ship while you're with her, or if it's big news.
- **Getting around costs money.** The **Star map** shows a fare and a time instead of fuel, and **Book passage** takes you there by yourself, on whatever's going that way. The further and the more dangerous it is, the more it costs, and it's slower than a jump. A few places have no passage at all, because nobody goes there for ordinary reasons, so you'd need the story to get you there. Going aboard the Meridian costs a fare too, unless she's docked where you are.
- **You can work.** Where your job is, you'll find it at the bottom of your registry printout (click your name, or press C), and under **You can** in **What's here**. A shift takes eight hours, wears you out, and pays a few credits, a little more as your skill improves.
- **You have to sleep somewhere.** It's free where you live and aboard the Meridian. Anywhere else, you can rent a bunk for 6 credits, or sleep rough for nothing, which doesn't bring back your health or energy, and leaves you no calmer. Both are in the same two places as your job, and at the bottom of your registry printout.
- **Leads are written for you.** Stories reach you the way they'd reach someone who isn't in charge. The few that only work for the captain, such as the Commandant's request, stay closed.
- **It can change.** If the story gets there, a written scene can hand you the chair, or take it away from you. The orders and the **Ship** menu's buttons then become yours, or stop being yours, and the ship goes on from wherever she's got to.

## Bodies

Everyone in the game has a body, made up when a new game starts: the people with files, everyone with only a name, and you. A body has six parts (the head, the chest, both arms and both legs), and each part can carry old marks from a life, like a scar, an old break, old burns or two fingers gone, and wounds of now, like a cut, a broken bone or a burn. Start another game and the same people have different marks, though what someone's own file says is always there: Chief Marrow is always missing two fingers from his left hand, and Rennick always limps.

- **Where they come from.** How many old marks someone has goes by their age, and rough work, like the engine room, the docks or the marines, brings them twice as often and makes the marks that go with it likelier: burns in the engine room, a bad knee on the docks, scars on a guard. Synthetics carry their own kind, like scuffed casing or a replaced joint, and don't bleed.
- **Your own body** comes from your life story. The injuries in it leave their marks, like the burns over half your body from losing your ship in an ambush, or the lungs that never quite recovered from a lab accident, and so do the traits One-Eyed, Old Wound and the cybernetic arm and eye. The wounds you have now sit on a part: a broken arm is on one of your arms, a concussion on your head, and blood loss lowers your blood.
- **Where to see them.** **Look** at someone (right-click them) and you read how they walk if it isn't normally, and the worst of what can be seen on them. Their **Registry** printout has the chart of their body: a figure with each part shaded by the worst on it, how they walk, their blood, and every mark and wound, worst first. Your own is on your printout (**C**) and on the figure in your inventory (**I**).
- **Vik and Hana** start the game laid up on the medbay's cots, Vik with a broken leg and Hana with a deep burn. Once they've been treated and have rested, they get up and go back to their days, and their cots are free for whoever needs them next.
- **Fights land on the body.** Every hit lands on a part, and hard hits leave wounds that stay after the fight: see [Hits land on the body](#hits-land-on-the-body).
- **Wounds bleed until they're dressed.** A cut bleeds 4 an hour and a deep cut 12, and blood goes down with it, on the printout's blood bar. Once nothing bleeds, blood comes back at 2 an hour. Under 60, every check you make is harder. Where there's a medbay (aboard the Meridian, and on Anselm's Mercy), someone whose blood runs out dies of it, and so do you (see **Your own death**, below); anywhere else nobody does: there it stops at 35. The top bar says **HURT** with how many things are wrong with you, wounds and conditions together, and **BLEEDING** with the blood you have left while a wound's still open; and the story tells you once when you've lost a lot of it and are still bleeding.
- **Dressing.** Anyone dresses a wound that isn't serious themselves, an hour after they get it, and the story says when you dress yours. A serious one, like a deep cut, a deep burn or a broken bone, waits for someone else: the medics in the medbay, or, for you, a **medkit**, which now dresses or splints your worst wound as well as healing you. The printout says which wounds are still bleeding, and which are dressed or splinted.
- **Wounds go bad.** A cut, a burn or a deep cut still undressed after two days goes bad. A wound gone bad is serious, and makes your checks harder. Aboard the Meridian, one still undressed five days after it went bad kills, and yours is no different: the story tells you each day it's left how long it leaves you.
- **Healing.** Each wound heals in its own time: a bruise in 2 days, a cut in 4 (2 once dressed), a deep cut in 8 once dressed, a broken arm in 30 once splinted, a broken leg in 40 once splinted. One that only heals once it's treated takes twice as long left alone. Yours heal faster while you sleep and slower while the Meridian is out of medicine, and a Thessan's at three quarters the pace. When one of yours heals, the story says so.
- **Walking.** A wound on a leg, or a bad knee, is a limp: anyone with one walks at 60% of the pace, and on a leg they can't stand on, like a broken one, they hobble at 30%. That's you too, walking around and in a fight in the room.
- **Hands.** With a hand that's no use, like a broken arm, nobody carries crates for the stores or works in the galley, and you can't work your shift.
- **Your head, and the pain.** A concussion makes every check harder, and your wounds wear on you: 2 stress a day for each, 5 for a serious one.
- **The morning queue.** From 07:45 to 10:30, anyone aboard the Meridian who's hurt and on their feet goes and waits in the medbay, and nobody else does. Now and then someone hurts themselves at their work, more often in rough work like the engine room or the galley: a burn, a cut or a bruise. The medics see to the queue once the laid up have been treated.
- **Down where they fell.** Someone beaten in a fight, or out cold, with no leg they can stand on, or too weak from losing blood (under 40), lies where they fell, with a red cross over them, instead of vanishing. **Look** at them for how they are and how fast they're bleeding. Someone beaten stays down an hour to three at least, and anyone stays down while they're still bleeding. Nobody lying down can be attacked or have a pocket picked, and someone lying where they fell keeps whatever they had to ask you until they're awake on a cot.
- **The medics come.** Aboard the Meridian the call goes to the medbay at once: a nurse (or, with both nurses busy, a spare hand) walks out to them, picks them up, carries them back through the decks and lays them on one of the medbay's four cots, all in front of you, and the story says so. With the cots full, they go on the medbay's floor. They stay until they can get up, have stopped bleeding and have been treated, and then the cot's free. Someone beaten who can still walk gets up after a while and makes their own way to the medbay, slowly. While someone's down or laid up, the watch bill covers their job.
- **You get carried too.** Beaten aboard the Meridian, you come to on a cot in the medbay hours later, with all your money: nobody robs you there. Anywhere else it's as before: you come round where you fell, a quarter of your credits gone.
- **You can carry someone.** Right-click someone down for **Carry them**: you walk over and pick them up, and walk at half your pace while you carry them. Right-click the floor for **Put them down**: where you stand, or on a free cot if you're in the medbay. You can't fight or pick a pocket with someone in your arms. If they die while you're carrying them, they died in your arms, as **Look** and the record say, and you're still carrying them until you put them down.
- **Treatment.** Dr. Aduro and the nurses treat whoever's laid up, one wound at a time, the worst off first, then the morning queue, and you too, if you stand in the medbay with a wound that needs them. A dressed cut stops bleeding at once, a splinted bone starts to knit, and a treated wound heals at its treated pace. Each treatment takes doses of medicine and some time: a cut 1 dose and 10 minutes, a deep cut 2 and half an hour, a broken bone 2 and an hour, a deep burn 3 and an hour, and so on. The better the medic's Medicine, the quicker: Dr. Aduro is faster than a nurse. Walk out in the middle of yours and it stops, and the doses go back. While you're in the medbay, the story says who treats whom.
- **The medicine.** The medics open a crate from the medicine cabinet when the one they're using is empty, and a crate holds 20 doses. With the cabinet empty, nobody can be treated until a crate is brought, and the story says so if you're there.
- **Bed rest.** Someone with a serious wound stays on their cot for three days after it's been treated, and then gets up. Whoever's waiting on the floor moves to a cot as one comes free, the worst off first.
- **The laid up** can still be talked to: right-click them on their cot for **Talk**. Someone out cold can't. A story waiting on someone laid up waits for you to go to them in the medbay, and the quest tracker says where they are; if they're laid up while you're at another place, it says they're laid up in the medbay.
- **On the Ship menu**, the medbay's section shows each cot and who's on it, their worst wound, how long they've been there and whether they're waiting, being treated or resting, then who's on the floor, the doses left in the open crate, and **Treated lately**, folded.
- **Dr. Aduro's record.** The chief medical officer is now also marked on **patients left waiting** (10 off for each hour someone laid up waited with nobody treating them, after their first hour) and **people reached in time** (25 off for each call reached more than half an hour after someone fell), on the Command menu.
- **When help doesn't come.** Death is possible now, but only when nobody reaches someone in time: they bleed to death where nobody comes for them, or on a cot with no medicine to dress them, or die of a wound gone bad that's left undressed. With the nurses free and the doors open, it almost never happens. The same goes for you: see **Your own death**, below.
- **Without air.** People die without air now, wherever they are. Everyone gets out of a room whose air has gone bad, to the nearest room that still has good air (before, most of the crew stayed where they were: anyone at a bench, a bunk or a console). Whoever can't get out is without air: someone lying where they fell in such a room, and everyone at a place once no room of it has good air left, which aboard the Meridian means her air plant stopped, the junction box in Engineering dead, no power at all or no water in her tank, and her reserve air used up. After two to three hours of it they pass out where they are, and an hour or two after that, still without air, they die: each person has their own two times, the same all game. Carried somewhere they can breathe, by a nurse or by you (**Carry them**), or once the air's back, they come round ten minutes later, and they get their breath back twice as fast as they lost it.
- **Who's spared.** Whoever's in a bad room on work the ship's work board or the medbay sent them to, while there's good air elsewhere: an engineer at a fire or a breach works in a mask. Whoever the medics have laid on a cot: there are masks at the cots, which only fail once there's nowhere left aboard with good air. And synthetics, who don't breathe: Mercy Seven and the seventy or so on the register never pass out or die for want of air, and don't leave a room for it. You aren't spared: see **Your own death**, below.
- **Reserve air.** With her air plant stopped, for whatever reason, the Meridian breathes her reserve air: five days of it, reaching every room whose own vent hasn't been shut or broken, power or none. The story says when it starts ("The air plant has stopped. The ship is breathing reserve air now: there's 5 days of it, and after that nobody lasts more than a few hours."), when a day of it is left, and when it's gone. **Look** at the air plant says how much is left. Once the plant runs again the reserve fills back up within a day. Five days is long enough for a distress call's tanker to reach a ship with no fuel cells (see [The ship](#the-ship)); it's the `reserve air: 5` line in `world/systems/meridian.txt`. Stations have none: their people switch the air back on instead.
- **A ship with no air** loses everyone who breathes, you with them if you're aboard. The story says once that there's no good air left anywhere aboard and that nobody lasts more than a few hours. People passing out in the room you're in are said one by one, the rest together every quarter of an hour; each of the crew who dies is named, with the room ("dies, suffocated in the Long Mess"); and the people on the register die by the quarters they sleep in, counted together ("78 more of the people on the register have died for want of air. That's 117 of them."). Every name goes up on the Names Bulkhead, the Ship menu's medbay lists them under **Died**, each death costs its morale, and the records terminal says how each of the nine hundred ended. A day without power kills nobody, since there's the reserve. Once the reserve is gone, six hours kill the ship.
- **Stopping the air yourself.** As captain, nobody aboard starts the air plant again for you, so shutting it down says so once, plainly: "That stops the air for the whole ship. She has reserve air for 5 days; after that nobody aboard lasts more than a few hours without it, and nobody will start it again but you." If you're one of the crew and not her captain, someone starts it again after six hours, as with anything you switch off. At a station, its people put the air back on within the hour once a room's air has gone bad.
- **Where it's written.** Two lines at the top of `world/health/wounds.txt`: `without air: out cold after 2 to 3 hours of bad air, dead 1 to 2 hours after that`, and `back in good air: comes round after 10 minutes, and gets their breath back twice as fast as they lost it`. And one wound, `no air, the chest: passed out for want of air; out cold until they can breathe; not for: synthetic`: its last words are what spares the synthetics. Take them off and synthetics need air like anyone; take the line out and nobody passes out or dies of it. The Story board's **Health** page shows all three as boxes.
- **To try it:** as captain, walk to Engineering, click the air plant and **Hit the shutdown switch**. Open the **Ship** menu and click **Wait 3 days**, then **Wait 1 day** twice: that's the five days of reserve air. Close the menu, set the clock to **Fast** and stay in a room with people in it. About two hours on the first of them drops, and an hour after that the first dies. **Start it up again** before then and they all come round. You're aboard too: stay, and a little over two hours on you pass out with the rest, nobody comes, and the game ends on a page of the ship's log, with **Go back to this morning** on it. (**Wait 3 days** twice shows the whole of it in the wait's own lines, without watching, and ends the same way.)
- **Your own death.** Since 2026-11-16 your character can die, of the same things that kill the crew: no air, bleeding out, a wound gone bad, a fever nobody doses. You always go down first, someone carries you to the medbay as they would anyone, and you die only when nobody reaches you in time. A lost fight is as it was: it ends on a cot.
- **Going down.** Three things take your legs. Passing out for want of air: after your own two to three hours of bad air in the room you're standing in, or as soon as the bad air has taken your health down to 1. Too little blood: under 40, where there's a medbay. And a wound gone bad or a fever, twelve hours before it would kill you. Whatever you were doing is over: a talk, a computer, someone in your arms. (A skill check you're in the middle of is finished first.) A medkit or any other dressing you carry goes on first, one for each wound that's bleeding, or for the wound gone bad or the fever; if that settles it, you never go down at all ("You get a medkit onto it in time").
- **Who comes for you.** Whoever answers a call for anyone: aboard the Meridian a nurse, then a spare hand, and a sleeping nurse is woken. A locked door in the way keeps them out, as it does for the crew. Half an hour later you're on a cot in the medbay, where the doctor and the nurses treat you in your turn with what the cabinet has, and where there's a mask for the air. At the dock the Meridian's at, her shuttle crew bring her own people across to her medbay. Anywhere with no medbay you lie where you fell. You see none of it happen: the time goes by, the story says what was done, and then where you came round, how much later, and who carried you. "You come round on a cot in the medbay, 7 hours later. Nurse Haru carried you here."
- **When nobody reaches you in time,** you die. Of the air: an hour or two after passing out, still without it, where you fell or on the cot once there's no air left anywhere aboard. Of your blood: when it runs out, where you fell or on a cot with nothing in the cabinet to dress you. Of a wound gone bad: twelve hours after it took your legs, still untreated. Of a fever: at the doctor's midnight rounds, twelve hours or more after it took them, with still no medicine aboard. Away from a medbay your blood stops at 35 and a wound gone bad doesn't kill, as for anyone there; the air kills anywhere.
- **You're warned.** Bad air says so when it starts, and takes your health as it goes ("You're close to passing out" near the end). Bleeding is on the top bar, and the story says when you've lost a lot of blood. A wound gone bad is said each day it's left, with the days it leaves you ("Left like this it will kill you in 2 days: a medkit will see to it, or the medbay"). A fever with no medicine aboard is said each night on the rounds.
- **The ending.** The game ends on a page of the ship's log, in red, over everything: your name and who you were, the day and how you died, why nobody could help when there's a reason, what became of the ship, and the last things the story said, the line where you went down first. The clock has stopped and nothing behind the page answers, by the mouse or the keys. Its ways out, by a click or their number: **Go back to this morning**, **New game**, and **The title screen**, where your own saved games are. Opened again later, the game is still over, and still offers them.
- **The mornings.** The game keeps a save by itself the first time it's saved after six each morning: the last three mornings of the game you're playing. They're in `data/saves` beside your own, and they aren't on the title screen: the ending offers them, newest first, as "this morning", "yesterday morning", or by their day and hour. If you died in the very thing that crossed into a new morning, a day waited in one go for instance, the game as it stood just before that is kept as the morning's, so dying never costs more than the day. Go back to an older morning and play on, and the newer ones are dropped as new mornings come. Another game's mornings make way for the game you're playing.
- **Synthetics don't breathe.** A synthetic character of yours takes nothing from bad air and never passes out for it: the story says the air's bad and that it's nothing to you. On a ship with no air left, you and the other synthetics are the ones still standing.
- **Everyone aboard dead but you** doesn't end the game: you keep the ship, alone. Nothing more is built for that yet: there's no new crew to find, and nobody to work her.
- **A long wait is cut where it has to be.** While the air's going where you stand, a wait goes by a few minutes at a time, and it's cut at the moment your blood, a wound gone bad or a fever would take your legs, so that you go down when you should and not at the end of the day you asked to wait. The time you were down comes out of the wait.
- **Where it's written.** Two more lines at the top of `world/health/wounds.txt`: `you collapse: 12 hours before a wound gone bad or a fever would kill you`, and `bleeding stops at: 35, away from a medbay`. The air's times are the `without air` line's, the blood that takes your legs the `too weak to stand` line's, and what kills is on the wounds' own lines (`kills after 5 days undressed`, `kills 1 in 3 left untreated 5 days`).
- **To try it:** to see the fall and the cot, stand in the Long Mess, click its vent and choose **Seal the vent** (an easy check), set the clock to **Faster** and wait: the air's bad in about twenty minutes, and a little over two hours later you go down and come round in the medbay. To see the ending, do as under **Without air** above and stay aboard.
- **What keeps the medics out.** A locked medbay door: nobody goes out to a call, nobody's carried in, and nobody queues there in the morning. A locked door to the room someone's lying in. A medbay with no power, where nobody can be treated. An empty medicine cabinet. When the nurse on duty is out and no spare hand is awake, the call wakes the other nurse. The story says why nobody's coming.
- **The dead.** Someone who dies lying down stays there under a blanket, and **Look** says how they died. A click on them opens their menu; nobody can talk to them or carry them. Once the way's open, whoever answers calls carries the body to the Quiet Room, and then they're gone. That goes for everyone, an officer with a file of their own as much as a deckhand.
- **The names.** Their name goes up on the Names Bulkhead on deck 1: **Look** at the chalked names for the newest, or **Use** them to read who, which day and how. The Ship menu's medbay lists them too, under **Died**.
- **What follows a death.** One less aboard, and the ship's morale drops by 3. A story person's contact is dead, and any story waiting on them fails. Whatever post they held goes to whoever its chooser picks, and someone from the crowd leaves the watch bill, the spare hand covering for them keeping the job for good. Dr. Aduro's record and the captain's count the death, and `<their name>_died` goes on the record, such as `mess_hand_bao_died`, for a story to react to.
- **Losing a limb.** Now and then, a hard hit on an arm or a leg that's already broken takes it off: one time in four. A limb whose wound has gone bad and been left three days is taken by the doctor when it's finally treated, "the wound had gone too far". A lost arm leaves the hand no use and takes 3 off the attack; a lost leg can't be stood on. Either bleeds badly until the stump's dressed in the medbay, and it never grows back.
- **Artificial limbs.** For anyone who's lost a limb and had the stump dressed, a tech walks to the Repair Bay and builds an artificial arm or leg from 2 parts crates, in about six hours, quicker the better their Engineering; then the doctor fits it in the medbay, in about two hours. It works nearly as well: an artificial arm takes 1 off the attack, and an artificial leg walks a little stiffly, at 90% of the pace. With no parts crates aboard, nobody gets one until some come. Now and then someone has one from before they came aboard, more often in rough work.
- **Synthetics are mended, not treated.** Their dents, cracked casing and ruined joints are mended in the Repair Bay by the chief engineer or a tech, not by the nurses: a dent in 20 minutes, cracked casing with a parts crate in an hour, a ruined joint with a parts crate in two. One who can't get up is carried to the Repair Bay's bench; one who's hurt and on their feet goes there in the morning, instead of the medbay queue.
- **On the Ship menu**, the Repair Bay's section says who's being mended or what's being built and how long it has left, who's waiting for a limb, which limbs are ready to fit, the parts crates aboard, and what was done lately.
- **The register.** The Meridian carries nine hundred, and most of them have no day of their own: they're on the register instead, each made up when the game starts, with a name, an age, a species and the quarters they sleep in (the Overflow, the Hot Bunks or the Aft Holds). The same people all game, and different ones in the next.
- **Illness.** A cough and a fever go round the ship. Each is caught now and then, more often by someone sharing quarters with someone who has it, twice as often on half rations and half again as often on reduced, or when the kettle runs dry for some of the sittings. A cough goes by itself in six days. A fever is serious: eight days, or three once it's dosed, and left undosed for five days it kills one time in three. The crew catch them too, and so do you while you're aboard: a fever is on your printout and makes your checks harder, though it never kills you. Synthetics catch nothing.
- **The doctor's rounds.** Once a day, at midnight, the rounds dose everyone seriously ill, on the register and in the crew alike: one dose each, from a crate the rounds take from the medicine cabinet for themselves. The medbay's log says which of the crew were dosed. The sick crew still go to the morning queue, but their doses come on the rounds. With the cabinet empty and the rounds' crate used up, nobody's dosed, and the fevers start to kill.
- **The ship's health** settles where the rations put it, less a point for every hundredth of the register who's sick, and three for every hundredth who's seriously sick.
- **Deaths with names.** Someone on the register who dies of a fever costs a point of morale. When the ship's health is so low that people die of it, the ones who die are on the register, the seriously ill, the ill and the oldest first, and they cost nothing more than the ship's health already does. Every name goes up with the rest on the Names Bulkhead: **Look** shows the newest, and **Use** the last dozen, with how many more there are.
- **The medicine** now goes only a dose at a time, by the medbay's treatments and the rounds, so a ship that's mostly well takes very little, and the Ship menu counts its days from the doses of the last week.
- **The records terminal** stands beside the scan bed in the medbay. **Use** it, and under **Logs** is **The register of everyone aboard**: everyone, crew and register, with their name, age, species, quarters and how they are, and a count at the top. Click a heading to sort by it, and again to turn it round. Sorted by **How they are**, the dead come first, then the laid up, the seriously ill, the sick and the well.
- **On the Ship menu**, the medbay's section has a line for the rounds: how many on the register are sick and with what, how many seriously and how many of those have been dosed, and how many died this week, in red while the seriously ill outnumber the dosed. The open crate's line says how many doses the rounds still carry.
- **To try it:** put the ship on half rations for a week, then open the medbay's records terminal and sort by **How they are**.
- **The story knows how someone is.** A written scene, a conversation or a story's clock can ask whether someone's hurt (`? hurt tarrow`), badly hurt, ill, can walk (`? can walk tarrow`, or `? can't walk tarrow`), is laid up in the medbay (`? laid up tarrow`) or is dead: you, anyone aboard by their name, a story person anywhere, or any. A scene can wound someone (`* wound tarrow deep cut on the left arm: The reason`, or without the part for one the game picks), heal them (`* heal tarrow: The reason`, or one kind of wound), or let them die in its own words (`* dies tarrow: of her wounds, before anyone came`): they fall where they stand and are carried to the Quiet Room like anyone who dies. A story can't kill you. What a scene does goes on the record as a fact (`tarrow_wounded`, `tarrow_healed`, `tarrow_died`), and a limb lost in a fight or to rot leaves one too (`tarrow_lost_a_limb`), so later stories can react. `npm run check-story` says what's wrong with a line, and `world/scenes/_how-to-write-a-scene.txt` lists it all.
- **Tarrow says how she is.** Ask her how she's doing, and her answer follows how she is: laid up on a cot, unable to stand, badly hurt, scratched, or well. The new answers are my draft for you to rewrite, in `world/scenes/talk_tarrow.txt`.
- **On the Story board**, **Health** shows the wounds file and the medbay as boxes, and a species' body has a box of its own (see **Story board** below).
- **Anselm's Mercy has a ward.** The hospital ship's Long Ward has six cots, and its nurses and orderly carry whoever goes down aboard her to one, the same way the Meridian's do. Dr. Adeyemi, Doc Marsh and the nurses treat them with the ship's own medicine, and the walking hurt queue on the Sorting Deck from 06:30 to 09:30. Knocked down there yourself, you come to on a cot in the Long Ward, and they charge you 2 credits for each dose they give you, or what you have if it's less. Someone left there without help can die, as aboard the Meridian; you can't.
- **At the Meridian's dock.** Knocked down at a port with no clinic while the Meridian is at its dock, and she's your home (you're her captain or her crew), her shuttle crew find you and bring you back aboard, and you come to in her medbay with nothing taken. Anyone else, or anywhere else, comes round where they fell, a quarter of their credits gone, and nobody dies of their wounds there.
- **More clinics.** Any place can have one: a file in `world/health` named for the place, with its ward, its cots (spots in the place's file in `world/life`), who answers a call and who treats, by name where the place has no chain of command, its medicine (its own, from what the port has, as buying it would), and what a dose costs you. `world/health/_how-to-write-health.txt` says how, and the Story board's Health page shows each one. The other ports have none yet: they have no room for one and nobody who treats people, so who patches people up at Haven, Kessara and the rest is yours to write.
- **Where they're written.** `world/health/wounds.txt` has every kind of wound and old mark, where on the body it can be, what it looks like and its rules, and how often people gather old marks. `world/health/meridian.txt` (and `anselm.txt`, Anselm's Mercy's ward) has who starts the game hurt aboard the Meridian, the medbay's room and cots, who answers a call, who treats, where the medicine is, the bed rest and what each treatment takes, where the dead are laid out, what their names go up on and what a death aboard costs, how long fitting a limb takes, the workshop, who mends there and where its parts crates come from, what each kind of mending and each artificial limb takes, when the morning queue is, and how often people have accidents at work, where the people on the register sleep, the doses the doctor's rounds give a serious illness, and what a death on the register costs. The wounds file also has which wounds are illnesses (how often they're caught, how they spread in the same quarters, whether hunger makes them worse, and how often one left undosed kills), how much likelier hunger makes them, and how blood, bleeding, dressing, healing, walking and pain go, and which wounds accidents leave. A species' file has a **Body** part with how often a hit lands on each part and what its people do differently, and a person's file can have a **Body** part with what's always true of them, which the game never changes. `world/health/_how-to-write-health.txt` explains all of it. `npm run check-story` checks it too.

## The ship

The Meridian is a frigate built for three hundred, carrying nine hundred, and she's on her own: the fleet she once led is a thing of the past. The captain feeds, fuels and moves her, and she can run out of things. This section is how that works if the captain's you. If it isn't, someone else is doing all of this: see [Who you are](#who-you-are). All of it runs on this computer, and what happens shows in the story as a line or two.

**Stores.** Food, medicine and parts are what's in the rooms' stores, described below, counted together: food is every ration crate and basket of produce aboard, counted as crates. Fuel is the tank, for jumps. Nine hundred people eat about nine crates a day at the kettle, the ship burns a little under half a unit of fuel a day keeping station, the medbay and the doctor's rounds open a crate of medicine each time they've used one up, a dose at a time, more often the sicker people are, and the engineers a crate of parts about once a week. While a store is empty nothing's used from it and nothing's owed, so the first crates to come aboard after a shortage last their whole time. The shuttle stores are topped up every six hours, so the food reads **holding** on the Ship menu instead of a count of days, and settles at twenty-odd crates: the shuttle stores full, the pantry kept at about four by Brandt, and the produce eaten as fast as the garden grows it. Nobody counts the days of a supply that's holding: the council won't cut the rations for it, and whoever has the chair won't go shopping for it. She starts with about two weeks of fuel, twelve of parts, and enough medicine for months while the ship stays well, unless you do something about it. You'll get a warning in the story when something's about five days from running out, and then:

| Runs out | What it does |
|---|---|
| Food | Morale and health fall fast, and you go hungry yourself, which costs you Endurance |
| Fuel | She can't jump, and the lower decks go cold |
| Medicine | Health falls, people aboard start dying, and your own wounds heal half as fast |
| Parts | Nothing gets mended, and the hull wears a little every day |
| Water | The galley can't cook and nothing grows, and once the air plant has used what it had, it stops making air |

**In the rooms.** Her stocks are also kept somewhere you can walk to: the shuttle stores in the lighter bay, the fuel rack in engineering, the pantry in the galley, the garden bins, the medicine cabinet in the medbay, the parts lockers in stores, the comforts locker and the soup kettle in the mess. (The water tank in the pump room and the reserve tanks in life support are there to walk up to as well, but like the kettle they hold a count, not things to take out: see **Water and reserve air** below.) Walk up to one and press **Open**, in its window or its right-click menu, and its grid opens beside your pack, the way a pile of loot does: take a crate out, put it back, and the Ship menu's **In the rooms** section follows the count. They start full. Where each one is and what it holds is written in `world/logistics/meridian.txt`, which the **Story board** shows under **Logistics**, and `npm run check-story` reads it too.

**The machines and the sittings.** The same file says what the ship's machines do, and they run on the clock, at the same beat as everyone's days. While a cook's in the galley and the room has power, the galley turns a basket of produce or a ration crate from the pantry into two hundred sittings in the soup kettle every hour; while a gardener's in the garden, the beds give a basket of produce into the garden bins every two hours; the generator is a machine too, and has its own paragraph below. The Ship menu's **Machines** lines say what each is doing, or why it's waiting: no power, waiting for a cook, its store full, or nothing left to cook. Everyone aboard eats from the kettle at four sittings a day, two in the morning and two in the evening, four hundred and fifty people at each, and the kettle says how much is left in it when you look. Whoever finds it empty goes without: the people you can see eating are "finding the kettle empty" instead, you're told when it runs dry, and at midnight the ship's morale feels the share who went hungry, through the same rule as food running out, with a line in the ship's log. Nothing happens while the clock is paused. The daily count above still runs on its own for now, and a later step makes the two one count.

**The work board.** Nobody writes routes for the crew. Each store's line in the ship's file says what it wants kept in it, and the ship's work board does the rest: the fuel rack in engineering wants three cells, the pantry four ration crates and four baskets of produce, the parts lockers two crates, the medicine cabinet two. Whenever a store has fewer than it wants, a job comes up, and it's fetched from the nearest store that can spare the thing: crates and cells from the shuttle stores in the lighter bay, produce from the garden's bins. A store only gives what it has over what it wants itself. **The deckhands carry first**, the four who work in the lighter bay beside the shuttle stores. If none of them is free it's the store's own people (the techs for the rack and the lockers, the cooks and then the quartermaster for the pantry, the nurses for the medicine cabinet), and after them the spare hands. As many go at once as the want needs, each with what one trip carries (two crates, two cells, four baskets), and what they've gone for is set aside for them, so two people never go for the same crates. You'll see them leave what they were doing, walk there, stand at the store a few minutes, and walk back with the load, their line saying what they're carrying all the way; then they go back to their day. Take the pantry's crates yourself and watch two deckhands go down for more.

**Urgent, and nothing to bring.** A want is urgent when the store is under the smaller mark its line gives (the fuel rack's is two cells), or when whatever uses the thing up is asking: the generator on its last cell, the galley with a bare pantry, the medbay with someone waiting and no doses left, a repair waiting for its spare. Urgent jobs are given out before the others, and one wakes a sleeper when nobody else is up; anything else that runs short at night waits for whoever's on watch, or for the morning. When nothing aboard can be spared, nobody is sent to stand at an empty store: the line says there's nothing aboard to bring, and it's for you to buy some.

**Nobody is in two places.** The board is the only thing that sends anyone to carry or to mend, one job to a person, and it knows who's already taken by something outside it: a guard at an alarm, whoever's down or carrying the fallen, whoever's walking with you. They're given nothing until they're free, and the next of the store's carriers goes instead. A carrier who's called away hands the job back: before they've loaded, the next free hand starts over; with a load in their arms, it's set down and the next free hand carries it on, so nothing is lost and nothing is doubled. Someone laid up carries nothing. Bad air stops nobody who's carrying, because a ship with no power has no air, and the cell that brings both back still has to reach the rack. A walk takes as long as the way through the decks is, at the walker's own pace, whether you're watching, resting or ashore: a trip with crates from the lighter bay to the pantry is five minutes to load and about ten to walk.

**The Work page.** **Work**, under The ship, is the board itself. At the top it says how many of the people the board has work for are free, and what the officers have ordered. Then comes every store with a row of squares, a filled one for each thing it has and an empty one for each it still wants, with what's on its way to it and what's set aside to leave it, and the kettle's sittings with the time of the next one. Then every job, the most urgent first: `!!` marks an urgent one and `!` one in the part of the ship that's been put first, each with who's on it and since when, or why it's stuck ("nobody free: 2 are asleep, Tech Sami is mending the camera in The Bridge", "nothing aboard to bring"). The jobs are everything the crew is sent to do: carrying, repairs, carrying the fallen and the dead, treating, the workshop's bench, fires, breaches and alarms. Point at a job for who does that kind of work. The Ship menu keeps the stores, the machines, the refills, the repairs and the sittings, with one line saying how many stores are short.

**The officers' orders.** The captain gives the board no orders: the officers do. Whoever is first officer looks at how the ship stands about once an hour and puts one part of her first: the medbay when people are lying where they fell or waiting with no medicine, the engines when the generator has stopped or is on its last cell, the galley when the kettle is empty or the cooks have nothing to cook, repairs when they're waiting for parts. It's whichever is in the most trouble, or nothing at all ("As it comes"). Jobs in that part go before the rest when hands are short, after the urgent ones, and the page says whose order it is, since when, and why. Whoever is quartermaster looks every four hours or so: a store that ran low since their last look wants a trip's worth more from then on, up to what it holds (the fuel rack goes from three cells to five), and one that's been quiet for three days is eased back a step. How often each of them looks goes by their skill for the post, so a better first officer notices trouble sooner. With nobody in the post, or its holder dead, locked up or laid up, nobody decides: jobs go as they come, and the stores want what they wanted. If you'd have decided otherwise, give the post to someone else in the Command menu, or carry it yourself.

**I'll do it.** A job nobody's on has an **I'll do it** button. The crew then leaves it to you, for two hours, or three quarters of an hour if it's urgent, and it's in the corner of the screen like a quest, saying how many to bring and where there are some. Carry the things into the store yourself: open the store they're in, take them, walk over, open the other and put them in. It's done when the store has what it wants. If you don't get to it in time the crew takes it back, and **Leave it to the crew** gives it back at once. When the store is one your own job carries for, it's paid by the piece as before. This is the fourth step of the logistics rework. The numbers in it (an hour, four hours, three days, two hours) are a first guess.

**Repairs are on the board too.** Something that breaks is mended, as before, some time after it broke, an engineer spending its last hour fetching the spare and working at it. What's new is who: the board gives each repair to the first engineer who's free, the chief engineer and then the techs, **one repair to an engineer**. Break three things at once and three of them go; break a fourth and it waits until one is free, and its hour starts when someone takes it. The Work page lists every repair: who's on it and what they're doing, when the engineers will start, that it's waiting for a spare, or why nobody has come ("nobody free: Chief Engineer Marrow is mending the camera in The Bridge, 2 are asleep"). A tech who's carrying isn't also mending, and a tech who's mending isn't sent to carry. You can take a repair yourself with **I'll do it**: the engineers leave it to you, and it's done when you've walked up to the thing and fixed it by hand.

**The order of urgency.** When there's more to do than hands to do it, the ship chooses by a list, the most urgent first, which is the last part of the ship's file and is yours to reorder (on the Story board's Logistics page, the box **The order of urgency**, with **Up** and **Down** beside each line): carrying someone who's bleeding, a fire, a breach, carrying someone who's down, the alarm, urgent carrying, a repair the ship can't do without, treating someone, carrying the dead, carrying, a repair, mending in the workshop. The list does two things. Jobs are given out from the top. And **someone already at work leaves it only for a job two lines higher or more**: a tech mending a speaker drops it to fetch the generator's last cells, which is urgent carrying, five lines up, but not for a crate the pantry merely wants, one line up. What's dropped waits for the next free hand: a load is set down where it was, and a repair is still broken, with an hour's work for whoever takes it up. A repair the ship can't do without is one to whatever carries her power (the junction boxes) or to the air plant; it's marked `!!`, takes an engineer off a lesser repair when none is free, and gets one out of bed when nobody's up, while an ordinary repair at night waits for whoever's on watch, or for the morning. Nobody's woken while someone who's up could leave lesser work instead. The chief engineer's day is marked down for the minutes a repair the ship can't do without stood with nobody to send to it. This is the first part of the fifth step of the logistics rework, and the two-lines rule, what counts as a repair the ship can't do without, and which kinds wake people are a first guess.

**The medbay and the workshop are on the board too.** When someone goes down, who comes for them is the board's choice now, by the same list. The health file still says who ought to go, a nurse and then a spare hand, and the first of them who's free goes; but **a nurse who's carrying crates sets them down for someone who's fallen, and a nurse who's treating someone leaves the patient**, whose doses go back in the crate and who waits for the next medic. Before, the same nurse could be carrying, treating and answering a call all at once, on paper. Someone bleeding comes before someone who's only down: with nobody else to send, whoever had gone for the one is taken off for the other, and the first lies where they were left until someone's free. That's among the ship's own; a beaten mutineer is carried in their turn, after the crew, as before. The dead are carried too, after what the living need, and nobody's woken for them. A patient who's waiting takes a medic off an ordinary errand, which is two lines below treating, but not off urgent carrying; and a medic who's carrying or mending isn't at the medbay's table as well. In the workshop, a tech who's wanted for something clearly more urgent **sets the work down on the bench**, and whoever's free next takes it up where it was left, so six hours spent on an artificial leg aren't lost to one trip with fuel cells. The Work page lists all of it with the rest: "Carry Deckhand Kofi to the medbay, out cold: Nurse Haru is going to help Deckhand Kofi, since 08:36", "Treat Vik's broken right leg: Nurse Haru is seeing to it, 40 minutes left", "Build an artificial right leg for Mess Hand Bao, set down on the bench: nobody free", or why nobody can ("the medicine cabinet is empty", "the medbay's door is locked", "nobody free: Dr. Aduro is off watch"). These you can't take with I'll do it: carrying someone yourself, or treating them, is still done from the person. This is the second part of the fifth step, and that a nurse leaves a patient for a call, and that the bench keeps half-built work, are a first guess.

**Fires, breaches and the alarm.** These are the last kinds of work to join the board, and with them everything the crew is sent to do is on the one list. **An engineer is sent to a fire the moment it starts, and walks there**: before, "the engineers" were simply counted as being at it after three quarters of an hour, whatever else each of them was doing. The fire still can't be turned in under those three quarters of an hour, so with an engineer free it burns as it always did; what's new is that someone is really there, busy with it and nothing else until it's out, that a fire takes an engineer off a repair or out of bed, and that **with no engineer able to come nobody fights it** but you. A breach is the same: an engineer is sent, stands at it, and patches it two hours after the plate went, with a parts crate out of the stores; with no crate aboard they say so once and go back to their work, and someone's sent again when a crate comes aboard. The guards answering an alarm are on the list too, so a guard who was carrying sets the load down and carries nothing until the alarm stops. The Work page shows them at the top: "Fight the fire in the Galley, it's burning hard: Chief Engineer Marrow is fighting the fire in the Galley, since 10:00", "Patch the hull in the Boat Bay, the air's going out of it", "Answer the alarm in The Bridge: Lieutenant Tarrow is answering it". A fire or a breach with nobody to send counts against the chief engineer's day, like a repair the ship can't do without. Fighting a fire and patching a breach yourself are done in the room, as before. This is the third and last part of the fifth step of the logistics rework. One engineer to a fire is a first guess.

**Left waiting, and brought to you.** The board no longer leaves a job stuck in silence. A job is *waiting* when it's asking for a hand and nobody who does that work could come, even if they were woken, or when it's asking for goods and there are none aboard. After half an hour for urgent work, or three hours for the rest, whoever that part of the ship belongs to has it to ask you, with the amber **!** over them: see **Work the ship can't get done**, under Requests. Two lines at the top of the ship's file set the times and the posts (`left waiting: 30 minutes if it's urgent, 3 hours if it isn't` and `who asks: the chief medical officer for the medbay, ...`); they're in the numbers box of the Story board's Logistics page, and without them nobody is brought anything. What the officers say is in `world/scenes/request_work_hands.txt` and `request_work_goods.txt`, yours to rewrite. The pantry being short of produce isn't waiting for goods, since the garden is growing more. A job you took on this way has no **Leave it to the crew** on the Work page: you gave your word, and it goes back to the crew when its time is up. This is the sixth step of the logistics rework. The times, and which officer brings which part of the ship's work, are a first guess.

**The story and the board.** A scene, a conversation, a computer's program or a request can ask about the board and change it. `? work waiting` asks whether a job has been left waiting (add `hands` or `goods`, or a part of the ship: `medbay`, `engines`, `galley`, `repairs`); `? work first engines` asks what's been put first; `? work job sweep_bay done` asks how a job of the story's own stands (waiting, taken, done or none). `* work first engines: The reactor comes before everything` puts that part of the ship first, and those words are what the Work page says for why; it stands a day, and then the first officer looks again. `* work job sweep_bay in The Lighter Bay, 2 hours, by the deckhands: Sweep the Lighter Bay` puts a job of the story's own on the board: someone of those people walks there and does it, it's on the Work page like any other job, and done it leaves the fact `work_sweep_bay_done` on the record for a scene to wait on. While nobody's on it, you can do it yourself, in its room, under **You can** (**Sweep the Lighter Bay (2 hours)**). `* work job sweep_bay off: The reason` takes it off the board. `world/scenes/_how-to-write-a-scene.txt` has all of it, and `npm run check-story` names a room the ship hasn't got or people the board can't send.

**What things are worth to the ship.** Whatever is food, medicine or a spare counts for what it's worth, in the store that uses it. A ration crate or a basket of produce feeds 200 at the kettle and a tin feeds one; a medicine crate is 20 doses and a medkit four; a parts crate is one repair, and so is a hull repair kit. So what you find can be handed in. Put tins in the pantry and the cook uses them once the produce and the crates are gone, a pot at a time. Put medkits and bandages in the medicine cabinet and the doctor opens them when there's no crate left, the most doses first. Put a repair kit in the parts lockers and an engineer mends with it. Point at a thing in the inventory and it says what it's worth and where it has to be ("Feeds 1 at the kettle, once it's in the pantry."). The Ship menu's Food, Medicine and Parts count it all for its share of a crate, to a tenth, and the asks for food, medicine or parts take any of it: five medkits in the cabinet are one of the two crates Dr. Aduro asked for. For 900 people a tin is one meal of 1,800 a day, so found food only matters in bulk, while medicine and spares matter at once. Drink, sweets and tobacco feed nobody. This is the second step of the logistics rework.

**The generator and the power.** The generator in engineering burns a fuel cell every eight hours, taken from the fuel rack, which the deckhands keep up from the shuttle stores, and it gives the hundred power that everything aboard draws: the air plant, the doors, comms, navigation, the cameras, the lights room by room, and the computers. The same file says what each draws and the order they're let go in when there isn't enough: cameras first, then comms, lights, doors, navigation, and the air last. At rest the ship draws eighty, so there's some to spare. Under way the engines take their thirty, and for the jump the cameras and comms are let go. If the rack runs dry when the generator needs its next cell, it stops, and nothing aboard has power: every room goes dark, the air stops, the cameras see nothing, the galley stops cooking, and the ship can't jump, exactly as when a junction box is cut, until someone puts a cell in the rack. The Ship menu's **Power** section, the **Machines** lines, the reactor gauges' readouts in engineering and any computer's Power screen say what the generator gives against what's drawn and what's been let go, and a dead part says why. To try it: empty the fuel rack and the shuttle stores' cells and wait for the generator to stop; then bring a cell to the rack and watch the lights come back. With her stores full there are forty-two cells aboard, two weeks' worth, and more come only at a dock that sells fuel.

**Restocking at a dock.** Nothing comes aboard from nowhere any more. While the Meridian is docked at a port that sells them, every six hours, on the hour from midnight, the shuttle stores in the lighter bay are topped up: ration crates where the port sells food, fuel cells where it sells fuel. They're real crates and cells out of the port's own stores, at its price that day, and they're paid for out of the ship's purse. A port that's down to six sells six. In open space nothing comes. Each restocking is written in the manifest, which you read by looking at the shuttle stores (it's on the clip) or in the reactor gauges' logs in engineering, and the Ship menu's **Restocking** lines say the last one. Medicine and parts aren't restocked: those you buy by the lot, or find. The purse, what a day costs, and what happens when the money or the fuel runs out are under [The ship's purse, and living off the ports](#the-ships-purse-and-living-off-the-ports). A logistics file can still say *from nothing* for a store, which fills it from nowhere as every game used to; the Meridian's own doesn't.

**Water and reserve air.** The ship keeps her water in a tank in the Pump Room: 800 measures when it's full. Everyone aboard drinks from it, nine measures a day between the nine hundred of them; the galley takes a measure for every pot, the garden one for every basket it gives, and the air plant one every three hours, because it makes the decks' air out of water and power. That comes to about thirty measures a day, so a full tank lasts about twenty-five days. That's only what's lost for good: the **pumps** in the Pump Room bring the rest back, and while they're stopped (broken, switched off, or the whole ship without power) everything goes four times as fast. The pumps are a part of the ship like any other: you can look at them, switch them off and break them, the engineers mend them with a crate of parts, and now and then they wear out by themselves, like a junction box. The Ship menu's Stores list has a **WATER** line under the fuel cells, with how many days the tank will last as things stand, and the Work page lists the tank with the other stores.

At a dock that has water, the tank is kept up along with the shuttle stores, every six hours: tanks of water out of the port's own stock, a hundred measures each and a few credits each from the ship's purse, about a credit a day, and the line reads **holding**. In open space nothing comes. When the tank is under 200 measures and no dock is keeping it up, the quartermaster puts water on ration at their next look, and says so: everyone gets half, and so does the garden, which gives half as much. It comes off again once the tank is well over the mark, or at a dock that sells water. With none at all the galley can't cook and nothing grows (their **Machines** lines say the water tank has no water), and when the air plant has used the measure it had, **it stops making air**, exactly as if it had no power: the ship breathes her reserve, and after that, nothing (see [Without air](#bodies)). You're told once when the water is down to about five days, and again when the tank is empty.

**The reserve tanks** in Life Support are her reserve air: 120 hours of it, the five days she already had, now somewhere you can walk to, with an **AIR** line on the Ship menu under the water. It's breathed while the air plant is stopped, for whatever reason. Once the plant runs it fills again within a day, which takes water: twenty measures for all five days. And when a breach is patched, **the room is filled again out of the reserve tanks at once**, six hours of their air. If they haven't that much left, the air plant fills the room by itself over about three hours, and the room's air stays bad until it has.

Two things can be done by hand. A **tank of water** you've bought or found, carried to the water tank, can be poured in (**Pour in a tank of water**, in the tank's window or its right-click menu). A **reserve tank of air**, which the yards and some ports sell, can be fitted at the reserve tanks (**Fit a full reserve tank**): a day of air. And the answer to a distress call brings a couple of days of water with the fuel, if she's nearly out.

To try it: break the pumps in the Pump Room, take the parts crates out of the parts lockers and the shuttle stores so nobody can mend them, and watch the WATER line's days fall to a quarter. Or lie in open space until the tank is under 200, and wait for Brandt to ration it. The **Test save** keeps its tank full, like its fuel.

It's all written in the ship's own files. `world/logistics/meridian.txt` has the two stores, the air plant's line, *and 1 water* at the end of the galley's and the garden's, the water tank's line under Refills, and eight lines at the top (what everyone drinks, what a tank of water fills, how much faster it goes with the pumps stopped, when it's rationed, what a reserve tank gives, what a day of reserve air takes, and the two for a breached room), which the Story board's Logistics page has as boxes under **Water and reserve air**. The pumps are in `world/systems/meridian.txt`, under **Water**. A game from before this is told once how it goes now, and finds its tank full. This is the eleventh step of the logistics rework, and every number in it is a first guess: how fast the tank falls is for you to set by playing.

**Comforts.** The crew uses up three comforts, a little every day: **drink**, **tea and tobacco**, and **clothes**. They're kept in the **comforts locker** in the Long Mess, a store like the pantry that you can walk up to and open: kegs of beer and spirits, chests of tea and tobacco, bales of clothes, and the bottles the crew has brewed for itself. The nine hundred aboard get through 20 of drink, 20 of tea and tobacco and 10 of clothes in a day, where a keg is worth 80 of drink, a chest 100 of tea and tobacco and a bale 100 of clothes: a keg lasts them four days, a chest five, a bale ten. She starts with about three weeks of each. Anything else in the locker that's worth some counts too, the way a tin counts in the pantry: a tea brick, a bottle of Korrun stone ale or a spare jumpsuit, put in by hand. The crew opens the smallest thing first, so the loose bottles go before a keg is broached. Point at a thing in your inventory and it says what it's worth (**3 of the crew's drink, once it's in the comforts locker**).

What they do is in two rules. **While things are going wrong** (short rations, something run out, a kettle that ran dry: anything that has morale settling lower than it does on a fed ship at ease), every comfort the crew still has steadies it by 2, so a crew with all three settles 6 higher than it otherwise would, never past where it settles when all's well. And **every comfort they've run out of sours it by 2**, however things are going: with all three gone, morale settles 6 lower, and gets there a point a day. You're told once when one runs out, the **tea urn** in the Long Mess runs dry with the tea (**Have a cup** greys out and says there's no tea aboard), and people near you say so under their breath.

The Ship menu's Stores list has a **COMFORTS** block under the air: a line each for drink, tea and clothes, with how many days are left, in red for one that's run out, and a sentence on what they're doing to morale today. At a dock whose port sells them (Haven sells all of them) it has a button for each thing the locker keeps, **Keg of beer and spirits, 26 cr** and so on. One click buys one, out of the port's own stock and the ship's purse, straight into the locker, and never on the slate. Keeping all three up costs about 12 credits a day at Haven's prices, which is more than the levy leaves over once the ship has paid for her day: comforts are paid for by freight, or done without.

**The beer bucket and the still.** Two small makers turn a basket of the garden's picking into drink. The **beer bucket** in the Galley brews 48 bottles of the ship's beer about once a month, while a cook's there; the **still** in the Crawlways gives 24 bottles of moonshine every three weeks or so, while someone's down there. Their times are hours of work, so they only count while someone's with them, and each batch needs room in the locker, which there is once the last one's been drunk. Together that's about a fifth of what the crew drinks, for about a hundredth of what the ship eats. Both are on the Ship menu's **Machines** list. The ship's law looks away from the still: `world/law/meridian.txt` says `the still: the law looks away`, and changing that line to `the still: against the law` shuts it, so nobody runs it.

**A good table.** The locker also keeps three **hampers of provisions**: bread, honey, sausage. After a hard day (a death aboard, fighting on the decks, a fire or a hole in the hull, or the alarm), if spirits are low, the quartermaster has them laid out at the first sitting of the evening, a hamper for every three hundred aboard, and morale lifts at once: by 6 with enough for everyone, by less when the hampers only reach some. Spirits are low while morale is under where a fed ship at ease settles, a table never lifts it past that, and there's one in three days at most. The story says so (**Quartermaster Brandt had a good table laid in The Long Mess this evening, after a death aboard**), and for that evening people talk about the bread. Hampers are bought at a dock like the kegs. With nobody able to act as quartermaster, no table is laid.

**Gifts.** Some things are worth giving: a bottle of Vantor red, a case of fine things, cigars, a pocket watch, down to a bottle of the ship's beer. With one in your pack aboard the Meridian, right-click someone, or open their window, and choose **Give a vantor red wine**: the best three you carry are offered. They remember it, an officer's card in the **Command** menu says so (**Day 5: given a vantor red wine by you**), and it counts in how they feel about you, and so in an officer's loyalty: 8 for a case of fine things, 6 for Vantor red, 1 for a beer. Someone remembers the last two gifts they were given, one a week counts with any one person, and like everything people remember it fades. Nobody asleep, laid up or lying where they fell can be given anything, and what you're wearing isn't offered.

**The Gilded Wheel pays for what it can't get.** When the Wheel is short of fine things, Madame Oriel asks anyone who comes through for two bottles of Vantor red, and pays twice what they cost on the market (see [Requests](#requests)). And like any port it pays more for a load of whatever it's short of: the **Market** page's list of where things are cheap and dear says where.

To try it: dock at Haven, open the Ship menu and buy a chest of tea for the crew. Or walk up to the comforts locker in the Long Mess and take the chests out (as captain they're yours to take), then use **Wait 3 days** on the Ship menu, twice, so the crew finishes the chest that's already open, and see the TEA line go red, the tea urn run dry and the talk in the mess change. Put a chest back and within the hour they have their tea again.

It's all written in files you can change. `world/logistics/meridian.txt` has the locker with what it keeps, four lines at the top (what the crew uses in a day and from which store, what each comfort steadies, what going without one sours, what a good table lifts), which the Story board's Logistics page has as boxes under **The crew's comforts**, and the two makers under Machines. What a thing is worth is in its own file in `world/items`: `comfort: 80 drink`, `comfort: 3 clothes`, `comfort: a good table for 300`, and `gift: 6` for how well it's remembered. A game from before this is told once how it goes now, and finds its locker full. Not built: whoever else has the chair doesn't buy comforts for the crew, and nobody goes thirsty for want of a drink. This is the twelfth step of the logistics rework, and every number in it is a first guess of mine: which things count, for how much, and what a gift is worth are yours to change.

**The repairs.** When something aboard has been damaged (a junction box's cables cut, an injector pulled out of the drive, a door forced, anything broken), an engineer fixes it within two hours, and the repair uses up a crate of parts. In the last hour you'll see the engineer walk to the parts lockers, or to the shuttle stores if the lockers are empty, their line saying they're fetching a crate of parts, and then to what they're fixing; the story says when it's done and where the crate came from. With no crate of parts aboard, the repair waits: the story says so once, the part's look says Marrow can't fix it until there's a crate in the stores, and the Ship menu's **Repairs** lines list what's waiting. Within the hour after a crate comes aboard, it's fixed. A rag over a camera's lens, a seal on a vent, a jammed intake or a scrambled alignment are put right with nothing, and so is anything you fix yourself. The line *a repair takes* at the top of the logistics file says all of this, and you can change it. A crate can also be made from salvage at the bench in the Repair Bay (see [Your inventory](#your-inventory)). To try it: cut the cables of the junction box in engineering, and watch Marrow go for a crate.

**The chain of command.** The **Command** menu, under The ship, shows every post aboard as a card: who holds it, where they're usually found, who chose them, their numbers in the skills that count for the post, the jobs they head, and since when. You're the captain at the top if you started as one, and otherwise whoever has the chair is. Under the captain come Commander Voss, the first officer, who keeps the watch bill, and the heads of the ship's departments: Quartermaster Brandt, Chief Engineer Marrow, Lieutenant Tarrow on security, Dr. Aduro and Deck Chief Hask. Under Voss come the bridge's comms, helm and sensor officers, and Speaker Halm is chosen by the civilians. Click the card of one of the story's people to read their registry printout over the room. Everything that used to name someone for their post now asks who holds it: the officers' council's rations decisions name the quartermaster and the speaker, the alarm brings whoever's chief of security, the chief engineer fixes what's broken, the quartermaster carries the ration crates, and the captain's chair is the captain's post. Posts change hands at the officers' reviews, and a mutiny can take the ship, both below. The posts, who chooses each, the jobs, who holds what and who does which job when a game begins are in `world/command/meridian.txt`, and a game keeps its own from the start, so a change there reaches new games.

**The watch bill.** Under the cards, the Command menu has the watch bill, kept by Commander Voss: every job aboard (cooks, mess hands, gardeners, techs, nurses, deckhands, ensigns and guards), the room it's done in, how many it wants, who's doing it, and how many it's short, with the spare hands at the bottom, six civilians who can be put to work. Everyone on it lives their job's day: put a cook in the garden and she walks to the garden and tends the beds, at her own shift. What's done follows: the galley only cooks with someone whose job is cook in it, carrying goes to whoever does the jobs a store names and is free, the techs are the engineers who fix what's broken, and the guards come with the chief of security when the alarm rings. As captain you move people with two clicks: click someone, and **Move here** comes up on every other job; click the one they're going to. If someone's lost from their job, beaten and being patched up, or dead, Voss puts a spare hand on in their place and says so, and sends the spare hand back once they're back. The guards start with nobody, so they're two short until someone's put there. If you started as crew aboard, the engine hand, the orderly or the marine, you're on the watch bill too, among the techs, the nurses or the guards, and if you're ever moved, the shift you work moves with you. To try it: take both cooks off the galley before dinner, and watch the sitting go hungry; then put them back.

**How well they do.** Each post's card has a duty bar for whoever holds it: the average of their marks over the last week, each a day's mark out of 100, worked out at midnight from what the game already counts, with the last day's marks under it. The quartermaster is marked on sittings missed and stores run dry, the chief engineer on power short, repairs waiting and how worn the hull is, the chief of security on alarms, tampering seen and thefts, the doctor on medicine running out and deaths aboard, the deck chief on urgent carrying left with nobody to do it, the first officer on how much of the watch bill is staffed, and the captain on morale, shortages and deaths aboard. Someone new in a post starts with a clean record. Skill shows in the work too: each point of the best worker's skill above 5 makes the work 8% quicker, and each point below that much slower. Marrow's engineering 8 takes a two-hour repair down to about an hour and forty minutes, and two spare hands put in the galley cook about a quarter slower than the cooks. Someone without a file of their own is a 5 at their own job and a 2 at anything else. A bad record can cost someone their post, below. To try it: take both cooks off the galley, wait two days, and watch Brandt's bar fall.

**Rising and falling.** Every seven days the officers review their people. Someone whose duty has been under 60 for five days of the last week is warned straight away by whoever chooses for their post, and relieved at the next review if it's gone on: the best of those the post is picked from takes it, and the one relieved takes the job the new holder left. You hear it in the story: "Commander Voss has relieved Quartermaster Brandt after six days of empty kettles, and given the post to Gardener Benno. Brandt is a gardener now, and takes it quietly." The new holder lives the post's day, sleeping in its cabin and working at its station, and the ship's own lines name them ("Food is down to about five days. Quartermaster Benno has started checking the count twice."). If whoever took over has the same trouble, Voss decides it isn't the post's doing, says so, and stops relieving people over it until it's over. As captain, the reviews come to you as Voss's recommendations, under **Waiting on you** at the top of the Command menu: **Do it**, **Keep** whoever holds the post, or **Leave it to Voss**. If you don't answer by the next review, she acts in your name. **Replace...** under a card gives a post you or Voss choose for to anyone it's picked from, in two clicks, and **Hold the reviews** stops anyone being warned, relieved or given a post until you let them go on. Someone you choose or keep on isn't reviewed for ten days. If you're crew, whoever has the chair decides without asking you. People remember being relieved, warned, passed over, told no, given a post or given one back, and by whom: it shows on their card, the watch bill says who was relieved, and for about a month it's the first thing they say when you talk to them. The ambitious push: someone with two points more skill for a post than whoever holds it, and the ambition for it, asks for the post, and one who's told no and has the temper works against their boss, taking ten off each of their days. Each officer's file has their ambition, temper and loyalty to the captain to begin with, and everyone else has the command file's. To try it: take both cooks off the galley and wait a week, and Voss warns Brandt and then recommends relieving her. Or relieve someone yourself with **Replace...**, then go and talk to them.

**Loyalty and mutiny.** Each officer's card has a loyalty bar: how loyal they are to the captain, from their file's own number, what they remember of the captain, and what's moved it lately, with the reasons under the bar ("Down for the rations, going hungry"). Half or reduced rations pull it down a little every day and generous ones lift it, a day of empty kettles pulls it down, morale under 30 pulls it down, a jump somewhere risky costs it, and so do the sides you take in the world, as far as each officer's beliefs disagree with the faction's. Each settles back once whatever moved it stops. The council has a confidence of its own, which follows morale, less for poor rations, empty kettles and shortages, and it's the speaker's duty bar. The top of the Command menu shows the officers' loyalty on average, the council's confidence, and how things stand aboard. When morale is under 30 and the officers' loyalty under 35, a mutiny brews, a day at a time. After a day there's grumbling: people at the benches are "grumbling about the captain", and the discontented say so when you talk to them. After four days, orders go astray: the council won't put a cut in the rations through for you, and a first officer who's lost faith won't move anyone on the watch bill. After seven, the officers come to you together, in a written scene that plays when you carry on the story. You can give them what they ask, talk them round, have the chief of security arrest whoever's leading them while security's still loyal, step down, or throw them out yourself, which is a fight like any other, with talking, threatening, paying and lying in it. Send them away, or leave them waiting, and they take the ship: whoever led them has the chair, you spend three days in the brig, and then you're crew, one of the spare hands, with shifts to work. If someone else has the chair and you're aboard, whoever's leading it asks you in, and you can join, stay out, or warn the captain; and if the officers once took the ship from you, they may offer it back when it's the new captain's turn. Whoever's arrested spends a week in the brig and comes out a spare hand. To try it: hold the council on the Ship menu, put the ship on half rations, take the cooks off the galley, relieve Voss, and wait about three weeks. If things ease while the officers are still waiting for your answer, they wait on with whoever speaks for them, so every answer still does what it says. When the ship changes hands or someone's arrested, the story says that first, and who was given the post they left after it.

**The story knows.** A written scene can ask who holds a post (`? holds quartermaster odessa`), what someone does on the watch bill (`? job Yusuf cook`), how loyal someone is (`? loyalty marrow < 40`) and how far a mutiny has got (`? mutiny brewing`), and it can change things, each with a colon and the reason: `* appoint quartermaster tobin`, `* relieve chief_engineer`, `* assign Yusuf cook`, `* loyalty marrow -10`, and `* remember marrow tarrow -15` for a grudge one person holds against another. A scene can name whoever holds a post in curly brackets, like {quartermaster}, and the game fills in their name. When a post changes hands, someone's put in the brig, or a mutiny ends one way or another, it goes on the record, under Facts on record in the Quests menu, so later stories can react to it. The Story board has a **Command** page: each post as a box with who holds it when a game begins and an arrow from whoever chooses it, each job with who does it and an arrow from the post that heads it, and the spare hands, all changed with pickers and saved into `world/command/meridian.txt`. To try it: talk to Chief Hask and ask who keeps the stores, then give the quartermaster's post to someone else with **Replace...** and ask him again.

**The hull.** With parts aboard the engineers mend it a little every day, up to its best. Without them it wears. The story can take hull too.

**Morale and health.** Each settles toward a level set by your rations, and the Ship menu shows you where. Low health kills people. Morale is a line the story hears.

**Your decisions.** All of these are on the Ship menu, and each shows its real cost and benefit before you choose:

- **Rations:** Generous, Standard, Reduced or Half. Less food used, against lower morale and health.
- **Wait** a day or three.
- **Buy and sell** supplies for the ship with your own credits, at docks that deal in them: food by the five crates, medicine and parts by the crate, fuel by the unit. What you buy goes into the stores that keep it (food into the shuttle stores, then the pantry), so there has to be room, and what you sell comes out of them. What they cost changes from day to day: see [The wider world](#the-wider-world).

**The officers and the council.** If the food's run low and stayed low, they don't wait to be told. Quartermaster Brandt cuts rations when the count gets low, Speaker Halm puts everyone on half rations when it's nearly gone, Halm talks the council into generous rations for a while when morale's very low and the stores can stand it, and Brandt puts them back once people are happier, or once the stores can carry standard rations again. Each decision comes up in the story and in the **Officers and council** section of the Ship menu.

They give you a day or two first, they make one decision a day, and an order of your own stands for a few days before they'd change it. You can overrule anything they do, and they put back only what they changed themselves. If someone's been lost, the council does it instead. If you'd rather they didn't do any of this, press **Wait for my word**, and they won't, whatever happens.

**The star map.** Click any star to see the fuel and time it takes to get there, how dangerous it is, who controls it, and what it sells to the ship, then press **Set course**. A jump moves the Meridian and takes 1 to 3 fuel, depending on how far it is. The dock she's at is a short shuttle ride away, and so is she from it. The Sundial, the farm ship, and Anselm's Mercy, the hospital ship, are places of their own on the chart now, a jump away like anywhere else. The story line says where you've gone, and if there's a written arrival there, or a scene waiting for the Meridian to get there, **Continue the story** plays it when you're ready.

**The chart on the bridge.** The star map is also a program on the bridge: **Navplot**, on the plot table's computer, NAVCOM 7. Sit down at the plot table (it's the captain's own, so you're straight in), click the **Charts** icon on its desktop, and click it again to open it. A loading screen plays for about three seconds, and a click skips it. Then the chart fills the computer's whole screen, under the red RESTRICTED bar: every place you know of, the Meridian, and a ring round her for how far each fuel reaches. Click a place to read about it on the right (how dangerous it is, who holds it, who has a say, what it has, what it sells the ship), press **Plot course** to draw the course to it, and **Engage jump...** to be asked once before she goes. **Ports** is the same places as a list, **News** is the news from around, **Everything it holds...** lists a port's stores, and the menus along the top (Chart, Course, View) do the same things and can put the rings and the names away. The row along the bottom gives her fuel, where she is, whether the navigation core is up (with it down every jump is plotted by hand, a fuel more and half as long again) and the time. The × at the top right, or Esc, closes the program and leaves the desktop as it was; what you'd picked is still there when you open it again. It goes by the same places, costs and rules as the **Star map** menu, which is still there for now, and a captain's jump from the chart puts you ashore where she arrives, as **Set course** does. **The plot table is the captain's alone.** Anyone else who sits down at it meets its logon, and has to get past it like any computer that isn't theirs. Whoever does get in can read the chart and order a jump too: she goes with them aboard, her log says the order wasn't the chair's, and SENTRY, the computer's watcher, hears it.

**What's left of the fleet.** The stories, the places and the people were written when the Meridian led a fleet of forty-one, and most of them still speak of it. The game reads a story's `fleet:` lines as the ship's own (morale, food, medicine, parts and fuel) and lets the rest go, and `npm run check-story` ends with a list of everything that still speaks of the fleet, to rework in time. Eleven stories that stand on a ship of the fleet are set aside in `world/stories/_set-aside.txt`: they stay in their files, and out of the game, until their line is taken out of that list.

## Walking around

**Story view** under **Reading** in the Settings menu switches between **Isometric**, where you see the room you're in from an angle, **Walk-around**, which is the same thing in console art, and **Classic**, where the story has the whole window. Isometric is the default. Isometric and Walk-around work everywhere in the world, and fights, skill checks and menus are the same in all of them.

**Top-down** is a test, there to judge how the game would look seen from above in ready-made pictures, the way most art packs for sale are drawn. It's Walk-around with the room drawn in a free tileset instead of characters: walls with their faces and consoles, floors, doors, the space outside the hull, and the game's own light over them, so a room without power goes dim and screens glow. **Top-down art**, under it in Settings, picks the tileset: **Tech Lab**, dark metal and teal floors, or **Sci-fi Interior**, bright and cartoonish. Both are public domain, from OpenGameArt.org, by ansimuz and by Buch, and neither has furniture, so the furniture is drawn in simple shapes in the tileset's colours. People, things and ways out are Walk-around's own marks over the picture, and walking, clicking and the right-click menu work as they do there; fights open in their own window, and the story stays in its panel under the room. Nothing else in the game changes when you pick it, and Isometric is still the default.

**Pixel art** is another test: the isometric view with its floors and walls drawn in a bought pixel-art pack, **Isometric Space Station** by @pixel_Salvaje, in place of the game's own drawing. **Pixel art look**, under it in Settings, picks the pack's **Dark** or **Light** design. The room is shown a little bigger than in Isometric, because the pack draws a floor tile 128 pixels wide and the room is drawn at exactly half that, so its pixels stay even; zooming in past that makes them a little soft. The pack's walls are a little shorter than the game's, so they're stretched up to the game's height, and the game's thick walls have their tops in the pack's colour. The packs' furniture is drawn at its own size, which is a little smaller than the game's own, and in this view people are drawn as much smaller beside it, so they still fit its chairs. The doors between rooms are the pack's too: they slide open for whoever comes near, with a green light, and a locked one shows a red light. Most of the furniture is drawn from a second pack by the same artist, **Isometric Interiors**: chairs and the captain's chair, each seen from whichever of its four sides is turned to you (someone sitting with their back to you is drawn behind the chair's back), the mess's steel tables, benches, steel lockers, wire shelving, screens on their stands, a stove with its pot for the soup kettle, plants and washbasins. The first pack's own pieces are the plot table, desks with their terminals, barrels and crates, and they stand in for lockers if the second pack isn't in its folder. All of it is drawn a little bigger than the packs draw it, to keep to the walls and to the game's people, and a piece the packs draw from one side only is the same picture turned round when it faces the other way. Any other piece of the two packs can stand on a deck as well, put there with the Map editor's **From the packs** ([Drawing the ship yourself](#drawing-the-ship-yourself)), and is drawn from its own picture: on the floor, on a table if it's a small thing, flat under your feet if it's a carpet, or on the face of a wall, turned to lean the way that wall runs. A picture is fetched the first time a deck needs it, so a piece just put down can show as a plain shape for a moment. Everything else is the game's own: the windows and screens on the walls (they show the ship's power), the people, the light and darkness, fire and sparks, and every click (what was the game's until step 5 of the map's rework is in "Beds, consoles, low walls, stairs and fittings" below). The packs are only on this computer, in the folders `licensed/isospace` and `licensed/interiors`, because their licence lets the game use and change the pictures but not give the files away; they're never put on GitHub, and an installer made for others doesn't have them. Without the first, Pixel art draws the room as Isometric does, and Settings says the pack wasn't found; without the second, the furniture it would have drawn is the game's own, and Settings says so.

**The bridge is the first room redone for the packs**, the first of the ship's rooms to be reworked. It's the same either side of the ship's middle line and faces the bow, which is straight now where it used to come to a point. The helm's four consoles sit in the nose, at a window the whole width of it. Behind them, on the middle line, are the plot table and the two command seats, yours and the first officer's. Either side, behind a low wall, is a wing of three consoles facing the screens ahead of them: comms to starboard, the sensor pit to port. Aft, either side of the way in, are the ready room and the captain's sea cabin. In Pixel art the consoles, seats and machines are the packs' own pictures; in Isometric they're plain shapes in the game's colours, so the bridge looks its best in Pixel art. A game saved before a deck was redrawn opens with everyone settled where their day has them on the new deck.

**Light rooms, steel, lamps and worn floors.** The medbay and the galley are drawn in the packs' Light look, toned down so they sit beside the dark passages. Furniture the packs draw in wood or orange is drawn in the ship's blue-grey steel where a piece says so: the tables, desks, shelves and cabinets, and the game's own benches and washbasins. In the Map editor's **Pieces** window, a piece's **+** has **Its wood and orange: As drawn / In steel**, to change that for any piece. Warm lamps make small pools of light at the bridge's stations, on the ready room's desk and by the captain's cot, in rooms whose own light is cold. The bridge's middle has a floor of its own, a mesh, and on deck 1 the Map editor has a second floor paint for laying more of it. And in the Isometric and Pixel art views the floor is a little paler where people walk, from the doors to the seats, beds and things.

**Beds, consoles, low walls, stairs and fittings.** In the Pixel art view these are the packs' too. A row of consoles drawn on a deck's plan is the pack's control console to each two squares, with a terminal on an odd one, dark where the room has no power; in the galley the same rows are steel kitchen counters with a stove for every third (a room's `banks` line in `world/looks.json` says so). A wall cut down so you can see into a room is the pack's low wall with its posts, on a dark sill. The lamps on the walls are the pack's strip lights, and go dark with their room. Bunks are the pack's, two end to end, and the sickbay's cots are the second pack's plain bed with its frame in steel; whoever's asleep or laid up is drawn into them, a head on the pillow and the rest under the bedding. A bed of the packs put down with the Map editor is a real bed as well: it isn't in anyone's way, someone whose day sends them to sleep on it lies in it (two in the pack's bunk or the wide bed, and seen through the lid of a medical pod), and someone awake there sits on its edge. The stairs stand where each stair room has its row of posts: two flights of the pack's steel steps with their rails, up to a landing, for a way up; a well in the deck with steps going down into it and hazard paint along its edge for a way down; one of each in a room with both. The posts still keep people off those squares, and the striped plate you click to change deck is where it was. On the walls, the cameras, light switches (green on, red off, dark with no power), vents, junction boxes and door keypads are the second pack's fittings, with the game's own marks over them for what the pack can't show: a camera's blinking light, the cloth over a covered one, the tape over a sealed vent, a cut box's red slash, a keypad's green or red. Pipes and blue cables run along some stretches of bare wall, the same ones every time, and a floor plate here and there is a shade off the rest and bolted down, as if it had been replaced. The engines are banks of the pack's tanks with lit cores, pump units down their sides, a rail round each and hazard paint at their foot; the ship's boat is a dark hull with a well in it, a pilot's console and two seats, and paint on the deck round it; a heap of scrap is the pack's drums and cases. Anything tall and narrow on the plan that isn't a stair (the Map editor's **Locker**) is the packs' locker. The alarms are small domes on the walls that glow red while they ring; the comms relay is a rack of lights, the navigation core a glass-fronted cabinet, the air plant a standing unit with its duct and the engines' own part a pump unit, each standing at the foot of its wall with one light over it (its own colour while it works, amber when it's jammed, dark red when it's dead); and a camera that works turns on its bracket. A thing that's none of the game's furniture is drawn by what its name says it is: the reactor a lit tank, a chest a box, the scan bed a medical pod, the tea urn a kettle on a table, a rota a sheet of paper, and anything else a small case; what's somewhere to look and not an object (the forward viewport, the chalked names) shows only its mark. Still the game's own in this view: wall consoles and docking clamps, which only the ports have, the garden's beds, the hull outside, and the people. The Isometric view is as it was.

**Deck 1, laid out again.** The ten rooms aft of the bridge are furnished from the packs, each from what its own description says it's for, and are yours to correct in the Map editor. The doors off the Names Bulkhead are a full tile wide. The Chart Room has its long table in the middle, its files behind a low wall and two desks; Comms & Sensors has racks along the hull and two rows of stations; each officer's cabin has a bed, a desk and that officer's own things; there are benches and paper lamps under the names; the Wardroom has one long table, a sideboard, a pantry behind a low wall and easy chairs; the Quiet Room has cushions in rows and nothing that hums; the Heads have basins under mirrors, showers and stalls; the two stairs have their way down at the head of the flight; and the Boat Bay has suit lockers, a bench of tools and pumps by the launch. In Isometric the packs' pieces show as plain shapes, so these rooms look their best in Pixel art.

**Deck 2, laid out again.** The civilian deck's thirteen rooms are furnished from the packs the same way, and are yours to correct in the Map editor. The bow is straight now, like the bridge's above it: the medbay ends in a short nose with windows and two easy chairs. The Medbay is in three parts between low walls: a waiting room under WAIT YOUR TURN with benches for the queue, a hall with the watch's consoles, and the ward, where the four cots stand head to the hull, the curtained one between two screens; the Surgery has the scan bed with the records terminal beside it and the medicine cabinet on its wall, and the Lab has its benches and tanks. The Long Mess has a serving line along the hull with the kettle in it, six long tables with benches, the far table and the wobbling one; the Galley has its range down the middle with the cooks behind it and the pantry on the wall; the Garden keeps its beds, with a potting bench, water and pots along the hull; the Armory has a cage behind a low wall with its own door; the Brig has the two cells its description always said; the Hot Bunks have their racks in two rows and, at the far end, the three cabins with doors; the Overflow has its cots in rows, each with something of its family's beside it, a table for lessons and a wall of photographs; and the stairs' ways up and down are at the ends of their flights. **Deck 3, laid out again.** The engineering deck's ten rooms are furnished from the packs the same way, which makes the whole ship redrawn. The bow is straight here too: the Crawlways end in a nose, and their ducts stand in a plain grid of blocks with lanes between them, with what the people living down there have made of it: a camp by the warm bedrolls, a stove, a stash, paper lamps, and grilles and pipes on the ducts. The stair's way up is at the foot of its flight. The parts lockers, the security desk and the reactor safety controller are drawn on the deck now, where they belong, so a game saved before this finds the parts lockers full once.

**People stay the game's own.** The packs have no people, so the game's own drawn figures walk the new rooms. At step 9 of the map's rework a pixel style for them was built to compare with the figures as they are (the same figures with flat colours and a dark line round each), with a switch in Settings between the two, and the answer was to keep them as they're drawn. So that's how people look in every view, and the switch is gone again.

The game's automatic tests keep their own copy of the ship as she was before this (`test/kept-ship`), so that redrawing a deck, yours or mine, can't break them: they're run with `npm test`. One file of them, `test/ship-as-drawn.test.js`, looks at the real decks instead, and checks what must hold however they're drawn: every room reached, everyone's places on the deck, everything that opens within reach, the opening battle fought to an end.

In Isometric and Walk-around, the main window shows the room you're in, with you standing in it. Every part of every place is its own level, with its own shape, colors and things in it. The Meridian's Bridge has its viewport and sensor pit, the bar at Haven has its weapons rack and its stain, the Long Furnaces at Korrath roar along both walls, the Gilded Wheel is one enormous wheel, and the 18:40 tram is still waiting on Founders Walk in Asterion. You arrive at each place by its way in, such as its docks. In Isometric the story comes up over the room: see [The story over the room](#the-story-over-the-room). In Walk-around it stays in a panel under the room, showing the latest scene, and **Show the whole story** opens all of it. Typing an action works the same way in all of them.

**What's here** floats over the right side of the room, in a pane with corners like the speech panes. Its tag has the room's name and deck. Under it are **About this room**, which opens a few words about the room, the button for the place's plan, the **People** here with what each is doing, the room's **Systems** as small tags (teal when they work, amber when they're off or broken, red when an alarm's ringing), anything wrong with the room, and the **Ways out**, with **You can** under them when you have work or a bunk. The things in the room aren't listed: they're the `?` marks in it. Click the tag to fold the pane down to one line, such as `4 people · 7 systems · 1 way out`, and again to open it, and it stays the way you left it. While it's open, the room keeps you clear of it, and the story's panes over the room keep to its left. On a phone-sized window it sits under the room instead.

- **To walk,** hold **W**, **A**, **S** or **D**, or the arrow keys, or click the floor. You walk freely, not a square at a time: walls stop you, you slide along them, and walking straight at a doorway eases you into it. A click takes the shortest way to the very spot you clicked, and pressing a key on the way takes over from it. Walking into a way out that's open takes you through.
- **The keys walk.** A number key picks one of the story's choices, and **Space** pauses the clock. In Walk-around, the typing line under the room only has the keyboard when you click it, or press Enter, and **Esc** gives the keys back to walking.
- **To go to another room,** click a way out, such as `v The Lower Forward Stair`, on the level or under **Ways out** in **What's here**. Or press the plan button at the top of **What's here**, which has its own name in each place, such as **Deck plan**, **Station plan** or **Colony map**. It shows the whole place and who's in each room, and you click where you want to go. On the Meridian each deck is one continuous level with its rooms drawn on it, so you walk from room to room through their doorways, the room's name and who's in it changing as you cross, and only the stairs between decks are ways out; the deck plan has a tab to a deck, and in Walk-around the deck scrolls to keep you in sight.
- **Some ways in are closed,** and they show an `x` instead of an arrow: a guard, a locked door, a picket line. Click one to walk up to it and read why. **Ask to go through** follows that door's own rule: some open when asked, some need something you've earned, and some open a skill check whose result decides. If the story takes you through, that door stays open from then on, even after you've left and come back. Whatever's behind a closed door is closed too, on the map as well. There are 15 of these in the world, and none on the Meridian.
- **To talk to someone,** click them, on the level or under **People** in **What's here**. You walk up to them, and a talk window opens.
- **To look at something,** click its `?` on the level. You walk up to it, and a window comes up beside it on a line, in the game's console look: its name and state in the bar and what it looks like under it, with an **Actions** window glued to its side holding what you can do. Its colour says what it is: gold for things, teal for the place's parts, amber for a part that's off or dead or a door that's shut, and red for a locked door or a ringing alarm. Nothing behind it is dimmed, and the room goes on around you. It keeps clear of the room's panel on the right, hanging its **Actions** under it when there's no room beside it. Pull the window aside with the mouse to see what's under it, and it slides back when you let go. Walking anywhere, or **Close**, or Escape, puts it away. **Use** gives the thing's own answer in the window, or picks it up, and anything that hasn't been written yet says so plainly. A closed or locked door answers in the same kind of window.
- **Right-click** anyone, anything, a door or the floor, on the level or in **What's here**, and a menu slides open where you clicked, in the same console look and in the colour of what it's for, or the person's own colour. Click a choice or press its number. A click anywhere else, Escape, or walking puts it away. What needs you up close walks you over first, and **Look** looks from where you stand.
  - **Someone:** **Talk**, **Look**, **Registry**, **Pickpocket** and **Attack**. **Look** opens their window beside them, with how they look, how they walk if it isn't normally and the worst of the marks on their body, and what they're doing, and the same choices in its **Actions**. **Registry** feeds their record out of the printer in the map's corner, the same amber printout as your own (your name at the top left, or C): who they are, their post, attributes, skills, how they'd fight, how hard their pocket is, the chart of their body, and what they're doing. Someone with a file prints what it says, never their secrets. Anyone else gets a thin record made up from their name, the same every time, with their job taken from their name, and the fight and pocket it shows are the ones the rules really use. **Your own record** on it prints yours, and **Registry** on them again, **Tear off**, **C** or **Esc** puts it away.
  - **Something:** **Look** and **Use**. A part of the place's systems has **Look** and what can be done to it, such as **Pull the lever down** or **Cut the cables**. Something that holds things, or a pile on the floor, has **Look** and **Open**, which opens it beside your pack in the inventory.
  - **A door:** **Go through**. A closed one has **Look** and **Ask to go through**, and a locked one **Look**, **Force the door** and **Hack the door panel**.
  - **The floor:** **Walk here**.
- **Picking a pocket** is a Stealth check in the check window. It's as hard as the person's Awareness in their file, so Commander Voss, with 4, is hard. Someone without a file is easy, or moderate if their name says they watch for a living, like a guard or a hunter, and anyone asleep is a step easier. A clean lift pays a few credits, and nobody knows. If they catch your hand, they think less of you, whoever holds the place takes 2 off your standing (on the Meridian, as her captain, it's 2 off morale aboard instead), it goes on the record for the stories, and a camera on the room rings the alarm. Either way, they keep a hand on their pocket for a day.
- **Attacking someone** asks first, then walks you over and starts a fight: in the isometric view it's played in the room, with them where they stood, and in the other views in the fight window. Someone with a file fights the way their file says. Anyone else fights as a Dock Thug under their own name, or as a Customs Marine if they're a guard. It costs 5 standing with whoever holds the place (5 morale on your own ship), turns anyone with a file against you, goes on the record, and a camera on the room rings the alarm. If you win, they're off getting patched up for 12 hours. All of these numbers are in `world/deeds.json`.
- **The clock runs on** while a thing's window is open, through every skill check (working a part of the place's systems, forcing or hacking a door, hacking a computer you've walked up to, picking a pocket, and the story's own checks too), while you're using a computer, and while a menu or the map of the place is open. So the world doesn't wait while you try, and the air can go bad or the alarm stop while you're at it. Talks and fights stop it, and so does **Space**.
- **When the story moves you,** the map follows, and people the story has with you stand beside you.
- **When the story goes somewhere that isn't on the map,** such as somewhere a scene names that no map has, that place gets a room of its own, drawn to suit: ducts look like ducts, a brig has bars, and a chapel has pews. The scene says what kind of room it is and what's in it that matters, and the room is kept with your campaign, so it stays the same if you come back. It's listed on the map of that place from then on.

**Isometric** is what the game looks like, seen from an angle the way Fallout is. It's the same rooms, people, clock and clicks as Walk-around, and everything above applies, except that the room is drawn instead of written out in characters.

- **To walk,** hold **W** to walk up the screen, **S** down it, **A** to the left and **D** to the right, or the arrow keys the same way. Two at once walk along the room's own edges, so **W** and **D** together go up and to the right. Or click the floor, or anyone, a `?` or the name of a way out, and you walk over to it.
- **The camera** shows the whole room if there's room for it. If not, it stays with you, and **What's here** lists the people, systems and ways out that are off the edge; things off the edge come into view as you walk toward them. While **What's here** is open, the camera keeps you in the part of the room it doesn't cover, and folding it gives the camera the whole width back. The mouse wheel zooms in and out. Zoomed all the way out, the room is drawn at 82% of its usual size, so the ship stays big enough to read.
- **One place at a time, loaded whole.** When you arrive somewhere (opening a saved game, starting a new one, crossing over to a dock and going back aboard), a **Loading** screen comes up with the place's name and a bar while every level of it is drawn: all three of the Meridian's decks, or all of a port's rooms. The clock is stopped and you can't walk meanwhile, and a new game's opening scene waits until it's done. Then the place stays loaded until you leave it, so taking the stairs to another deck, or a way out into another room of the same place, shows it at once and whole, with nothing being drawn in front of you. Leaving lets all of it go before the next place loads. On the computer it was tried on, the ship took one to two seconds and Haven's five rooms well under one. A deck saved in the **Map editor** loads the ship again, behind the editor. The game behind the screen works the same way: it keeps only the place you're at, and the ship, which goes on running her kettle, her medbay and her work board while you're away; any other place is put together only when something needs it, such as asking where someone who lives there is, and let go of when you next move on. Only the isometric view draws pictures, so the other views have nothing to load and no loading screen.
- **Walls at the front of a room** are cut down, so that they don't hide anyone.
- **The look** owes something to old isometric games like UnderRail: dark rooms, pools of colored light, gritty floors and walls, and clutter. It's only the style, though. None of their art is in here.
- **The game draws all of it by itself** from each level's file, with no art from anywhere else and no graphics engine, so anything you change in `world/maps/` shows up here too. It works out its own metal, concrete, rock, wood and rust. A mark's shape comes from what it is: `#` is a wall, `=` is a console or a bench, `[]` is a crate, `|` is a post, `~` is water, `%` is rock, scrap or crops depending on the place, and anything shut inside an outline stands up as one solid thing.
- **Lamps and fire:** lamps on the back walls throw pools of light on the floor, and a row of `*` on a plan is something burning, such as a furnace or a reactor. A `*` on its own is a star.
- **Furniture** is drawn too, in the same light as everything else: chairs, the command chair, tables, desks, bunks, sickbay cots, lockers, shelves with tins on them, a plot table with its chart glowing over it, screens on stands and on walls, the galley's kettle with its stove glowing, barrels, potted plants, washbasins, and windows with the stars outside. The Meridian's three decks come furnished, as a first go for you to change with the **Map editor** ([Drawing the ship yourself](#drawing-the-ship-yourself)): chairs at every bank of consoles, windows in the bridge's forward wall, bunks and lockers in the officers' cabins, a long table in the wardroom, benches in the Quiet Room, basins in the Heads, cots in the medbay and the Overflow, real bunks in the Hot Bunks, tables and chairs in the Long Mess, shelves in the galley, the stores and the repair bay, lockers in the armory and the airlocks, plants, barrels and screens on the walls here and there. The beds and tables that used to be drawn as console blocks are real beds and tables now. Tables, lockers, shelves and the like are in the way, and you and everyone else walk round them; chairs, bunks and cots aren't. A thing whose name says what it is is drawn as that instead of a box: the captain's chair as the command chair, the plot table as a plot table, the cracked repeater as a screen on a stand, the soup kettle as the kettle.
- **People use the furniture.** Anyone who stops on a chair, a bench or the command chair sits in it, facing the way it faces: at a console with their back to you and the chair's back between you, at a table facing their food. Anyone asleep or laid up on a bunk or a cot lies in it, head on the pillow and the blanket over them, two to a bunk, one in each bed, so someone in a lower bunk is mostly hidden by the one above. Anyone awake who stops on a bed sits on its edge, as the medbay's morning queue does on the cots. You sit too when you stop on one. Pell and Sorel are in their chairs at the helm and at comms, and so is the night watch; Voss does her paperwork at the ready room's desk; the officers sleep in their cabins' bunks and the crew in the Hot Bunks'; everyone who eats at a mess table has a chair; and the curtained cot in the medbay is turned like the others, so whoever's laid up beside it is in it. A chair or a bed takes only its own square: at a table or in a row of bunks, everyone's name goes over their head.
- **The rooms show how the ship is getting on.** A room with no power has its screens and consoles dark as well as its lamps, and a room with its lights out has no lamp shining. When the ship draws more power than the generator gives (under way, say), the screens and console lights that still have power stutter. Something broken (cut cables, a smashed machine, a pulled injector, anything shot up in a fight or burnt in a fire) throws sparks now and then. A fire burns on more of its room's floor as it grows, with flames, smoke and light of its own; a hole in the hull is a ragged black hole in the wall, rimed with frost, with the air streaming into it and an amber light pulsing beside it. The doors between rooms slide open for whoever comes near and shut behind them; one the ship's systems have locked stays shut, with a red light blinking over it. The stores you can open are drawn as what's in them, crates and fuel cells on a pallet, fewer as they're used and more as they're filled (the lockers keep their own look, and the soup kettle steams while there's soup in it). And the garden grows: each bed goes from turned earth to seedlings, leaves, flowers and fruit and round again, a stage each time the garden has given six lots of produce, so only while it has power and someone tending it. Each bed is at a stage of its own.
- **Wear and clutter.** The ship looks as worn as her hull is battered and as cluttered as her morale is low. With the hull high she's clean; as it falls, oil and rust soak into the floors, puddles shine on the worst of them, and rust runs down the walls from the pipes. With morale high the floors are tidy; as it falls, more is left lying about, and past a point it's the rubbish of people who've stopped caring: tin cups on their sides, meal trays, rags, wrappers. As she starts (hull 70, morale 55) the rooms look as they always did. And what happens leaves its marks: a room a fire burned in keeps the soot on the squares that burned for twelve days, and wherever someone fell there's a dark stain for four, both fading as the crew clean them away.
- **People talk among themselves.** Every few seconds, someone near you says something to nobody in particular, and it hangs over their head for a moment, with a little tail pointing at them: two at most at once, and nobody twice in twenty seconds. What they say is about what's going on, the most pressing first: a fire in the room, the alarm, a hole in the hull, a fire elsewhere, the power or the lights out where they are, a death aboard, the days after the mutiny, a mutiny brewing, an empty kettle, food or medicine run out, their own hurts, a jump, the ship's mood, and otherwise their meal, their work, the night or anything at all. Nobody asleep, laid up or talking to you says anything, and nobody does in a fight or while a story or a window is open. Nothing changes because of it. The lines are in `world/chatter.txt`, to change as you like.

### The story over the room

In Isometric, the room gets the whole window and the story comes up over it, in the same console windows as the rest of the game.

- **A scene comes up a piece at a time.** Click anywhere, on the room or on the story, or press Enter, to go on to the next piece. **Back** goes back. Nothing makes you read it all: you can pick a choice at any point.
- **What someone says** comes up on a pane by their head, in their color: four corners, a tag with their name, and a thin line down to their head. It slides out from the head, and the words write themselves out a letter at a time. A click finishes the line at once, and the next click goes on. If they're far away, the camera moves to keep them in view, and a long speech is cut into pieces that fit.
- **Pull the pane aside** with the mouse to see what's under it. It slides back when you let go.
- **What happens** comes up over the top of the room while you read it, and goes once you've gone on. The last of it goes by itself a few seconds after you've read it, since it's all kept in the quest log. So does anyone with no head in the room to put it over, such as a voice over comms or someone who isn't on the map. Nothing of the story stays along the bottom.
- **What you can say** hangs off the pane of whoever the story is waiting on, once a scene has been read, the way it does in a talk: click one, or press its number. When nobody's being answered, it hangs off your own head. While a quest is on hold there's nothing to answer, and the tracker says who to go back to. It comes up as soon as the last words are written, without waiting for anything else to happen in the room.
- **What's happening:** news, supplies and what your choices did come up as short lines at the top left and go away by themselves, under the story's words when those are up there too. Click one to dismiss it. They all stay in the whole story. A line waits for the piece of the story it goes with, so it doesn't give anything away. The clock runs on while you read, so press **Space** if you'd like the world to wait.
- **Esc puts the story away,** and **The story is waiting for you**, at the top of the room, brings it back, and so does clicking whoever it's waiting on. A new scene brings it back by itself. Until you've read to the end of a scene, a click on the room goes on with the story and doesn't walk anywhere, so to walk off in the middle of one, press Esc, or walk with the keys.
- **The whole story** is in the quest log, quest by quest, under **What happened**, and all of it together is in Classic, under **Reading** in the Settings menu.
- **With less room,** such as in the smallest window, it all goes under the tracker: what happens comes up below it, and the panes keep out of its way. It's one thing at a time: narration goes away while someone's talking, and the choices wait until you've read to the end.
- **In a talk,** what the person says comes up on a pane by their head the same way, and the things you can say hang off it once their words are all there. See below.
- **Big moments come up as a page of the ship's log** instead: the end of the opening battle, and Lieutenant Tarrow's question about the prisoners. It's a console window over the room with a green bar (**Ship's log · day 1, 08:00**), a drawing at the top in the game's own characters (the Meridian, or the brig's two cells) beside the title, all of the words at once, and what you can decide, each answer with its labels (**[MORALE +2] [COUNCIL +3]**, a skill check with your odds). Click an answer or press its number; a page with nothing to decide has **Close** (or 1, or Esc). The clock stops while it's open, the notes and the list in the corner step aside, and you can pull the page aside to see the room. In a talk, the page shows what's said to your answer and what it did, then only **Close**. A closed page stays closed, after a reload too. Any scene or request can be a page: two lines in its file (`PAGE:` and `DRAWING:`, see `world/scenes/_how-to-write-a-scene.txt`), and the drawings are plain text files in `world/drawings` that you can draw yourself.

Under **Reading** in the Settings menu:

- **Story over the room:** **Popups** is all of the above. **One box** keeps everything in the one window at the top of the room, with nothing over anyone's head. **Under the room** puts the story back in a panel under the room, the way it is in Walk-around.
- **People's words:** how fast they write out: **Slow**, **Normal**, **Fast** or **All at once**.
- **Popup font:** **Terminal** matches the rest of the game, and **Book** is the one the story uses in Classic.
- **What each place is made of** and how it's lit is in `world/looks.json`: a cold blue Bridge, an orange furnace, red carpet at the Wheel. Change a word or a color there, and restart the game. It can also put one big thing in a room in place of what's on its plan, which is how the Great Wheel gets to be round.
- **It doesn't change your save,** and the choice is kept with your other reading settings.

**Talking to someone.** In Isometric, with the story over the room, a talk comes up by the person's head: what they say on a pane in their color, with how they feel about you on its tag, and **What do you say?** beside it, on whichever side has the room, once their words are all there. It has the things you can say, to click or pick by their number, and **What was said** folded under them. Your own line comes up over your own head while they answer. Click their pane to finish a line at once, and pull it aside to see what's under it. In Walk-around and Classic, and with the story under the room, the talk has its own window instead, showing the person, how they feel about you, what's been said, and the same things to say.

- **What they say:** someone with a written conversation of their own answers in their own words, and what you say leads on. Everyone else has stock lines, made from who they are and what they're doing. **Ask what they've heard** passes on what's going around.
- **Time:** the clock stops during a talk, so talking can never use up a day aboard. A conversation can't pass time or move you, and sleeping and the Ship menu's orders wait until you've closed the window.
- **Checks and fights:** if something you say leads to a skill check or a fight, its own window takes over, and the talk ends there.
- **Afterwards:** the story keeps one line about the talk, with **What was said** folded under it.

## Doing things yourself

What the ship and the people in it do by themselves, you can also do with your own hands. Everything in this part was added on 2026-11-11, and its rules and numbers are first drafts.

### The crew's jobs by hand

- **Work a machine.** In the Galley, the panel's **You can** offers **Cook a pot (1 hour)**, and in the Garden **Tend the beds (2 hours)**. The time goes by with you at work, the story says what came of it ("You cook for an hour: 200 sittings into the soup kettle"), you get a little practice in the job's skill, and it tires you like any work. It makes nothing from nothing: the galley still needs power, something in the pantry and room in the kettle, and says which it's missing. It matters most when the cooks or the gardeners aren't there: at night, hurt, or short-handed.
- **Treat someone.** While you carry a medkit, anyone hurt has **Treat with your medkit** on their right-click menu and in their window: their worst wound is dressed or splinted, twenty minutes go by, the medkit is used up, and aboard the Meridian they remember it. In a medbay, a patient who's waiting has **Treat them yourself**: you do the medbay's own treatment with its doses, quicker the better your Medicine. A wound that's gone too far is left to the doctor.
- **Pay by the piece.** If you're crew aboard the Meridian, real work at your own job is paid as you do it, a quarter of a shift's pay each time, up to six times a day: a pot cooked for a cook, a patient treated for a nurse, a repair by your own hands for a tech, and a load carried into a store your job is named to carry for, while it has fewer than it wants (fuel cells to the fuel rack and parts crates to the parts lockers for a tech, crates and produce to the pantry for a cook). The eight-hour shift you skip through is still there.

### A use for the things in the rooms

Most of what's drawn in a room used to have words to look at and nothing to do. Now a thing does something by what it's called, and the list is a plain-text file you can change, `world/uses.txt`:

- **Sleep here** on a bunk: the same eight hours as **Sleep** under You can, wherever you'd sleep anyway.
- **Sit a while** in a chair: an hour goes by and takes a little tiredness and stress with it. The captain's chair on the bridge still does what it did, and can be sat in besides.
- **Eat here** at a table in the Long Mess: only while a sitting's on (06:00, 07:30, 18:00, 19:30), once for each, and out of the real soup kettle, so there's nothing to eat when it's empty. At a port's kitchen or noodle stall a plate costs 2 credits.
- **Have a cup** at the tea urn in the Long Mess, or at a kettle, a coffee machine or a bar anywhere: once every four hours.
- **Wash** at the basins in the Heads, once a day.
- **Read it** on a board, a slate, a rota or a ledger: what it says is kept in **What you know**. The Sitting rota in the mess also says when the sittings are, which one is on and how many the kettle has fed today, and Haven's fee schedule and the Wheel's price board say what the port is short of today.
- **Open** on the footlocker in the Hot Bunks, and on the sea chest in the Wardroom: a place to leave your own things, which stay there.
- **Take a moment here** at a viewport, a photograph, a shrine or a garden: fifteen minutes and some stress off, once a day.

A thing that already did something keeps it: something to pick up, written words, a computer, one of the ship's stores. In `world/uses.txt`, each use has the words that mean it ("called bunk, bed, cot, hammock"), how long it takes, what it takes off and how often it can be had; a line under **Things** names one thing at one place and says what it does instead, or nothing (a scan bed isn't a bed); and a line under **Put in the rooms** stands a new thing on a free square of a room, which is how the footlocker, the sea chest, the basins, the tea urn and the bench in the Quiet Room got there. `npm run check-story` reads the file and says what's wrong by its line. After changing it, close and reopen the game.

### Questions for everyone, and two things to ask of them

Fourteen people have conversations of their own; everyone else used to say hello and what they were doing. Now anyone without written lines also answers from what the game knows at that moment:

- **What do you do aboard?** Their job on the watch bill and where they do it, or that they're a spare hand.
- **How's the work going?** A cook says how many sittings are in the kettle, or that it's empty; a tech how many repairs are on the list, or waiting for parts; a nurse how many are waiting to be seen; a guard whether the alarm's going; and anyone, that their job is short-handed and by how many.
- **Where can I find someone?** A list of the ship's officers, and whoever the story is waiting on; pick one and they say where that person was last seen and what they were doing.
- **What's the talk?** The worst thing going on: a mutiny brewing, empty bowls, a supply that's run out, a death, an illness going round, or nothing much.
- **How are you holding up?** From their own body: what's hurt, what they're ill with, or that they're hungry like everyone.

And two things on anyone's right-click menu and in their window:

- **Come with me.** They walk beside you, room to room and deck to deck, for two hours or until you tell them **That's all**. The captain's crew come ("Captain." and they fall in), except an officer who thinks too little of the captain; if you're crew, people of your own job and spare hands come, and an officer has a post to keep; away from the ship, only someone who likes you, or anyone at the place your own life began. Nobody comes asleep, hurt, or with their hands full (a load to carry, a repair, a call). Someone walking with you who can fight stands with you if a fight starts.
- **Go to the medbay**, to someone hurt and on their feet: they go now, and are seen as soon as a medic's there, at any hour, without waiting for the morning queue.

The sentences are in `world/asks.txt`, several to choose between for each, with the words the game fills in written in braces; `npm run check-story` says if a line uses a name or a word the game doesn't know. After changing it, close and reopen the game.

### Emergencies in the rooms

Three things can go wrong aboard by themselves, and each can be put right with your own hands. They only start while you're actually playing aboard with the clock running: never while you sleep, work a shift or skip time, so nothing burns while you couldn't have done anything about it. The story says where it is when it starts.

- **A fire**, in the Galley or Engineering (more likely with something broken in the room). It grows every quarter of an hour, from small to out of control. It spoils the room's air, so the people in it leave; once it's burning hard it breaks something in the room each time it grows; and at its worst it spreads once through a door that isn't locked. In the room, **You can** offers **Fight the fire (10 minutes)**: each go takes one off its size (two if your Engineering is 4 or more) and may burn you. It also shrinks by itself while the room's air is shut off (seal its vent by hand or from a computer). Aboard the Meridian the work board sends an engineer the moment it starts, who walks there, and from three quarters of an hour in gets the better of it and puts it out, usually after it has cost a part. With no engineer able to come it burns on until one can, or you fight it yourself.
- **A breach**, in an airlock, the Boat Bay or the Lighter Bay (more likely with the hull under 40): a plate goes and the room's air goes with it. **Patch the breach (20 minutes)** takes an emergency hull patch or a parts crate from your pack. Left alone, the engineer the work board sent patches it two hours after the plate went, with a parts crate from the ship's stores; with none aboard it waits for one.
- **A brawl**, at a sitting in the Long Mess while morale is under 40: two people come to blows. **Step between them** is your Persuasion against a number, with the odds shown; fail and you take a bruise and they carry on. Nobody steps in, and twenty minutes later they're both hurt and the ship's morale is a point lower.

The rooms and every number are in `world/emergencies.txt`, plain text you can change; `npm run check-story` reads it. Not in this first version: the alarm isn't rung, and the story can't start one yet. A fire and a hole in the hull are drawn in the room (see [Walking around](#walking-around)); a brawl isn't.

### What's yours and what isn't

The ship's stores belong to the people who keep them: the pantry to the quartermaster and the cooks, the medicine cabinet to the doctor and the nurses, the parts lockers and the fuel rack to the chief engineer and the techs, the shuttle stores to the quartermaster and the deckhands. The Armory and the Brig are closed to all but the chief of security and the guards. The captain owns all of it, and if you're crew, what your own job keeps is yours.

**The containers** in the rooms (the lockers, shelves, desks and the rest in [Your inventory](#your-inventory)) belong to someone too, and when one isn't yours its window ends by saying whose ("It belongs to the quartermaster and the cooks."). Aboard, the Galley's are the quartermaster's and the cooks', the Medbay's the doctor's and the nurses', Engineering's and the Repair Bay's the chief engineer's and the techs', the Stores' the quartermaster's and the deckhands', the Bridge's the first officer's, the Wardroom's the captain's alone, the Armory's and the Brig's the chief of security's and the guards', and those in the officers' cabins, the Hot Bunks and the Overflow belong to whoever sleeps there; anywhere else they're anyone's. Ashore, they belong to whoever holds the place: the faction its file names (the Kessara Syndicate at Haven, the Hegemony at Vantor) or, where it names none, the people who live there ("It belongs to the people of Tessen Colony."). In the rooms people live in (the Hook at Haven, the Lower Stacks, the Empty Cells, the Line, the Far Homesteads, the Hearth Rings, the Low Halls and the Warm Stair), they belong to whoever sleeps there.

None of it matters unless you're **seen**: by someone awake and on their feet in the same room, or by a camera that works, and only with the room's lights on. So the hour, a light switch, the cameras' power and who's about decide it, and whoever's walking with you looks the other way.

- **Seen taking** from a store or a container that isn't yours: the story says who saw and what goes back ("Cook Imre saw you take a ration crate from the pantry. It goes back."; everything you took out of one container at once is one line, and one taking), and what you took goes back where it was. Aboard, the witness remembers it, and it counts against the chief of security as a theft. Ashore, your standing with whoever holds the place drops by 3 ("Kessara Syndicate -3: seen taking from the weapons rack at Haven Station"), except from whoever sleeps there, who aren't a faction. A second time within a week, aboard or ashore, also costs you 10 credits.
- **Unseen**, you keep it. The first time for each store and each container, the story tells you whose it was and that nobody saw.
- **Seen in a closed room**: you're told to leave, and if you're still there ten minutes later you're walked out.

Who owns what aboard, the closed rooms and the costs are in `world/law/meridian.txt`; every other place goes by `world/law/elsewhere.txt`, until it gets a file of its own named by its id. Both are plain text you can change; `npm run check-story` reads them. Not yet: the brig for a thief, a Stealth check to take something under someone's eyes, stores at the ports, and a container that's one person's in particular.

### The ship's purse, and living off the ports

Since the eighth step of the logistics rework the Meridian lives off the ports, in every game: nothing comes into her stores from nowhere. A game saved before it is told so once, in the story and in the ship's log, the first time it's opened.

- **The ship's purse** is her own money, apart from yours: 600 credits to begin with, and a levy of 60 a day. It pays for everything bought for her, which is her restocking and what you buy by the lot from the Ship menu, and what she sells and what she's paid for cargo and passengers go into it. Your own credits are yours and aren't touched. Whoever has the chair has the purse. The Ship menu's **The ship's purse** section shows what's in it and what comes in, and under **Put in your own credits** you can move 50 or 200 of your own into it; they're the ship's from then on.
- **What a day costs.** At a dock the same section says what a day there costs her: the fuel cells her generator burns, the fuel she uses keeping station, and what goes of food, medicine and parts beyond what's made aboard, at that dock's prices today. At Haven Station that's about 55 credits, which the levy covers with a little over; medicine, parts and jumps come out of the rest, and cargo and passengers pay for the jumps. Where a day costs more than comes in (the Gilded Wheel is dear), it says how many days the purse will last there. In open space nothing's bought, and she lives on what's aboard.
- **How long the stores last.** Full, she carries 42 fuel cells, two weeks of power, and 36 ration crates beside what the garden grows, about two weeks of food; twelve measures in the tank are a month keeping station, or a handful of jumps. On the Ship menu's **Stores**, food says *holding* while a dock that sells it keeps her stocked, with a line that the dock sends the rest for the purse to pay, and counts the days she'd last everywhere else. A new **CELLS** line does the same for the generator's fuel cells. A fuel cell is only what the generator burns now: it costs a few credits, and it can't be poured into the jump tank any more. Jump fuel is bought for the tank, by the measure.
- **A port sells what it has.** A lot of food is five crates, but a port with three left sells three (**Buy the last 3 crates**), and one with none has none. The same goes for what the dock restocks.
- **The slate.** With an empty purse at a port she's still sold what she needs, on the slate: half again dearer, and up to 300 credits owed at one port. While she owes anything the dock only keeps her going, filling the shuttle stores a third full and not to the top. You can buy by the lot on the slate too, while the port still gives credit: the Buy button says *on the slate*. Each midnight whatever is left in the purse goes to the slate, and then the day's levy comes in, so the day's needs are still bought at the usual price and she climbs back out. Every day something's still owed, your standing with whoever holds that port falls by one. The purse section lists what's owed and where.
- **A distress call.** In open space with no fuel in the tank for even the shortest jump, or with the last fuel cell burned and the generator stopped, she can't get herself to any port. The story says so, and the purse section has **Send a distress call**. About three days later a tanker from the nearest port that sells fuel brings enough to reach that port, and fuel cells for two days if she has none, at twice the price, out of that port's own stores. What the purse can't pay is owed on the slate there. Someone else in the chair sends it without being asked.
- **Freight** is real goods out of a port's stores, in a hold you can open: see [Freight](#freight), below.
- **Passengers.** While the ship is docked at a port and you're ashore there, **You can** offers a party of passengers for another port on your chart ("Take 6 passengers to Vantor Prime"), a new one each week. The fee goes into the purse when the ship gets there, one party at a time, and they eat at the sittings on the way. In the Lighter Bay the same list shows who you're carrying.
- **Whoever else has the chair is careful too.** A captain who isn't you pays for everything as well, so they lie at ports where the fuel and the food cost less, don't fill the tank past its reserve where fuel is dear, and leave a port that costs more in a day than comes in before the money's gone.

Every number is in `world/trade.txt`, plain text you can change; `npm run check-story` reads it. The store sizes and the line that says the shuttle stores are restocked *from the dock* are in `world/logistics/meridian.txt`. The [test save](#the-test-save)'s always-full switch keeps the fuel cells full as well as the tank. Not built: haggling, and buying for the ship by anyone but the captain. One thing to know: the old stories' clocks are still written in the fleet's amounts, so a stage that takes "food 5" takes every ration crate aboard at a stroke, and those now have to be bought back.

### Freight

Since the ninth step of the logistics rework, what the Meridian carries between ports is real. It's all on the **Market** page, wherever you're standing, while you're her captain.

- **The freight hold** is a store in the Aft Holds, on deck 3. It starts empty, and it has thirty squares. Walk up to it and open it beside your pack like any of the ship's stores: whatever she's carrying is in it, as things. The Market's **Freight** section says what's in it, how much of it is hers and how much is carried for a fee, and the Ship menu lists it with the other stores.
- **Everything a port holds can ride in it.** Each good is an item now, in its own count: a sack of meal, a load of ore, a flask of extracts, a keg of beer and spirits, a crated machine. There are forty-four, and a dead ship is the one good nobody carries. Bulky things sit two to a square and small ones six.
- **Freight for hire.** While she's docked, **Freight for hire** lists up to three loads the port can spare for a port you know of that's running low on that good ("5 medicine crates, for Tessen Colony, 60 cr. Tessen Colony is short of them."). **Take it** and the load leaves this port's stores and is craned into the hold. When she docks where it's going, it goes into that port's stores, which really have that many more, and the fee is paid into the purse. On a day when no port you know of is running low on anything this port can spare, nothing's offered: look again tomorrow, or at another dock. The more ports you know of, the more there is to carry.
- **What it pays.** Four credits for each square of the hold the load takes and each measure of fuel the trip takes, times how badly it's wanted there, which is the same number that makes a thing dear where it's short. So a far port that's nearly out pays several times what a near one that's only running low does. It has eight days to arrive; late, it pays half, and the port you were meant to help has gone shorter meanwhile. Only what's still in the hold when she docks is paid for, and if some has gone missing, whoever holds that port thinks a little less of you.
- **Buying and selling for herself.** **Buy and sell by the load** lists every good the port she's docked at holds, how many it has, and what one costs. **Buy 1** and **Buy 5** take them out of the port's stores into the hold, paid from the purse; there's no slate for cargo. **Sell 1** and **Sell all** put what's hers back into a port's stores, wherever a port holds that good. Each one bought or sold changes what the port has, and so its price. Selling pays about half of what buying costs, so a thing is only worth carrying from where there's plenty to where it's scarce.
- **Where things are cheap and dear.** The last list on the page says, for every good, where it's cheapest and where it's dearest today among the ports you know of, the widest gaps first. That's the reason to read the star map: a strike, a fever or a failed harvest somewhere is a price somewhere.
- **Jump fuel is a thing you can carry.** A port that holds jump fuel sells it by the measure into the hold like anything else. Take a measure out into your pack and use it, and it's one more in the tank; so the hold can carry more fuel than the tank does. A drum of refined fuel no longer goes in the tank: it's what a refinery makes cells and jump fuel from. Cinder Reach's market sells measures of jump fuel in its place.
- **What you carry for someone isn't yours to sell,** and the ship's own food, medicine and parts are what's in her stores, not what's in her hold: a ration crate in the hold feeds nobody until someone carries it to the pantry.

The freight line is in `world/trade.txt`, the hold's line (*holds freight*) in `world/logistics/meridian.txt`, and each good's item is named in square brackets on its line in `world/goods`, with the item's own file in `world/items` giving its price, weight and how many go to a square. All of it is drafts for you to change. The story can ask how many of a good a port holds and change it (`? goods ration crates < 200 at haven`, `* goods ration crates -150 at haven: The convoy never came`); `world/scenes/_how-to-write-a-scene.txt` has both. Not built: the crew loading and unloading by hand, and freight for a captain who isn't you. The ships that carry the rest of what the ports send each other are in [Ships you can meet](#ships-you-can-meet).

### A port's stores, where you stand

Since the tenth step of the logistics rework, a port's goods are in its rooms while you're there. Go ashore and they're things you can walk up to.

- **Each port has two or three stores**, named for the place: the granary and the bonded store on Haven's Manifest Docks and the chandler's cage in the Hook, Tessen Colony's grain sheds, leaf barn and cistern shed, the cell yard behind the fence at Cinder Reach's Stack, the pharmacy cage on Anselm's Mercy. Forty-eight across the nineteen ports. Each stands in its room as a stack of crates, and **Open** shows what's in it beside your pack, like any of the ship's stores.
- **What's in one is its share of what the port really has.** Haven's granary usually has twenty ration crates in it. When Haven has half the crates it usually holds, the granary has ten; when Haven is all but out, the granary is nearly bare, and looking at it says so: **It's nearly bare.** The stack in the room is drawn as high as the store is full. A store never has more of a thing than the whole port does, so a small port's store can be most of what it has: the two loads of night flowers in the herb store at Ilun are the monastery's.
- **Only where you are.** A port's stores are filled to their share the moment you arrive, and follow what the port has while you're there. Every other port stays numbers, and so does this one once you've left. Anything of your own that you leave in a store is still in it when you come back.
- **The port's own people carry**, on a board of their own, which isn't the ship's. Haven's dockers, Tessen's farmhands, the Deepyards' melt-hands and cutters, the Keepers at Sarn: whoever the port's file names, while their own day has them up and about in a room with one of the stores or where goods come and go. A docker drinking at the bar isn't carrying, and nobody carries at night. When a store has more or fewer of a thing than its share, one of them carries a trip's worth out to the docks, or brings one in: two ration crates, three fuel cells, one parts crate. You'll see them at it, and their line says so: **carrying 2 ration crates to the granary**.
- **What a ship takes at the dock comes off these shelves.** When the Meridian restocks, when you buy a lot on the Ship menu, when you take a load of freight, that many are carried out of the store that keeps the thing, a trip at a time, and then the store is made up again from what the port has. What she sells or delivers is carried in the same way. So buy five crates of food at Haven and watch the granary: three dockers each fetch a load off it, and then bring it back up.
- **Carrying changes nothing the port has.** The port's amount is the truth, and a store is where some of it stands. Nothing is made or lost between a store and the rest of the port.
- **None of it is yours.** What you take out of a port's store comes out of the port, in real crates: it has that many fewer, and its price goes by what's left. What you put into one is the port's from then on, which makes it a way to give a hungry port something by hand. The law treats a store like any container that belongs to whoever holds the place: seen taking, it goes back and they think less of you; a second time that week is a fine; unseen, you're told once that it wasn't yours (see [What's yours and what isn't](#whats-yours-and-what-isnt)).
- **The Market page lists them.** While you're standing in a port, **Market** has a section for its stores: each one with its room and what's in it against what it usually has, and under them who's carrying what, or why nobody is (**Nobody's about to carry (2 gone home, 1 off elsewhere)**).

Each port's stores are a **Stores** part at the end of its file in `world/ports`, a line a store: `- the granary in The Manifest Docks: usually 20 ration crates, 8 meal, 6 ration packs: A caged bay of crates and sacks...`. Two lines at the top say who carries (`carried by: Docker Pav, Docker Renko, Docker Bo`, names from the place's file in `world/life`) and where goods come and go (`goods come and go by: The Manifest Docks`). The how-to in that folder explains them, `npm run check-story` says when a store is in a room the place doesn't have, holds more than its thirty squares or keeps something the port doesn't hold, and the Story board's **Ports** page has a box for each store. All of it is drafts for you to change: the names, the rooms, the numbers and who carries. Not built: buying straight out of a store (the ship's lots and freight are bought as before, on the Ship menu and the Market page), a port's makers and people walking to their stores (what they make and use is still the midnight's numbers), and carrying a job for the dockers yourself.

### Ships you can meet

Since the thirteenth step of the logistics rework, the hauls between the ports are ships. Everything a port sends for travels in a freighter with a name: **the Cold Windlass**, **the Late Hinge**, **the Patient Harvest**. They're other people's ships, each flying for whoever holds the port she sails from. You can meet them and you can't command them: the fleet stays gone.

- **A freighter's run.** She loads for a day at the port that's sending the goods and sails that night. She's on the way for as many days as the star map puts between the two ports, and then she unloads for a day where she's bound: what she carries is in that port's stores at midnight. What's in her hold is real. It left the first port's stores when she loaded, and it's in nobody's stores until she's unloaded. There are about a hundred and fifty of them out at any time, two dozen or so between the ports a new captain knows of.
- **On the star map.** Each ship under way between two ports you know of is a small **›** on the chart, where she is along her run; rest the mouse on one and it names her, her run, what she carries and when she's due. A place's card lists the **Freighters in port** there and the ones **On their way here**, with what they carry and in how many days. Under the list of places, **Freighters on the lanes** unfolds every one of them. The plot table's chart on the bridge marks them as small diamonds, and lists them in a place's panel.
- **In port beside you.** While the Meridian is docked, the **Market** page has **Ships in port at Haven Station (11)**: a card for each freighter loading or unloading there, with who she flies for, where she's in from or where she's bound, and what's aboard. Anyone can read the cards. The buttons on them are the captain's.
- **Hail her.** Her master answers over the comms: where she's been, what she carries, and what's short and what's plentiful at the far end of her run ("Kessara Drift is short of medicine crates, and has parts crates to spare."). It costs nothing. If the far end is a port you hadn't heard of, it goes on your chart, so hailing is a way to learn the map without going there. With the Meridian's comms down, nobody can be raised.
- **Buy from her hold.** Beside each thing aboard is a button, **Buy 1, 23 cr**: one at a time, out of the ship's purse and into the freight hold. The price is halfway between what the thing costs at the two ends of her run, so a ship in from where a thing is cheap sells it for less than the port she's unloading at. A master sells up to half of each thing aboard; the rest is promised where she's bound. What you buy never reaches that port.
- **See her in.** A freighter that's loading for a port the star map calls Risky or worse (Kessara Drift, Vantor Prime, the Graveyard, Maris Deep), or for a port where convoys are being lost, pays for company: **See her in, 80 cr**. That's 20 credits for each measure of fuel the jump takes you, times the danger. Give your word, then be docked at that port when she comes in, at the midnight her card names: her master pays the fee into the purse, and whoever she flies for thinks a little better of you. Where convoys are being lost, a ship sailing alone never arrives and one the Meridian is waiting for does, so there it's the difference between a port getting what it sent for and not. If you aren't there when she comes in, her master keeps the fee; if she's lost after you gave your word, it costs you standing with whoever she flew for. **Ships you're seeing in**, at the top of the Market page, lists what you've promised and by when.
- **Board her.** **Board her...** asks twice, because it can't be taken back. You're taken down to the **Port Airlock** on deck 2, where she's made fast, and her crew meet you there: the master and a hand, and a second hand when what she carries is worth more than 1,500 credits. If you were ashore, the shuttle takes you back aboard first, which takes an hour. It's a [fight in the room](#a-fight-in-the-room) like any other: it starts stopped, you're just inside the lock's door with whoever's walking with you beside you, and her crew are by the hatch at the far end. In the views that don't play fights in the room it's in the fight window, as every fight is there. Win, or talk them into standing aside, and everything that fits in the freight hold comes across, the dearest first; the port she was bound for goes short of it, and hears why. Lose, and you wake in the medbay as after any lost fight, with nothing. Either way it costs 10 standing with whoever she flies for and 5 with whoever holds the port you did it in, the moment you go for her. A ship you've boarded has nothing more to say to the Meridian. With the door of the room you're in locked, you can't get to the lock, and nobody goes across.
- **A freighter that never arrives is a shortage where she was going.** While convoys are being lost at a port (the star map's news says when), the ships bound there go missing with what they carried, and the news names them: **The Low Errand, out of Cinder Reach, never arrived at Kessara Drift: 41 measures of jump fuel lost with her, and the price of what she carried is climbing there.** And where you are, a ship that's in with what the port was short of is news too: **The Broad Barrel is in from Tessen Colony with 500 ration crates, which Haven Station was short of.**

To try it: docked at Haven, open **Market**. Hail a ship that's in from a port you haven't heard of, and look at the star map afterwards. Buy a crate from one. Find one loading for Kessara Drift or the Graveyard with **See her in** on her card, give your word, set course there and wait for midnight. Or board one, with a medkit on your belt and someone walking with you (ask them with **Come with me**): you'll find yourself in the Port Airlock with the fight stopped, and Space starts it.

The names and the numbers are in `world/freighters.txt`, plain text with its how-to in its own first lines: the two lists of words the names are made from, the share a master sells, what seeing a ship in pays and earns, how many crew she has, what a boarding costs, and the room of the Meridian's where a boarding is fought. That last line is there because you chose the lock over the fight window; take it out, and a boarding is fought aboard the freighter instead, in the fight window. Her crew are two enemies like any others, `world/enemies/freighter_master.txt` and `freighter_hand.txt`. `npm run check-story` reads the file. Three things go on the record for a story to wait on: `boarded_a_freighter`, `robbed_a_freighter` and `escorted_a_freighter`. All of it is drafts for you to change. Not built: the freighter as a place to walk through (her crew come to your lock), freighters for a captain who isn't you, anything for piracy beyond the standing it costs (nobody comes after you), a page for the freighters' file on the Story board, and fights between ships, which the game doesn't have.

### Salvage runs

At the Graveyard, **You can** has **Take the shuttle out to the hulk of the Harrow**: a run out to a dead ship, for anyone, twenty minutes each way. In this first version the wreck isn't rooms you walk through: it's played from the **You can** list, one job at a time, against the 90 minutes of air in your suit's bottle. The air runs down with the clock whatever you do, and Space pauses it like everything else.

- **Bring the power back at the junction box** (15 minutes): the hatch answers quickly after that, and the bridge console wakes.
- **Get the hatch to the engine room open**: 30 minutes by hand, 5 with the power on.
- **Go through the crew lockers** (10 minutes): a medkit and an emergency hull patch.
- **Strip the engine room for parts** (25 minutes, behind the hatch): two parts crates, the prize of the run. One time in four something's still aboard, and a fight starts.
- **Pull the log from the bridge console** (15 minutes): it needs the power and Hacking 2, and the log is worth 60 credits.
- **Go back to the shuttle** shows the air you have left. Everything you took comes home with you, into your pack and your credits.

A tool roll in your pack halves the junction box, the hatch and the engine room. If the bottle runs out first, the shuttle's crew haul you out, what you were carrying stays behind, and it costs you some stress. After a run the wreck is picked over for three days.

The place, the wreck's name, every time, what's in the lockers and the engine room, the console's price and what may be aboard are in `world/salvage.txt`, plain text you can change; `npm run check-story` reads it. Not in this first version: a wreck drawn as dark rooms with its own doors and power, and taking crew along to carry. The three wrecks the rig's parts come from are in [Getting parts](#getting-parts).

## People's lives

Everywhere in the world, people have a day of their own. In Isometric and Walk-around you can watch them get up, go to work, eat, walk around and go to bed, and they carry on whether you're watching or not. Aboard the Meridian that's the 13 crew you know, each with their own habits, and 24 others. In the other 23 places it's the 44 people you can meet there and 231 locals, such as dockers at Haven, kelp farmers at Maris Deep, the debtors in the Gilded Wheel's cellars and the monks who sit in shifts at Ilun. Each place keeps its own hours: the Remnant stands for the anthem at six, nearly everyone at the Wheel stops to watch it spin at eight in the evening, and at Vantor Prime they're home before the last chime. All of this runs on your computer.

- **The clock** runs by itself while you explore, from the moment the game opens. It's under the level, with buttons for **Pause**, **Normal**, **Fast** and **Faster**, and **Space** pauses it, and goes on again at the speed it had. A day in the game takes about two and a half hours at **Normal**, 24 minutes at **Fast**, and 5 minutes at **Faster**. This is time like any other: you get tired and your wounds heal as it goes by, and each midnight is another day of the ship's food and fuel.
- **It runs until you pause it.** It goes on through the menus, the map of the place, reading the story, a thing's window, skill checks and computers, and when you've stepped away. It stops by itself only for fights and talks, which take their own time, while the story's being written, while there's something typed in the typing line, while the Story board is open, and while the game's window is minimized. In the Classic view it never runs by itself, and only the story moves it.
- **Who's in the room** is listed under **People** in **What's here**, with what each of them is doing. Click anyone to walk over and talk, even someone who's just a dim `o`. Someone asleep has to be woken up first. Someone who's on their way somewhere shows only their letter, and you can walk right past them.
- **The Hot Bunks** is the room where most of the Meridian sleeps, through a door on the Names Bulkhead. The map of each place shows how many people are in each room.
- **People go home.** At night a lot of people leave by a door and can't be found until morning, so a place can be nearly empty at three o'clock. You can wait, or press **Faster**.
- **The story knows about it.** A written scene waits for the person it needs, wherever their day has taken them. If the story brings someone over to you, they stay for a while, then go back to their day, from wherever they were standing.
- **Only where you are is walked step by step.** Everywhere else, people are simply where their day says they are whenever you turn up, which keeps a whole world of them quick.

## The wider world

Every place has its own supplies, prices and factions, and they change at each midnight, whether you're there or not. This runs on your computer too, and a whole year of it takes a fraction of a second.

- **Supplies are real amounts.** Each of the nineteen ports with supplies holds so many of each thing, and the star map says how many: **Food 3,616 ration crates, normal**. The word (plenty, normal, short, scarce, almost gone) is how many it has against what it usually holds, so everything that went by the word still does: prices, the news, the stories, what people ask of you.
- **Ports make, use and send for things.** Each midnight a port's makers make what they make, and stop when its stores hold what they usually hold; its people, its ships, its power and its sick use what they use; and when it's running short of something it doesn't make, it sends for a haul to the ports that supply it. A haul leaves the sender that day and arrives days later, by the distance on the star map: two days from Vantor Prime to Bastion Nine, four from Tessen Colony to Haven. Each one travels in a freighter with a name, which you can see on the star map and meet in port (see [Ships you can meet](#ships-you-can-meet)).
- **A shortage travels.** A sender that's running low sends less, and nothing once it's down to what it keeps for itself. So a strike at Cinder Reach stops the fuel being made there, Cinder runs down over a week or two, and then Korrath Forge and Kessara Drift, which have all their fuel from Cinder, run short in their turn. Haven has the Deepyards too and is hurt less. When the strike ends it all comes back, as the hauls arrive.
- **Everything starts in the ground.** Fourteen basic goods come out of the ground, and only where there's ground for them: grain from Tessen Colony's Long Fields and the Sundial's Long Furrow, kelp from the lines off Maris Deep's rafts, sour gas from the wells under Cinder Reach, ice from the Deepyards' Ice Cut, ore from Korrath's deep seams, wrecks from Claim Row at the Graveyard, night flowers from Lumen's Shade Houses, and on Anselm's Mercy the blood its people pay in. The ground gives to whatever hands there are, with or without power, and it's the one place where something comes from nothing: everything else is made from something, a step at a time, grain into meal into ration crates. So a field that fails is felt all the way up: no grain, no meal, no crates, and then the ports that send there for food go short. A station has no ground at all, and a ship has no water of its own: the Sundial brings hers in from Maris Deep.
- **A maker needs what it makes things from.** Ration crates are packed from meal or kelp cake, fuel cells are charged from refined fuel in casings out of a parts crate, medicine crates are made up from extracts or compounds, machines are built from parts crates and circuit boards, and so on up each tree. A port holds those goods too, makes them itself or sends for them, and its makers take them as they work: Haven's kitchens pack ration packs from meal that comes from Tessen Colony, and with no meal they pack none, however short Haven is of them. So a shortage travels down a tree as well as across the map. When Lumen Sanctuary shuts itself in, Anselm's Mercy runs out of the extracts it makes medicine from, then of medicine, and then the Sundial, which has its medicine from Anselm's Mercy, runs short in its turn, three weeks after it began.
- **And hands, and power.** A port that's short of food or of drinking water has fewer hands at its makers, down to three quarters of their work when there's none. One that's scarce of medicine has more of its people laid up, and one that's scarce of fuel cells has less power, down to four tenths of its makers' work with none at all: what can be done by hand. The makers of fuel itself run on what they make, so a port can always get its power back. When a port's makers are slowed, the star map says so under its supplies: **Its makers are short of hands (ration crates scarce): they're doing about nine tenths of their work.**
- **The map says why.** Under a supply that's short, the star map gives the reason in a line: **Nothing's being made here**; **41 measures of jump fuel from Cinder Reach are due tomorrow**; **Cinder Reach has none to spare (nothing's being made there)**; **What was sent here never arrived**; **Its makers are short of extracts (nothing's leaving Lumen Sanctuary)**. A port you haven't heard of isn't named. **Everything it holds**, folded under the supplies, lists the rest: forty-five goods in six trees (food, fuel, medicine, parts, water and air, comforts), each with how many, where it comes from (another port, or the ground here: **from the Long Fields here, up to 330 a day**) or what it's made from here, what the port's makers take of it in a day, and what's on its way. The **Market** has the same under **What this port has today**, and the Ship menu's list of what a dock sells says how many it has.
- **What goes on at a port does real things** now, not a nudge to a percentage: a strike or a cold furnace cuts what's made, a fever or a cornered market takes goods off the market, refugees or high season use more, raiders or a big trader bring goods in, a debt war shuts a port so that what was sent waits outside, and a lost convoy never arrives. A harvest or a bloom fills a port past what it usually holds, and food is cheap there while it lasts. A strike stops a whole tree's makers, the refinery as well as what's made from its fuel; what's taken off a market or brought to it is the finished goods, not what they're made from.
- **The Sundial and Anselm's Mercy are ports now.** The farm ship makes her own food from her own meal and sends ration crates to the hospital ship; the hospital ship makes her own medicine from Lumen's extracts, sends medicine to the Sundial, and sends Lumen the plasma and dressings its benches make medkits from. Both have supplies on the star map, their prices follow what they hold, and the food and the medicine the Meridian buys there are real crates out of their stores. The fleet's own people hold them, and that never changes; being on good terms with them gets you their friend's price, as at any port.
- **Where you stand, it's in the rooms.** Go ashore at a port and some of what it holds is there to see: stores you can open, each with its share of what the port has, and the port's own people carrying for them (see [A port's stores, where you stand](#a-ports-stores-where-you-stand)). Everywhere else a port is numbers.
- **Nothing at the ports is made from nothing any more,** but what their ground gives. Nor aboard the Meridian: she buys her ration crates and fuel cells at a dock, out of the port's own stores (see [The ship's purse, and living off the ports](#the-ships-purse-and-living-off-the-ports)).
- **Prices follow the supplies.** The less there is of something, the more it costs, up to about three times the usual price, and when there's plenty it's cheaper. That goes for what the ship buys in the Ship menu and for what's made from it in the Market: a medkit costs more where medicine's short, and so does what you sell there.
- **What you buy and sell is real crates.** A lot of food is five crates out of the port's stores, and one sold is five more in them. A big port hardly notices; a small one does: a crate of medicine is a tenth of what Tessen Colony usually holds, and its price climbs. A port with less than a lot left sells what it has, and one with none has none. What's bought is paid for from the ship's purse, and what's sold is paid into it. Selling to a place that's short pays well, and it helps them. The ship still needs what you sell, though.
- **Who holds a place** is worked out from how much of a say each side has there, which you can see on the star map. It shifts a little each day. Shortages get blamed on whoever's in charge, and things that happen move it further. A side that gets clearly ahead takes over, and you'll hear about it wherever you are. Some places can't change hands, such as Vantor Prime and Foundry Seven.
- **Whoever holds a place sets your prices there.** If they hate you, everything costs a quarter more, and if they like you, a little less.
- **Things happen,** each place with its own: raiders at Tessen, a fight on the picket at Cinder Reach, a bloom at Maris Deep, high season at the Gilded Wheel. Trouble makes a place one step more dangerous while it lasts.
- **News** is at the bottom of the **Star map** menu, for the places you know of. You're only told in the story about what's happening where you or the Meridian are, or anything big. People also pass the latest on when you ask what they've heard.
- **The Star map** shows all of this for each place, and which supplies are short. The list of factions on the BELIEFS page of your registry printout says what each one holds.
- **The story can change it.** When the story changes things, it changes them for real, so if you help the strikers take the Stack, they hold it from then on.
- **The rest:** the Choir's places, the Pale Gate and the Meridian herself have no daily supplies of their own. The Meridian runs on her own stores, on the Ship menu.

## Stories don't wait

The world isn't waiting for you. Each story has what happens if nobody steps in, and it happens at midnight on the day it comes round, on your computer. Leave the strike at Cinder Reach alone, for example, and the hired men try the Gate, the strikers throw them back, and the fuel stops coming out.

- **It really changes things.** A story that moves on can change what a place has and who holds it, and it can cost the Meridian morale, supplies or fuel.
- **Nothing's ever closed.** A lead that's moved on says how things stand now, and it's marked **it's moved on** in the **Leads** menu. If it's been written out, you can still take it up from there.
- **Once you've taken a story up, it's in your hands,** and its clock stops.
- **What you did has consequences, on a timer.** A story's clock can say what follows from how a story ended, or from a fact on the record, and how many days later, such as a dock closing to the Meridian a week after a raid there. Each happens once, in the news and under **Moved on without you**, and it can leave facts of its own on the record.
- **A stage can depend on the record.** A stage with a condition happens only if the condition holds when its day comes, and is skipped otherwise. Two stages on the same day with opposite conditions are a fork, so what the world does on its own fits what you did. `npm run check-story` checks the clock's conditions along with the rest.
- **Bigger stories start moving later,** and a story that follows another one opens once that one's finished or has run its course.
- **A story can have a clock by act.** `world/story-clock.json` can hold events for each act of a longer story, and the day an act ends by itself if nobody ends it. It's empty since the written main story was taken out on 12 November 2026, so nothing happens by act for now.
- **You won't be swamped.** Only a few of these come up in the story at once. All of it is under **Moved on without you** in the **Leads** menu, and in the **News** on the **Star map**.
- **Every game's world goes at its own pace,** so you won't see the same things on the same days twice.

## The record

The game keeps a record of what you did: how each story ended, and the lasting facts that left, such as a door closed to the Meridian or a debt owed. It's in the quest log (**J**, or a click on the tracker at the top left): a finished story's card carries its ending's name and its last line, and **Facts on record** lists the facts. Every story's file in `world/stories` names its endings (under *Where it can end up*), can name the facts the story leaves (*Facts it can leave*, a name and a line of plain words each) and can say what on the record changes the story (*What on the record changes it*, each a condition written like a scene's, such as `ended a_story its_ending` or `flag a_fact`, and a line on what changes). The written story ends a story with `* ending its_name: How it ended` and puts facts on the record with `* flag` lines, and its scenes can ask `? ended a_story its_ending`. A story's reactions are notes for whoever writes its scene: the checker reads their conditions, and the story clock's own forks and consequences read the record too. `npm run check-story` checks the notes along with the scenes: an ending without a name, a reaction the game can't read, or one waiting on a fact nothing leaves.

## Leads and the wider world

The **Leads** menu shows what you've heard about wherever you are: a few stories you could start, and what people are saying. **Look into this** starts a story that's been written out. One that hasn't says **Not written yet**: it moves on by itself, and the news says how it's going. For now that's all of them: the stories that were written out went with the main story on 12 November 2026, and the 108 that are left are notes for whoever writes them. Leads change as you travel and as time passes, and finishing one story can open another. They don't wait for you, either: see [Stories don't wait](#stories-dont-wait).

There's a lot more out there than the places you start out knowing about, and you'll hear about them as you go. Every place you know of is on the **Star map**.

## Your files

The world is kept in files you can open with any text editor. Be warned that most of them contain spoilers, including everyone's secrets and how the whole story ends.

- `world/scenes/` has the written story, one plain text file for each scene: its parts, what each says, the choices, the checks and fights they lead to, the conditions on them, and what they change in the game. `talk_<id>.txt` is what that person says in a talk, and `arrive_<place>.txt` plays when the player arrives somewhere. `_how-to-write-a-scene.txt` there explains it all, including a thing's `used` and a door's `ask` in the map files, and the stock lines at the end of `rooms.json`. These are read again whenever the story needs them, so there's no restart. `npm run check-story` lists every mistake with its line, and `npm test` checks them too.
- `world/characters/` has one plain-text file for each important person, whether you've met them or not: who they are, how they look, speak and think, their numbers, and how they fight. `_how-to-write-a-person.txt` there explains every line.
- `world/enemies/` has one plain-text file for each type of enemy, with its numbers, why it usually fights (`motive`), who for (`faction`), and what it says in a fight. `_how-to-write-an-enemy.txt` there explains every line. Against you, threat levels 2 and up fight harder than their files say, by the amounts in `FIGHT_DIFFICULTIES` in `src/combat.js`, depending on the difficulty you've chosen.
- `world/places/` has one plain-text file for each place: what it's like, its districts, who's there, what could happen and what people say. `_how-to-write-a-place.txt` there explains every line.
- `world/species/` has one plain-text file for each kind of people a character can be, with what the character creator gives them, and their **Body** part. `_how-to-write-a-species.txt` there explains every line.
- `world/chatter.txt` has what people say to nobody in particular, over their heads, by moment (Eating, Hungry, In the dark, After the mutiny, and the rest, listed at the top of the file with when each is true). A line can be 60 letters long at most. It's Claude's draft, for you to change.
- `world/battles/` has the battles: who fights each one, where they come in and when, one plain text file each. `_how-to-write-a-battle.txt` explains every line. See [Battles](#battles).
- `world/drawings/` has the drawings for the top of a page of the ship's log, each a few lines of plain characters in its own file. `_how-to-draw.txt` says how to draw one. See [The story over the room](#the-story-over-the-room).
- `world/health/` has the bodies: `wounds.txt` has every kind of wound and old mark, and a file for a place (`meridian.txt`) has who starts the game hurt there. `_how-to-write-health.txt` explains every line. See [Bodies](#bodies).
- `world/stories/` has one plain-text file for each story that can come up: its lead, what's really going on, how it might go, its endings by name, the facts it leaves, what on the record changes it, and what happens if nobody steps in. *For anyone who isn't the captain* is how it reaches someone who isn't the captain, and a story without one stays the captain's own. `_how-to-write-a-story.txt` in that folder explains every line, and the **Story board** in the game shows and changes the same files as boxes and arrows.
- `world/arc.json` has the acts and clues of the main story that was taken out on 12 November 2026. Nothing you see reads it now: it's kept for the acts' names and for the clues a written scene can note.
- `world/ship.json` has the Meridian: who's aboard and what they use up, what she starts with, the ration levels, what each shortage does, and everything she says in the story. `council` is what the officers and the council will do by themselves, and when. `chair` is how someone else in the captain's chair runs her, with each captain's own habits.
- `world/starmap.json` has the star map: where each place is, how dangerous it is, who controls it, and what the Meridian can buy there, at its usual price. `fare` and `passage` are what it costs to go there on someone else's ship.
- `world/starts.json` has every life the game knows, with `"off": true` on the ones that aren't offered when a new game is made (every one but the captain's, for now): where it starts, the job and what it pays, who you know, where you stand with each faction, your first quest, and notes on who they are. Instructions are at the top.
- `world/daily.json` has what changes at each place from day to day: which supplies it deals in, how much of a say each side has, how prices follow supplies, and everything that can happen there, with what each thing does to a port's supplies while it lasts. Instructions are at the top.
- `world/goods/` has the world's goods, a tree to a file (food, fuel, medicine, parts, water and air, comforts), each good at its step of the tree with what it's made from, which good each of the four supplies is counted by, and which goods a port's hands, its sick and its power need. `world/ports/` has a file for each port: what it usually holds of each good and where it sends for more, what comes out of the ground there, what it makes in a day and from what, what it uses, and where some of it stands in its rooms (its stores, who carries for them, and where goods come and go). They're drafts, with numbers worked out from how many people each port has; change a line and the game plays it. Each folder has its how-to, `npm run check-story` reads them, and the Story board has **Ports** and **Goods**.
- `world/story-clock.json` has a story's clock by act: events for each act, and the day an act ends by itself. It's empty since the written main story was taken out, and its instructions say how to fill it. Each story's own clock is in its file in `world/stories/`.
- `world/maps/` has one file for each place, with every room drawn in characters, which you can edit like a picture. Move a letter on a level to move a person or a thing. The file also has each room's colors, what each thing says when you look at it, what happens when you use it, what's in it if it can be opened (`holds`), what you can first say to each person, and which doors are closed. Instructions are at the top of `meridian.json`. A place with no file here just stays in the classic view.
- `world/maps/meridian/` has the Meridian's three decks, drawn in Tiled, one file to a deck with its rooms drawn on it as rectangles, with the same things in them as a room in characters, and `world/maps/tiles/` has the stand-in tiles they're drawn with. A deck or room drawn in Tiled counts instead of one in the place's file. See [Drawing the ship in Tiled](#drawing-the-ship-in-tiled). They're read again whenever one is saved, with no restart.
- `world/life/` has one file for each place, with everyone's day: where they sleep, eat and work, and what they do at what time. Instructions are at the top of `meridian.json`. A place with no file here keeps its people at their posts, with no clock.
- `world/looks.json` has what each place and room is made of, and how it's lit, in the Isometric view. Instructions are at the top.
- `world/rooms.json` has the kinds of room the game can draw for anywhere that isn't on a map, such as ducts, a brig or a chapel, with the things you'd usually find in each. It also has what anyone can be asked in a talk, and how people look when you come over.
- `world/items/`, `world/factions/` and `world/perks/` have one plain-text file for each item, each faction and each perk, with a how-to in each folder. An item's `size`, `weight` and `stack` lines say how many squares it takes in the inventory, how heavy it is, and how many go on one square. Its `found in` and `rarity` lines say which kinds of container it turns up in and how often, and its **Drawing** part is its little picture in the inventory, one row to a line between backticks (items without one are drawn as their kind). `world/loot/containers.txt` names the kinds of container: what a thing in a room has to be called to be one (a dresser, a crew locker, an armory's rack, a crate), how many squares it holds and how many things, which rooms and places hold more or fewer, and what the lockers, shelves, desks and barrels drawn on the Meridian's decks open as. Since the second step of the loot plan there are 300 things to find, each a file of its own, written by Claude for you to change (their `draft:` line says so; take it out once one is yours): clothes worn in the armor's place, weapons and armor, food and drink, medicine, tools and gadgets, parts and salvage, papers and data, personal things and contraband. A new kind, `type: goods`, is something to keep, sell, read or give back that does nothing by being carried. A supply's `effect` can be `stress 5` (it eases stress) or `dress 1` (it dresses a wound), and an item's **When read** part is what's written on it, which **Read** in the inventory shows. The character creator's shop doesn't sell the things that are only found, unless a market sells them too. Since the third step the Meridian's containers open (see **Containers aboard the Meridian** above): `world/loot/containers.txt` has a `chances:` line (how often a common, uncommon or rare thing turns up) and, after each kind, how a piece of furniture of that kind looks. Since the fourth step every place's containers open (see **Containers everywhere else** above): the file's **Rooms that get one** part gives a room a container of its own (`haven, The Hook: crew locker, called a refugee's footlocker` for one room, `rooms called bar, tavern: cooler` for every room with one of those words in its name, `nothing` for none; a room's own line comes first, and a room that already has a container gets no other), and its **Things** part makes a thing open as another kind than its name says, or not at all (`haven, Crates of paper: desk`). Since the sixth step an item can say `keepsake: yes` (whoever it was taken from asks after it, `world/scenes/request_keepsake.txt`) and `salvage: 2` (what it counts for toward a parts crate), and the logistics file's `salvage makes: 1 parts crate from 10 salvage, in The Repair Bay, in 2 hours, into the parts lockers` line says what the bench makes, where, how long it takes and where it goes. `npm run check-story` checks it all. The Story board shows found in (a tick for each kind of container), rarity, keepsake, salvage, the drawing and the words to read on every item, and a draft's About box says it's a draft; its Logistics page has the salvage makes line among the numbers at the top.
- `world/inventory.json` has the inventory's numbers: the size of the pockets, the pack, the belt, something that holds things and a pile on the floor, how much you can carry and how much faster too much tires you, each kind of item's usual size and weight, and what the people you beat might leave. A `loot` line in an enemy's or a person's file says what they leave instead. It's read when the game starts, so a change there needs a restart.
- `world/programs/` has one plain-text file for each program a rig can carry: its kind, the Hacking to write it, the memory it takes, the computers it works on, how much noise the watcher hears from it, how long it takes at the bench and where it can be copied. `_how-to-write-a-program.txt` there explains every line. They're read again a moment after one changes, with no restart.
- `world/rig-parts/` has one plain-text file for each board, casing and part a rig can be built from: its kind, grade, weight, worth, heat and what it adds, what a link reaches, a board's sockets on their grid, what making it at the bench takes, and where it comes from. `_how-to-build-a-rig.txt` there explains every line. Beside them, `_tools.txt` has the bench's tools, `_from-the-ship.txt` the kinds of the ship's parts that can be taken apart and what comes out, and `_finds.txt` the things that turn up aboard. They're read again a moment after one changes, with no restart.
- `world/wrecks/` has one plain-text file for each wreck in the Graveyard the rig's parts come from: its jobs, their minutes and Engineering, and what comes out. `world/dealers/` has one for each port's dealer in rig parts: what they buy, their markup, what they want and what they sell. Each folder has a how-to. They're read again a moment after one changes, with no restart.
- `world/computers/` has one plain-text file for each computer the player can get into: its class, where it is, what's on it, what its programs do, and what it reaches. `_how-to-write-a-computer.txt` there explains every line, and `_flavor.json` has the words for the computers the game makes up. These are read again when the board saves one, with no restart.
- `world/systems/` has a file for each place that has systems of its own: who guards it and keeps it running, and each system with its parts and the rooms they're in. `_defaults.json` has what every other place gets, the numbers they all start from, and each kind of part's look and work by hand. `_how-to-write-systems.txt` explains every line. These are read again when the board saves one, with no restart.
- `world/logistics/` has the Meridian's logistics: where each of her stores is and what it holds, what each machine takes and makes and how often, when the sittings are, what each store wants kept in it, when that's urgent and who carries for it, what each system draws from the generator and the order they're let go in, and what's refilled from nothing, how often and how far. `_how-to-write-logistics.txt` there explains every line. It's read again when the board saves it, with no restart.
- `world/command/` has the Meridian's chain of command: each post, the room it's kept in, who chooses whoever holds it, the jobs it heads and the skills that count for it; the jobs and how many each wants; and who holds each post when a game begins. A council decision in `world/ship.json`, a guard or engineer in `world/systems/`, or a store's carriers in `world/logistics/` can name a post (the quartermaster) instead of a person. `_how-to-write-command.txt` there explains it.
- `world/deeds.json` has the numbers for picking pockets and attacking people from the right-click menu: how hard a pocket is, what's in it, what being caught or attacking costs, who fights as what, and how long someone beaten is away. It's read when the game starts, so a change there needs a restart.
- `world/checks.json` has everything said in the skill check windows, and which game each skill uses. For hacking it also has the classes of computer with their numbers and what training adds. It's read when the game starts, so a change there needs a restart.
- `world/combat-text.json` has everything that gets said in a fight.
- `world/parley.json` has everything about talking in a fight: every reason to fight and how much harder each makes each option, the options themselves, and what gets said. Instructions are at the top.
- `app/` has the app that the game runs in. See [The app](#the-app).
- `world/art.json` has the console art for fighters. Each figure is drawn facing right, and the game turns enemies around. A character's or enemy's `art` setting picks which figure they use.
- `data/campaigns/` has a folder for each campaign. People from your character's past get their files here, and so did anyone an older game's AI game master added. They're kept for good, and their stats are generated by the game. Rooms the story has invented are in its `rooms` folder, and you can give one its own drawing: Section Fourteen's file shows how. `captain.json` is a readable copy of your character's stats, and changing it does nothing.

Saved copies of the same campaign share a folder, and a new game starts with an empty one. Restart the game after changing a file.

## Reading the story

- **People:** everyone who speaks gets their own name label and color, with a colored bar beside their words. Speech over comms has a dashed bar and looks like a transmission, machines look like machines, and the Choir sounds like nothing else.
- **Lore:** underlined words are people, places and ideas the game can explain. Hover over one for a summary, including how they feel about you or your standing with them. Click it to open a person's file in **People**, or anything else in the **Codex**, which fills up as you come across things. On a phone, tap the word.
- **Consequences:** a boxed **Consequence** in the story marks something that will come back later.
- **Results:** colored lines show what your choice actually did: checks, items, money, standing and beliefs. Green is good news and red is bad.
- **Choices:** tags before a choice show what it involves. `[PERSUASION 60%]` is your chance on a skill check, `[MERCY]` is the belief it expresses (red if it goes against your convictions), `[VOSS +]` or `[TARROW -]` means someone will approve or disapprove, and `[! costs 200 credits]` is a known cost or risk. Hover over a tag for details.

To change the story's font and size, use **Reading** in the Settings menu. **Book** is easiest on the eyes, and **Terminal** keeps the whole game in the console style. **Story font** is for Classic, the whole story and the panel under the room, and **Popup font** is for [the story over the room](#the-story-over-the-room). **Text size** goes for both. **Story view** is there too: see [Walking around](#walking-around).

**Display** in the Settings menu sets how big everything is, so the game fits your screen:

- **Interface size:** **Automatic** makes everything bigger in a bigger window, including the room, so it looks the same on any screen. At the size the app first opens (1400 by 900) and smaller, everything's drawn at its normal size. **Small**, **Normal**, **Large** and **Largest** set it yourself. A size can't go past what still fits in the window, so **Largest** in a small window is no bigger than **Normal**. Your choice is remembered.
- **Full screen:** press **Go full screen**, or F11 in the app. You can also pull the edges of the window to make it any size, and the screens rearrange themselves to fit.

## Creating your character

Press **New game** and pick **Build from scratch**, **Surprise me**, or a ready-made character you can still change. Building from scratch takes you through these steps:

1. **Identity:** name, pronouns and appearance.
2. **Your place:** the captain of the Meridian. The 21 other lives are switched off for now. See [Who you are](#who-you-are).
3. **Species, homeworld and upbringing:** these set your starting attributes, skills, money and reputation.
4. **Attributes:** spend points on eight abilities. These decide your odds in the next step.
5. **Life simulation:** live your life in four-year terms, choosing a career each time. Each term rolls for survival and promotion, and gives you a decision to make. The choices shape your skills, beliefs, friends and enemies. Stop whenever you like. More terms mean more skills, but slower learning later, and aging starts to cost you after term 4. Your life ends at the Fall of the Concord.
6. **Skills:** spend points across 25 skills, and tag 3 that you'll improve faster.
7. **Traits:** choose strengths, paid for with points that flaws give back.
8. **Ideology:** set your beliefs on five axes. Strong beliefs become convictions, with real benefits and costs.
9. **Drive and bonds:** what you want most, and who you'd do anything for.
10. **Gear:** spend your savings. Whatever's left stays as credits.

Your life history is on your registry printout (press **C**). Your traits and beliefs change your numbers, and the written story can ask about them.

## How the simulation works

- **Checks:** the story picks a skill and a difficulty, then you play out the check in its own window. The game rolls a d20 and adds your attribute, skill, bonuses and what you earned in the window. You see the full breakdown each time.
- **Learning:** skills improve as you use them, as well as from the points you get when you level up.
- **Stress and fatigue:** frightening events and hard choices build stress, and time builds fatigue. Both give penalties when they get high, and enough stress causes a breakdown. Sleep to recover.
- **Injuries:** these have real penalties, and heal with time or treatment.
- **Beliefs:** your choices move your beliefs. Acting against a strong belief causes stress, and sharing a belief with someone helps your social checks with them.
- **The ship:** a day's numbers are all worked out in one place, and the Ship menu shows them, so what you read is what happens.
- **Long waits:** a jump, or anything else that takes more than a day, passes a day at a time, so three days at once come out exactly like three single days: each day the medbay treats people and lets them go, the doctor makes the rounds and the stores are counted, however long you're away.

Saved games from before this system still load. They get a basic character profile, and you need to start a new game to use the creator. When you start a new game, your last one is kept as **Previous game** on the title screen.

### The test save

**Test save** on the title screen is a game made for trying things out, kept apart from your own saves. It's a new captain on the bridge on the morning of day 1, past the opening, with no mutiny fought. Three of the game's limits are switched off in it, and in no other game:

- **The fuel stays full.** The Meridian's tank is topped up whenever time passes, so FUEL reads 12 of 12 after any number of days or jumps. The fuel cells her generator burns are topped up with it, so CELLS stays full too and she never goes dark for want of one, docked or not. Her water tank is kept full with them, since the air plant can't run without water.
- **Your rig needs no parts to hack.** It connects to anything (a desk, a keypad, a junction box, a camera, the Navy's own system) whatever link is fitted, it always starts, it has room for every program you load (99 blocks), the most power parts can give, and no heat of its own. The hacking itself plays as usual: what each program does, the watcher, a computer's class, hardened and sealed systems.
- **Every hacking program is on your rig, loaded.** All twenty-four, without writing, buying or copying any of them: the **Rig** page lists them under what's loaded, and at a computer they're all on the rig's D: page to run. Each still only works where it would (a hardened computer still wants its way in found first). If you'd rather try with fewer, unload some on the Rig page: what you unload stays unloaded, and it's still on the rig to load again. A test save you made before this gets them the next time you open it, and its story says so once.

Saving another game into it makes that an ordinary save. To make a fresh one, run `node tools/test-save.mjs --again` in the game's folder (without `--again` it won't write over a test save that's already there).

## The story

A story is written in advance, in plain text files in `world/scenes/`, and the game plays it by itself. The choices are the ones on offer: click one, press its number, or type part of it. What the story can't answer, it says so, and nothing changes.

**For now the only written story is how the game opens.** On 12 November 2026 the main story was taken out of the game, at the owner's word: The Beacon and Fuel for the Ship, the three written openings, the arrival at Haven, the first act (The Warm Room, Two Weeks of Fuel, Visiting Hours and the council meeting), The Downstairs Crew, and Tharel's conversation. In its place, a new game opens with a mutiny. What that leaves:

- **A new game** opens with the mutiny on deck 1 (below), and no quest. After it, what there is to do comes from the ship and her people: what they ask of you (see [Requests](#requests)), the chain of command, her stores and systems, and everything under [Doing things yourself](#doing-things-yourself). Another life, in a game saved from when it was offered, begins with one line, **No story is written for now**.
- **The other stories,** 108 of them, are still in `world/stories/` as notes. Each shows in the **Leads** menu as **Not written yet**, and moves on by itself, by its clock. Two that could only begin after the main story are set aside with the fleet's.
- **A game saved in the middle of the old story** opens with that story's quests closed and one line saying so. What people have asked of you, the journal, the people you know, the record and everything about the ship are as they were.
- **The engine that plays a written story is all still there.** Put a scene file in `world/scenes/` and it plays as before: `opening_captain.txt` opens a new game, `arrive_<place>.txt` plays on arriving somewhere, and `thread_<story>.txt` makes a story playable from the Leads menu, which lists written ones first. The game's tests play small scenes of their own this way (`test/fixtures`).
- **Nothing is lost for good:** the files that were taken out are in the project's history.

### The opening battle

A new game starts at eight in the morning on the bridge. The lights go to red, and Ensign Vey, Lieutenant Sorel and Commander Voss tell you why: armed crew are coming up both stairs, with sidearms from the armory and red cloth round their arms, and they want the bridge. You can hold the bridge, or get on the speakers first and tell them to stand down, which they answer with a shot. Then it's a battle across the whole of deck 1 (see [Battles](#battles)), which starts stopped:

- **With you:** everyone on the deck who can fight: Commander Voss, Ensign Pell, Lieutenant Sorel and Ensign Vey, who hold the bridge, and Father Roe and Deckhand Kofi in the Names Bulkhead. The spare hands there take cover in the Quiet Room.
- **Against you:** twelve mutineers: six up the forward stair at once, six more up the aft stair twelve seconds in.
- **Help from below**, thirty seconds in: Lieutenant Tarrow up the aft stair, and the three ensigns up the forward stair from the mess. The guards would come too, but nobody's doing that job on the watch bill.
- **How it ends:** won, when the last of them are down or have given up, and then the story says the deck's yours again; or you go down, it's fought out without you, and you come to in the medbay, where the story picks up. Either way the mutiny is put down. Whoever went down, on either side, lies where they fell (see [After the battle](#after-the-battle)).

In about fifty simulated battles, staying on the bridge and shooting the nearest of them won nearly every time, doing nothing at all won two in three, and charging them alone lost every time. The mutineers and the crew who fight without a file of their own are new enemies in `world/enemies` (`mutineer_crew.txt`, `ship_hand.txt`), the opening's words are in `world/scenes/opening_captain.txt`, and the battle is `world/battles/mutiny.txt`, all drafts for the owner to change. What happens to her people and to the ship afterwards is below.

### After the battle

When the battle's over, everyone is where it left them, and the ship has to deal with them.

- **The fallen lie where they fell,** each with a red cross over them, on both sides: your own under their names, and the mutineers under the names the fight gave them, Mutineer #1 to Mutineer #12.
- **The medics carry your own first.** The nurses, and the spare hands when there aren't nurses enough, go out to each of the fallen, your side before theirs, and carry them to the medbay. Its four cots fill quickly, and everyone after that is laid on its floor and moved to a cot as one frees. Dr. Aduro treats the worst off first, one wound at a time, with doses from the medicine cabinet, and anyone with a deep wound rests for three days after it's treated. Someone wounded who's still on their feet can lose enough blood waiting to go down later, and be carried in too.
- **Someone can die of it.** Someone left bleeding too long dies, as anyone can (see [Bodies](#bodies)), and goes up on the chalked names on the Names Bulkhead. A mutineer who dies goes up as "An unnamed mutineer": the fight's name was only ever a label.
- **The brig.** The mutineers who gave up are taken to the Brig at once. The rest are walked there under guard once they're on their feet, with a line when each one is: in a test game, all of them by the fourth day. The Brig keeps them as a number, without names. Its **About this room** says how many are held there and how many are still to come, and so does the **Command** menu, under **In the brig**. What's to be done with them is a question for later.
- **What it costs.** Morale falls by 5, and by 2 more for each of yours who went down, and by 5 more if you went down yourself. The council's confidence in you falls by 10, or 15 if you went down. Both are lines in the battle's file.
- **They remember.** Everyone who fought beside you remembers it, on their card in the **Command** menu ("fought beside you on deck 1 when the mutineers came"), and it counts towards their loyalty to you.
- **Short-handed.** Whoever went down is off their job until they're back on their feet, and Commander Voss covers it with a spare hand, as the watch bill always does.
- **The ship, every time.** A fire breaks out in the room where the most went down that still has air to burn: usually the Upper Forward or Aft Stair, since the Names Bulkhead, where most fall, loses its power and its air with it. A plate goes in the Boat Bay, and the air goes out of it. The junction box at the Names Bulkhead is shot out, so the Names Bulkhead, the Long Mess, the Galley, the Garden and the Hot Bunks go dark. The nav core on the bridge is shot up, so until it's mended every jump is plotted by hand, slower and a fuel more. And a speaker or a camera is shot out in every room where anyone went down, with the comms relay too if anyone went down on the bridge.
- **If you went down,** more is broken: the bridge's own junction box, and another speaker or camera in each of those rooms.
- **Mending it.** The work board sends an engineer to the fire, who has it out after about an hour and a quarter, and another to the breach, who patches it after two hours with a parts crate; both are busy with that meanwhile, so with engineers among the fallen the rest waits. What was shot up they mend one thing at a time, power first: the Names Bulkhead's box about an hour and a half after the battle, the nav core an hour and a half after that, and so on into the evening, each with a parts crate. Fight the fire, patch the breach or mend a part yourself to have it sooner. In test games a battle won took the parts crates from 12 to 3 or 4, and one lost to 1 or 2, so whatever wears out next waits for parts until you buy or find some.
- **The kettle.** A dark galley cooks nothing, but the kettle is full in the morning and the galley has its power back long before the evening sittings, so it only runs dry if the box stays broken past six in the evening, for instance with no parts crates left to mend it. (After a battle the last sitting of the day can come up a little short anyway, about two in a hundred.)
- **The Medbay has a junction box of its own** now, on its wall. Before, it ran off the Names Bulkhead's, so a battle would have left the wounded in the dark with the air going bad.
- **Looked at,** a part shot up in the fighting says so, and who'll mend it with what.
- **After the mutiny, in the corner.** When the battle's over, the quest tracker in the top left shows **After the mutiny**: what the battle left behind, one box each, with where to go for the fire and the breach. It ticks itself off from how the ship stands, in any order, with a line each time ("After the mutiny: fire out (1 of 7)"): **Fire out**, **Nobody left where they fell**, **The breach patched**, **Power back in the Galley**, **The nav core mended**, **The prisoners settled**, and **The posts filled** if any officer went down. **The dead named** goes on it once the first of them dies, and is ticked when the dead have been carried to where they're laid out and their names are on the bulkhead. Something ticked stays ticked. Once it's all ticked, a line says so and the list leaves the corner; the quest log (J) keeps it, under Finished. No scene pushes you from one box to the next: the ship mends itself around you, and the asks below are how you have your say.
- **The officers' asks,** through the same asks as everything else aboard (the amber **!**):
  - **The prisoners:** right after the battle, Lieutenant Tarrow (or whoever is chief of security) asks what's to be done with them: **Keep them locked up**; **Put them to work under guard** (morale +2 and the council's confidence +3 at once); or **Put them ashore at the next port**, when the Meridian next docks somewhere with a market, where they go off the ship (so fewer aboard to feed) and morale rises by 2. The brig's line in the Command menu says which.
  - **The damage report:** right after the battle, Chief Marrow (or whoever is chief engineer) brings you what's still to mend, in the order his people are going, and asks whether it should go another way: **Power first** as it is, **The bridge first** (navigation and comms), or **The alarms and the cameras first**. His people keep their pace; only the order changes. (The plan had Commander Voss give it, but the chief engineer isn't in the fight, so he's on his feet to ask.) It's no longer needed once everything's mended.
  - **Their names:** once someone has died, Speaker Halm asks you to say the names of the dead at the Names Bulkhead, where the crew chalk them. **Say their names** is morale +3 and goes on the record. He asks once.
  - **A post with nobody at it:** while an officer is down or laid up, Commander Voss (or whoever is first officer) asks whether to give their post to whoever's best fit for it. It's given for good, so once they're on their feet they can ask for it back; **Leave it until they're back** lets it wait.
- **Not here yet:** the tuning: how many mutineers, how much breaks, how long help takes, once you've played it.

Walking, people's lives, the wider world, fights, skill checks, the ship and the menus all work as usual.

- **People:** walk up to anyone and they speak first. The Meridian's crew each have a written conversation of their own (`world/scenes/talk_<id>.txt`), with things to say that lead on, questions that go away once asked, and answers that change with what the story has done. Everyone else has stock lines, made from who they are, what they're doing and where you are, plus what they've heard.
- **Things and doors:** **Use** on a thing gives its written result or picks it up, and **Ask to go through** at a closed door follows that door's rule: some open when asked, some need something you've earned, and some open a skill check whose result decides. All 15 doors have a rule, and the bridge's things have results, with a plain answer anywhere that hasn't been written yet.
- **Writing it yourself:** open a file in `world/scenes/` with Notepad, change it and save. The game reads it again the next time the story gets there, with no restart. `world/scenes/_how-to-write-a-scene.txt` explains the format: parts, choices, checks, fights, what a part changes, and conditions such as `? has data_chip` or `? skill persuasion >= 3` that decide which choices are offered and where the story goes. A choice can lead to a check whose pass and fail go different ways, a part can remember a flag for any later scene, a part can wait for the Meridian to arrive somewhere, and a story can fill her tank, change her stores or move a story on to its next act.
- **Finding mistakes:** run `npm run check-story` in the game's folder. It lists every problem with the line it's on, in plain words, and changes nothing. A file the game can't read is explained in the game too.

**The AI game master is gone.** Until 10 November 2026 the game could also have an AI game master, which wrote whatever wasn't written and answered whatever you typed. It was taken out, and the game runs on its own systems now. A game it was running opens as a written game: a line in the story says so, its last choices stop being offered, and a written scene that was under way carries on. Everything else in the save, the journal, the quests, the people you know and the record, stays as it was. Nothing goes online, and nothing costs anything to play.

## The story board

**Story board**, under **The story** on the rail, shows any of the 108 stories, 24 places, 61 people, 6 species, 34 enemies, 64 items, 12 factions, 53 perks, 6 computers, the systems of every place and the ship's logistics as boxes and arrows, and lets you change it by clicking. Pick what to look at and which one at the top. On **The story**, the boxes are the lead, what's really going on, the beats, the ways through, the checks and fights, the rewards, and the endings by name. Click an ending and the facts it leaves, what follows days later, and the other stories that react to it unfold beside it. What on the record changes this story sits on the left, and the clock's stages along the bottom. Click any box and its words open on the right, with **Keep** and **Undo**; names are made from the words, conditions are picked from lists, and the buttons add an ending, a fact, a consequence, a stage of the clock, or a reaction. A stage's or a consequence's changes at a place and aboard the ship are picked there too. Drag a box to move it, and the board remembers where you left it next time; **Tidy up** puts everything back in its columns. Drop a fact or a consequence on an ending to link them. Drag the dark background to look around, and use the **−**, **+** and **Fit** buttons for the size.

**The written scene** shows a story's scene file, when it has one: each box a part, each arrow a choice, a check's two ways, or a rule. Click a part and its lines open as they are in the file, with the words on its arrows; click an arrow to change where a choice goes, drop its words on another part to send it there, or drop a part on another part to give it a choice that leads there. **As the file** shows the same story written the way its file holds it.

**A place** shows what the player sees, the feel of it, its districts, what can happen there, and the people and stories here, each a box you can open. **A person** shows how the game sees them, their looks, personality, voice, goals and secrets, their numbers and how they fight, with boxes with arrows for every number and nothing to type, and where they belong: their place, their species, their stories, and **The written lines** tab when they have a talk written out. **A species** shows what the player reads, the notes on how they play, their numbers and effects, and the people who are one. Its **Their bodies** box opens how often each part is hit and what its people do differently, with boxes and lists to change it. **An enemy** shows what the player sees, what they say in a fight, one box of lines per moment, who they fight for and where they turn up, with their numbers in the box on the left. **An item** shows what the player reads, its effects, and where it's sold or found, with its type, price and numbers picked from lists. **A faction** shows what the player reads, what they believe, and their people, places, enemies and stories. **A perk** shows what the player reads, what it changes, with every number in a box with arrows, and the perks it needs or leads to. **A computer** shows what the player sees when they sit down, its files, letters, logs and programs, each a box with buttons to add more, and the computers it reaches over its network, with its class, place and login picked in the box on the left. **The systems** of a place show who keeps it running, with its guards and engineers picked from its people, each system with its parts, where you add a part by picking it and a room from lists, each computer with arrows to what it runs, and the rooms with what's in each. **The logistics** of the ship show each store with what it holds, what it wants and who carries for it, with an arrow from every store that could supply it, and the machines, loads and refills as boxes, each with arrows to the stores its words name; a store's line is written with its room, what it holds, what it wants and its carriers picked from lists, a machine's, a load's or a refill's line in the words the folder's how-to explains, with the checker's complaint on the box when it can't be read.

**Save** writes the file in `world/stories`, `world/scenes`, `world/characters`, `world/places`, `world/species`, `world/enemies`, `world/items`, `world/factions`, `world/perks`, `world/computers`, `world/systems` or `world/logistics`, through the same reader the game uses, so a file that can't be read is never written, and the game uses the new version from the next turn. The checker's problems are marked in red on the boxes and listed on the right. Nothing is saved until you press Save, and Close asks again if something isn't. A changed person, item or faction reaches the codex when the game next starts.

**Health** shows the wounds file and each place's medbay, and saves them in `world/health`. **Wounds and old marks** has the lines at the top that say how blood, healing, walking and pain go, one box each; every wound and old mark; the weapons, with arrows to the hits each one makes; the hits and the accidents, with arrows to the wounds they leave; and the species, each a box that opens its own board. **CFS Meridian** has the medbay's lines (its room, its cots, who answers a call, who treats, the medicine, the doctor's rounds and the rest), each treatment, each kind of mending, and who starts the game hurt. Click a box and its line opens on the right, with how it's written; **Keep** won't take a line the game can't read, and says why.

**Requests** shows the requests people make (`world/scenes/request_<kind>.txt`). **Every request** comes first: each kind as a box, in a column for the lives it is for, with who asks, what is wanted and how long they give, and orders in their own colour; click one and **Open it**. One request has its lines on top down the left (For, As, When, About, Asks, Needs, By, Gone when, Handed to, with the ones not written dimmed), what they ask, an arrow for each answer the player can give, and how it ends: done, failed or no longer needed. Click a line on top to change it; For, As, About and By have buttons to pick from, so nothing needs typing, then **Keep** and **Save**. Click a part to change its lines. **Every part** shows the file the way a written scene is shown.

**Ports** shows one port at a time (`world/ports/<place>.txt`): a box for each good it holds, with how many it usually has, where it sends for more and how many go through it in a day; a box for each place something comes out of the ground there, with an arrow to what it gives; a box for each of its makers, with what goes into a day's making, an arrow to what it makes and one to it from each thing it's made from; a box for each line of what it uses; a box for each of its stores in its rooms, with an arrow from each good it keeps; the ports it sends to on the left and the ports that send to it on the right, each a box that opens that port. The port's own box has who carries for its stores and where goods come and go, picked from the people and the rooms of the place. Click a box to change its line in words, with buttons for the ports and the goods so that no name needs typing, then **Keep** and **Save**. **Goods** shows a tree at a time (`world/goods/<tree>.txt`): each good at its step, from the ground on the left to the advanced good on the right, with an arrow to what's made from it, the ports that make it or have ground for it, and how many are used, go into other things and are made a day across the map; the tree's own box has what hands, the sick and power need. A line the game can't follow isn't kept, and what the checker says of the files (a good that isn't there, a port that holds none, something used faster than it's made) is on the board.

## Drawing the ship yourself

**Map editor**, under **Game** in the menu, opens the ship's decks seen from straight above, to redraw with the mouse. Nothing is typed but names and words. The game's clock is stopped while it's open.

- **The deck** is in the middle, a small rectangle to a square: dark for floor, grey for walls, amber for doors, teal for consoles, brown for crates and lockers, green for plant beds. The three decks are along the top: **Deck 1: Command**, **Deck 2: The Civilian Deck** and **Deck 3: Engineering**. **+** and **-** make it bigger and smaller.
- **Paint** is on the left. Pick what to paint (**Floor**, **Wall**, **Door**, **Console**, **Crate**, **Locker**, **Bench**, **Plant bed**, **Lamp**, or **Open space** for outside the hull) and how: **Brush** paints square by square while you hold the mouse button down, **Block** fills a block you drag out, and **Room** makes walls all round with floor inside. A room made that way needs a door: paint a **Door** into one of its walls.
- **Rooms** are the dashed outlines with a name on them. They're how the game knows the Bridge from the passage outside it. **A room's outline** lets you drag a rectangle round part of the deck, and then you give it a name and a few words on what you see there. With **Choose and move**, click a room to choose it, drag it to move it, and drag its white corner to make it bigger or smaller.
- **Things** are the gold diamonds: what you can look at and use in a room. **A thing**, then a click on a square, puts one down, and on the right you give it a name, what **Look** says, and what you do with it. **Take it away** removes it.
- **Furniture** is on the left too: a chair, the command chair, a table, a long table, a desk, a bunk, a cot, a locker, shelves, a plot table, a screen on a stand, a kettle, a barrel, a potted plant, a basin, and for walls a screen or a window. Pick one and click a square to put it down there, and click more squares for more of the same: a row of chairs is a row of clicks. A seat put beside a console or a table turns to face it. With **Choose and move**, click a piece to choose it and drag it to move it; on the right, **Turn it** turns it a quarter round to its right, and **Take it away** removes it. On the deck, tan is furniture that's in the way, blue is a seat or a bed, and the bright edge is the side it faces. A window or a screen goes on a wall square and shows on whichever side of the wall the room is on. **Check** says if a piece is standing on a wall, on top of another, or on someone's post.
- **From the packs**, just above the furniture, is for the pieces of the two bought pixel-art packs. **Choose a piece...** opens the Pieces window showing only what's on the ship, and only what can stand in a room (the packs' floors, walls, doors and stairs come at a later step). Click one and the window shuts with that piece chosen; then click a square of the deck to put it down, or a wall for something that hangs on one, and more squares for more of the same. The pieces chosen lately stay listed there, a small picture and a name each, to pick again with one click. Once it's down, a piece of the packs is moved, turned and taken away like any other furniture. In the room it's drawn from the packs' own pictures in the **Pixel art** view, and as a plain shape of its size in **Isometric** and **Top-down**. What each piece is to the game is on its line in `world/furniture.txt`: a seat (someone who stops there sits in it), a table, something that stands on the floor, something to open (like a locker, shelves, a desk or a barrel, and it has things in it as those do), something that hangs on a wall, something that lies on the floor (a carpet: people walk over it and stand on it), or a small thing (a mug, a laptop, a games console: on a table's square it sits on the table, and nobody has to walk round it). Tables, machines and cabinets are in the way. The packs' beds are only in the way for now: nobody lies in them yet.
- **What a thing is drawn as** is under its name on the right: **Drawn in the room as**. Left as it is, a thing is drawn as what its name says (the captain's chair as the command chair) or as a box, and you can pick any piece of furniture, or **A plain box**. **Turn it** turns it like furniture.
- **People, stairs and the start flag** can be dragged to another square. The pink circles are where the officers stand at their posts, the teal arrows are the stairs, and the white square is where someone arriving on the deck stands. They can't be added or taken away here, and the stairs go on leading where they led.
- **Words on a wall** and **A name on the deck** put writing down where you click, a letter to a square.
- **Where people stand**, under **Show**, shows a purple dot for each spot someone's day sends them to: a bunk, a place at the benches, a station. Drag one to move it. A spot or a post on a seat or a bed (blue) is where they'll sit or lie. If you build a wall over one, it's moved to the nearest floor when you save, and the save says so.
- **The picture** under the plan is the room as the game will draw it, in the bought packs' pictures whatever view the game itself is in: floors, walls, the doors between rooms shut, the furniture, and someone standing at each post. It's drawn again a moment after every change (a piece put down, turned, dragged or taken away, a wall painted, **Undo**), before anything is saved, so you see what a change looks like before you keep it. It looks at the square you last clicked on the plan; a click in the picture itself looks there instead, and shows that square on the plan for a moment; and a pale outline in the picture follows the mouse over the plan, so you can see where a piece will land. **Picture**, along the top, puts it away and brings it back, and the editor remembers which you left it at. It shows what the deck's own file has: the power boxes, cameras, keypads, stores and whatever else the game stands in rooms by itself aren't in it, and nothing in it is dark, locked or worn. A piece of the packs used for the first time can show as a plain shape for a moment, until its picture has been fetched.
- **Undo** takes the last change back, and **Redo** puts it back again.
- **Check** tries the deck out against the rest of the game without saving, and lists what would go wrong, in plain words: "Nobody can get to 14 squares of floor in the Strongroom: there's no doorway to it.", or that a room the ship's systems lock has no door left. Click a line to see where.
- **Save** checks the same way first. If something's wrong it isn't saved, and **Save it anyway** keeps it all the same. Once it's saved, the room behind the window is the new one straight away, in a game that's already being played too. The deck as it was before is kept in `data/map-backups`, so nothing's lost by trying.
- **Pieces**, along the top, opens every picture in the two bought pixel-art packs: about 1,350 of them, gathered into 500 pieces (a chair's four sides are one piece, and so are a door's frames as it opens) and sorted by where each belongs on a ship: **Bridge and command**, **Quarters**, **Mess and galley**, **Medbay**, **Engineering and workshop**, **Stores and cargo**, **Heads and laundry**, **Passages, doors and stairs**, **Garden**, **Walls and what hangs on them**, **Floors**, **Small things**, and **Odds and ends**. Each piece has a tick, **[x]** for on the ship and **[ ]** for left out, and a click on the piece changes it. The first ticks are the game's guess: steel, screens, pipes and plain working things are on, and what belongs in a house (wooden beds, bright paint, a rubber duck, game consoles) is off. The **+** in a piece's corner lays it out along the bottom with every picture it has and a row of the places, to move it under another one. **All on** and **All off** tick or untick everything shown under a place, **On the ship** and **Left out** show only those, **Space Station** and **Interiors** show one pack, and **Undo** takes the last change back. Everything is saved as you click, in `world/pieces.txt`, a list in plain words that can be changed by hand as well. Laid out with its **+**, a piece also says what it is once it stands on a deck, as rows of buttons to change it: what kind of thing it is, what it opens like if it opens, how many squares it takes across and deep, and whether people walk round it. The first answers are a guess, from the piece's name and from measuring its picture, so some will be wrong; a click puts one right, **Undo** takes it back, and it's kept in `world/furniture.txt`, another list in plain words. A deck that's already loaded goes on treating the piece as it did until the deck is saved or the game is opened again. A tick changes nothing on the decks yet: it's the list the rework of the ship's map will build from, the first step of its roadmap. The pictures are only on this computer, so anywhere else the window says the packs aren't there.
- **What it doesn't do yet:** the other places (it's the ship's decks only), a new deck or a deck of another size, and adding or removing people or stairs. The ship's power boxes, vents, cameras, stores and cots aren't shown: the game stands those in each room itself, and moves them if you build where they were.

The decks can still be drawn in Tiled, as below; both change the same files.

## Drawing the ship in Tiled

The Meridian is drawn in **Tiled**, a free map editor, one file to a deck, and you can change the decks there or add rooms to them. Each deck is one continuous map with its rooms drawn on it, so you walk from the bridge to the names bulkhead and on down the deck without anything loading or jumping, and the stairs take you between decks. The three decks are laid out after the Anvil Carrack's arrangement, command on top, the civilian deck under it and engineering along the keel, as a starting point to redraw. Tiled shows a deck from the same angle as the Isometric view, built of plain stand-in blocks: grey walls, amber doors, teal consoles, brown crates, a green figure for each person, a gold diamond for each thing you can look at, a teal arrow for each stair, and a flag where you stand when the story puts you in a room. The game still draws its own picture. The blocks are only there so you can see what's where.

- **Getting Tiled:** it's free from [mapeditor.org](https://www.mapeditor.org). Its itch.io page asks for a price, and **No thanks, just take me to the downloads** gets it for nothing.
- **Opening the ship:** in Tiled, choose **File > Open File or Project** and open `world/maps/almagest-explorers.tiled-project`. The ship's decks are listed on the left, `deck1.tmj`, `deck2.tmj` and `deck3.tmj`. Double-click one.
- **A tile is a square of floor** the way the Isometric view draws it. The game's own squares are half as wide, so some tiles have something on one half only, such as a thin wall or a narrow door. A map can be 100 tiles across and 60 down.
- **The layers:** **Floor**; **Walls and things**, for walls, doors, consoles, crates and anything else in the way, where an amber door is a doorway between two rooms; **People and things**; **Words**, for words on the walls and the names painted on the floor; and **Rooms**, a rectangle round each room.
- **Drawing:** click the layer, pick a tile from **Stand-ins**, choose the **Stamp Brush**, and click or drag. The **Eraser** takes tiles away. Clicking a tile in the tileset shows what it is, under **what**.
- **The rooms:** each is a rectangle on the **Rooms** layer with its **Class** set to **Room**, drawn round the room, walls included. Its **Name** is what the room's called, and has to be its own; **id** is what the game's files call it, such as `bridge`, which the stories, everyone's days and the ship's systems go by; **desc** is what you read about it, and **aliases** its other names. Who's in a room, what's in it, what the story is waiting on there and whether its lights and air are on are all worked out from the rectangles, so dragging a person into another rectangle moves them rooms. A square in no rectangle belongs to the deck itself, which is fine for a passage.
- **People, things and stairs:** drag them with **Select Objects**, and each stands on the square under its feet. To add one, pick its marker in the tileset, choose **Insert Tile**, and click where it stands. Its properties say who or what it is: a person's **person** is the name of their file in `world/characters`; a thing has its **Name**, what you read when you **look**, and what you do when you **use** it; a way out's **to** is the deck it leads to, such as `deck2`, and you come out beside its way back. A flag inside a room is where the story puts you in it.
- **Furniture:** a layer called **Furniture**, once a deck has any, with a point for each piece on its back left square, its **Class** set to **Furniture**, its **Name** what it is (`chair`, `command-chair`, `table`, `long-table`, `desk`, `bunk`, `cot`, `locker`, `shelf`, `plot-table`, `screen`, `kettle`, `barrel`, `plant`, `basin`, `wall-screen`, `window`), and a **facing** of `n`, `e`, `s` or `w`. The Map editor is the easier way to put it down. A thing's **looks** property says what it's drawn as, and its **facing** which way.
- **The deck itself:** **Map > Map Properties** has its name, what you read about it, and its colours.
- **Saving:** **Ctrl+S**, or **File > Save**. The game notices within a second or two, even while you're playing, and draws the deck again the next time the clock moves or you take a step. If a wall lands where you're standing, you're moved to the nearest floor.
- **A new room** is a rectangle on a deck: carve its walls and floor, put a door in, draw the rectangle round it on the **Rooms** layer and name it. **A new deck:** open a deck, choose **File > Save As**, save it in the same folder under the new deck's name, change it, and give another deck a way out to it.
- **If something's wrong,** such as a person with no **person**, a way out to a deck that isn't there, or two rooms with the same name, the game leaves that one thing out and draws the rest, and `npm run check-story` lists it in plain words.
- **Still in `meridian.json`:** the ship's name and the room it starts in. The deck plan, from the button in **What's here**, is made from the decks, one tab to a deck.
- **The other places** still have their rooms in their own files, drawn in characters, one room to a level, and a Tiled file with no **Rooms** layer is one room the same way. Any of them can move into Tiled exactly as they are, with `node tools/to-tiled.mjs` and the place's name, and be joined into decks from there.

`world/maps/_how-to-draw-rooms-in-tiled.txt` explains all of it, step by step.

## Changing the game

- `world/` holds the characters, enemies, places, stories and game text, as described under [Your files](#your-files). These are the safest files to edit.
- `src/data.js` holds the fight abilities, the skills' checks and the story background. Restart the game after you change it.
- `src/character-data.js` holds the skills, origins, traits, beliefs and ready-made characters. The species are files in `world/species`.
- `src/life-data.js` holds the careers and everything that can happen in the life simulation.
- `src/lore.js` holds the entries of **What you know**. Only put what your character would know in there, because players can read it.
- `src/creator.js` works out a character from the choices made in the creator, and `src/role.js` works out who that makes them in the world.
- `src/rules.js` holds the rules: dice, leveling, stress and beliefs.
- `src/combat.js` holds the rules for fights, including talking in them, and `public/fight.js` draws the fight window. `src/live-fight.js` plays a fight in the room in real time on top of those rules (where everyone stands, walking, lines of fire, cover, being ready), and `public/live-fight.js` draws it over the isometric room, with the orders along the bottom.
- `src/ship.js` runs the Meridian's day: her stores, her hull, her people's health and morale, the officers' decisions, and whoever has her chair when the player doesn't. `public/ship.js` draws the Ship menu and `public/map.js` the star map.
- `src/logistics.js` puts each place's stores in its rooms from `world/logistics/`, full to start with, runs its machines, its generator, its refills and the ship's sittings on the clock, takes what the engineers' repairs use up from its stores (what's broken is the systems' own, in `src/systems.js`, and who mends it is the work board's), and counts it all for the Ship menu, whose food, medicine and parts are these stores and nothing else. Who carries between the stores, and when, is the work board's, in the next file. The generator's supply against the loads is settled in `src/systems.js`, which lets systems go in the written order and treats a stopped generator as no power anywhere. `public/logistics-text.js` reads and writes the files.
- `src/work.js` is the ship's work board, the only part of the game that sends anyone to carry between the stores or to mend what's broken. Each kind of work has its own people, and all of it comes in one order of urgency, the Urgency part of the place's file (`model.urgency`): one person, one job, the most urgent first, and someone at work drops it only for a job two lines higher or more (`handFor`). For carrying, it reads what each store wants and who carries for it (compiled by `src/logistics.js` from the store's own line), raises a job whenever a store has fewer than it wants and another can spare it, picks the nearest such store and sets aside what's been gone for, gives urgent wants out first (a store under its mark, or a machine, the medbay or a repair asking), and turns the carrier aside from their day the way the systems do, so the life engine walks them there and back. For repairs, it asks the systems what's due within the hour (`repairsDue`), gives each to the first engineer who's free, lets the systems walk them to the spare and the part (`repairHands`, read by `calledNow`), and finishes the repair when their hour is up (`finishRepair`); where a place has no logistics file, the systems still mend by the clock alone (`boardRuns`). It asks the medbay, the systems (`alarmDetour`) and the rooms' hooks who's already taken by something outside it, hands a job back when whoever's on it is called away, with whatever they'd loaded, and times a walk by the way through the decks (`walkMinutes` in `src/life.js`). It also keeps the officers' orders (what the first officer has put first, what the quartermaster has made each store want), the jobs the player has taken with **I'll do it** (`workCommand`, through `/api/work`), and makes the Work page's view (`boardView`), which `public/work.js` draws. The work the medbay and the workshop keep their own records of (who's answering a call, who's treating whom, what's on the bench) it reads as jobs like its own (`medbayWork`, `workshopWork`), so whoever's on one is on a job and can be taken off it for something clearly more urgent; it chooses who answers a call (`callHand`), and tells the medics and the menders who's on other work (`busyOnBoard`). Fires and breaches are jobs of its own (`troubleAsks`, `tendTroubles`): it sends an engineer to each, `src/emergencies.js` asks it who's got there (`troubleCrew`) before a fire gives ground, and it patches a breach when its time has come (`patchBreach`); the guards answering an alarm it reads from the systems (`alarmWork`). A job that's asking for a hand or for goods and getting neither is kept in `game.logistics[place].waiting` (since when, and which of the two), and after the file's `left waiting` time `workTroubles` hands it to the requests (`src/requests.js`) as a trouble whoever holds the post its `who asks` line names can ask about: `takeJob` and `leaveJob` hold a job for the player who said yes, `jobOver` and `jobDeed` say when it's over and whether they had a hand in it, and `jobWords` gives the officer's words. `src/work-hook.js` reads the story's words about the board (`? work`, `* work`), and imports nothing so that the scenes' reader can use it; the board tells it how each is answered and played (`workHolds`, `storyWork`), and does a story's job by the player's own hands as a room act (`work-story`). It's steps 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6 of the logistics rework, and what it keeps in the save is `game.logistics[place].jobs`, `.orders` and `.waiting`.
- `src/worth.js` says what goods are worth to the ship, from the `feeds`, `doses` and `spares` lines of their item files: so many sittings at the kettle, so many doses in the medbay, so many repairs. Whatever uses a kind of goods takes anything worth some of it: the galley's "any food" choice (`pickTake` in `src/logistics.js`), the medbay's `openMedicine`, a repair's spares (`takeSpares`), the Ship menu's counts (`aboardCount` in `src/ship.js`, with `worthAboard` and `usedFrom`), the asks (`broughtOf` in `src/requests.js`) and the inventory's line for a thing (`worthLine`). It's step 2 of the logistics rework.
- `src/command.js` reads the chain of command from `world/command/`, keeps who holds each post and who does which job in the save, and answers who holds a post or does a job for the council, the systems, the machines, the stores' carriers and the chair. It gives everyone on the watch bill their job's day, which `src/life.js` walks, lets the captain move people, and has the first officer cover for anyone lost. It also runs the officers' reviews: the warnings, relieving and replacing, what people remember, the ambitious asking for a post, and the captain's recommendations, choices and hold; and loyalty, the council's confidence, the mutiny and the brig, with the written scenes it starts; and it answers what a scene asks of the chain of command, plays what a scene changes in it, and puts what happens on the record. `public/command.js` draws the Command menu, and `public/command-text.js` reads and writes the files.
- `src/tiled.js` reads the rooms drawn in Tiled, both of its kinds of file, into the same rooms as the ones drawn in characters, and writes a room out for Tiled. `tools/to-tiled.mjs` moves a place's rooms into Tiled, with the stand-in tiles they need, and `tools/stand-in-art.mjs` draws those tiles' pictures.
- `src/walk.js` runs walking around, the levels, where you are, and talks. `public/walk.js` draws the levels, the **What's here** pane floating over the room, the clock's buttons and the map of each place, `public/move.js` walks the player freely, with the keys or along a click's way, and tells the server each new square, `public/iso.js` draws the isometric view, `public/tiles.js` draws the top-down test view from the tilesets in `public/art/`, `public/pixel-art.js` draws the pixel-art test's floors, walls, doors and furniture from the two packs in `licensed/` (served by `server.js` under `/licensed/`, and kept out of git), `src/pieces.js` gathers those packs' pictures into the catalogue of pieces that the Map editor's **Pieces** window shows (`public/pieces.js`), with the ticks kept in `world/pieces.txt`, `src/pack-furniture.js` says what each of those pieces is as furniture, from `world/furniture.txt`, and adds them to the game's own in `public/furniture.js` (it first guessed them, measuring their pictures with `src/png.js`), `public/popups.js` draws the story over the room, with `public/scene.js` cutting each scene into pieces, `public/writer.js` writing people's words out and `public/pane.js` drawing the pane by someone's head and a thing's window beside it and placing it with its line, `public/float.js` the check and computer windows' lines to what they're about, `public/pull.js` the pulling aside and sliding back that the panes and those windows share, and `public/talk.js` draws a talk, on a pane by the person's head or in its own window.
- `src/life.js` runs everyone's days, and how they get around. `src/daily.js` runs each place's supplies, prices and factions, day by day, and `src/ports.js` is the real amounts behind the supplies: what each port holds, makes, uses and sends for, what comes out of its ground, what its makers take and the hands and the power they need, the hauls on their way (`game.world.hauls`), what the world's events do to them, and the line that says why a port is short. A supply's level stays where it was (`game.world.places[id].goods`), as the share of what the port usually holds, so that whatever reads or sets a level reads or sets the amount. `src/trade.js` is the ship's purse, her restocking at a dock, the slate and her passengers, and `src/freight.js` is her freight: what a dock offers from what it can spare, the loads in her hold, buying and selling by the load, and the prices the Market lists (`public/freight.js` draws them). `src/story-clock.js` moves the stories on when nobody has stepped in. None of the others has any screens.
- `src/checks.js` runs the skill check windows, `src/check-games/` has the game behind each one, and `public/check.js` draws them.
- `src/computers.js` knows where the computers are and how the player gets in, `src/computer-use.js` runs one once they're on it, and `public/computer.js` draws the window. `src/charts.js` is the chart program on the plot table (what a jump costs the ship, and Engage), and `public/navplot.js` draws it.
- `src/programs.js` reads the rig's programs from `world/programs/`, `src/rig.js` keeps the rig (what it has and what's loaded), writes a program at the bench, copies one off a computer, says why one won't run, and does what each one does, with what's left behind on the clock; `public/rig.js` draws the Rig page.
- `src/rig-parts.js` reads the rig's boards, casings and parts from `world/rig-parts/` and adds a build up (its memory, power, speed, quiet and heat, the sinks' cooling, worn and broken parts, and why it won't start), with the bench's rules and the heat a session builds; `src/rig.js` keeps the build in the save and does the bench's jobs.
- `src/systems.js` runs each place's systems: where their parts go, what's working, the alarm, the air, repairs, and what they do to walking, checks, people's days and jumps. `public/systems-text.js` reads and writes the files.
- `src/story-library.js` decides which leads and rumors you're offered, and keeps track of the stories taken up and the main story's act.

- `src/person-sheet.js` makes anyone's registry printout for the right-click menu's **Registry**, and `public/sheet.js` prints it, and your own.
- `src/body.js` makes up everyone's body from the game's own seed, their species, age, job, past and file, and says how it looks, and keeps their wounds and blood on the clock. `src/medbay.js` keeps who's down, sends whoever answers the call to carry them to a cot, has the medics treat them with the cabinet's medicine, carries the dead away, and lets the player carry someone. `src/death.js` is what a death does, and the names. `src/workshop.js` is the Repair Bay: mending synthetics and building artificial limbs. `src/register.js` is the register of everyone aboard without a day of their own, the illnesses that go round, the doctor's rounds, and the records terminal's list. `src/story-health.js` is what the written story asks of someone's body and does to it. `src/room-hooks.js` lets a part of the game add things to do to a person, a thing or a room without the room's own code knowing it, and `src/hands.js` is the crew's jobs done by hand. `src/uses.js` gives the things in the rooms something to do, from `world/uses.txt`. `src/asks.js` is what anyone without written lines answers, and walking with you or going to the medbay when asked, from `world/asks.txt`. `src/emergencies.js` is the fires, hull breaches and brawls, from `world/emergencies.txt`. `src/law.js` is whose the stores, the closed rooms and the containers are, who sees, and what being seen taking costs, from `world/law`. `src/keepsakes.js` is whose a keepsake in someone's container is, and what's missed, for the request that asks after it; `src/making.js` is the bench that makes parts crates from salvage. `src/trade.js` is the ship's purse, restocking from the dock, cargo and passengers, from `world/trade.txt`. `src/salvage.js` is the run out to a wreck from the Graveyard, from `world/salvage.txt`. `src/wrecks.js` is the runs out to the wrecks the rig's parts come from, from `world/wrecks/`; ## The app

The app is a window around the same game. It starts the same local server that `start-game.bat` does and shows the same page, so nothing about the game itself is different.

- **Changing the game:** on this computer, the app runs the game from this folder, so a change here shows up the next time you close the app and open it again. There's nothing to rebuild.
- **Changing the app itself:** everything in `app/` is built into the app, so a change there needs a new build. Run these two, then run `dist/Almagest-Explorers-Setup.exe`, which updates the app in place and leaves your saves alone:

```bash
npm install
```

```bash
npm run build-app
```

- **Giving it to someone else:** send them `dist/Almagest-Explorers-Setup.exe`. It holds a copy of the game as it was when it was built, without your saves. On a computer that doesn't have this folder, the app runs that copy, and keeps its saves in `%APPDATA%\Almagest Explorer's\data`. Windows warns about installers from unknown publishers unless you pay for a signing certificate.
- **A second copy beside it:** `node app/build.js --copy` makes `dist/copy/Almagest-Explorers-Copy-Setup.exe`, which installs a second app, **Almagest Explorer's (copy)**, with an icon of its own. It plays the game from the folder it was made in, with that folder's saves, the way the first app plays this one, so a copy of the game downloaded from GitHub becomes a game that stays as it was while this folder goes on changing. Its settings are its own, in `%APPDATA%\Almagest Explorer's (copy)\`, and so is its port, 3220, so it never plays this folder or shows the first app's game, and the two can be open at once. Installing it leaves the first app as it is, and it's uninstalled on its own. A folder without the bought art can be told where the art is with `--licensed=<folder>`. Don't start that folder's `start-game.bat` while its app is open, because both would save into the same folder.
- **Moving this folder:** the app asks where it went the next time it opens, because your saves are in it.
- **What the app keeps for itself:** `%APPDATA%\Almagest Explorer's\` has `game.log`, which is what the black window would have shown, and `app-state.json`, which says where the window was and where this folder is. The game was called The Last Meridian until version 0.5.0, and an app installed under that name kept these in `%APPDATA%\The Last Meridian\`, with the saves of anyone without this folder: the first run under the new name copies them across, and leaves the old folder as it was.
- **Uninstalling it:** use **Installed apps** in Windows Settings. That only removes the app, and this folder and your saves stay where they are.

`app/main.js` is the app, `app/choices.js` has the decisions it makes when it starts and what its installers are made with, `app/build.js` makes them, and `app/icon.ico` is its icon.

## The Godot view

This is the game's room in **Godot**, a program for making games. What you see and click is being moved there a step at a time, while the game's rules stay exactly as they are. Two of seven steps are built. The first drew the ship; the second, this one, connects it to **your game**: it opens the game you're playing, with your captain where you left them, everyone aboard living their day, and the clock running. Walking in it is walking in the game, and it's saved as you go, in the same save.

It doesn't replace anything yet. The game in your browser and the app work as they always did, and **both can be open on the same game at once**: what Godot can't do yet (talking, fights, opening things, the menus) you do in the game's own window, and Godot shows the room while you do.

**To open it**, double-click **`start-godot.bat`** in the game's folder.

- The first time, a black window says it's fetching Godot 4.3, about 55 MB, from Godot's own page on GitHub. It checks what came down, unpacks it into a folder called `godot-engine` beside the game, and never fetches it again. You don't install anything.
- Every time, it takes a few seconds to get ready, and a window opens on a loading screen and then on the room you were last in.
- **If the game is already running** (the black window of `start-game.bat`, or the app), Godot joins it. **If it isn't**, Godot starts the game itself, with no window of its own, and stops it again when you close the Godot window. While it's running that way you can still open http://localhost:3210 in your browser, and you'll find the same game there.
- **A game that was started before this update can't talk to Godot.** If the window says so, close the game (its black window, or the app), and double-click `start-godot.bat` again.
- If Windows asks whether to run a program it doesn't know, that's Godot.

**What you'll see**

- **The room you're in**, in the Pixel art look, whichever deck you're on, or the port you're docked at. Its name and the deck's are along the top.
- **Everyone else**, as the game draws them: walking between their places, sitting in their chairs at the consoles and the mess tables, asleep in the bunks and cots at night, lying where they fell with a red cross over them if they're down. Their name shows when you're near them or point at them, an amber **!** over anyone with something to ask you, and now and then someone says a line over their head.
- **The doors between rooms** slide open for whoever comes near, not only for you. A door the ship's systems have locked is shut and red, with a red lamp blinking over it.
- **The clock** along the top, with **Pause**, **Normal**, **Fast** and **Faster**, the same speeds as in the game. **Space** pauses and goes on.
- **The ship's state:** a room with its lights out is dark; consoles and screens go dark where there's no power; a fire burns with flames, smoke and its own light, and leaves soot afterwards; a hole in the hull has air streaming out of it and an amber lamp beside it; broken parts throw sparks; the keypads, cameras, vents, switches, junction boxes and alarms are on the walls, and a working camera turns; the ship's stores are stacked with as many crates as they hold; the floor has as much litter and grime as the ship is worn.
- **What the game says as it happens** (a fire, the lights going out, someone getting up) comes up in the bottom left corner for a few seconds.

**What to try**

- **Walking:** W, A, S and D or the arrow keys, or click where you want to go. A click on someone walks you up to them; a click on a stair's name, or walking onto its striped plate, takes the stairs.
- **Zoom:** the mouse wheel.
- **Let the clock run on Faster** for a while and watch the watch change: people leave their posts, eat, and go to their bunks.
- **With the game's own window open beside it:** switch a room's lights off from the Ship menu there, or lock a door, and watch the Godot window follow a moment later.
- **The meter** in the bottom left corner says how long the slowest picture of the last second took to draw, in thousandths of a second. On most screens sixty are drawn a second, which is 16.7 each: a number that stays near that while you walk is smooth. Compare how walking feels here and in the game's own Pixel art view.

**What isn't in it yet, and what happens instead**

- **Talking, fights, skill checks and computers.** When one of those is open in the game, a line in amber across the top of the Godot window says so, and you can't walk until it's finished in the game's own window. A click on someone walks you up to them and stops there.
- **Things to click:** the **?** marks, opening a store or a locker, working a part, asking at a closed door. And every menu, the quest tracker, the room's panel, your inventory.
- **Starting a new game.** A new game opens with a written scene and a battle, which Godot doesn't play yet: start it in the game's own window and come to Godot once the battle's over, or use the **Test save**. If no game is open, Godot lists the saves there are, to open one.
- **Which window runs the clock.** With both open, the game's own window runs it, and Godot says "The game's own window is running the clock". Pause it there and Godot's own buttons take over.
- A few things are plainer than in the game for now: the windows, the wall screens, the garden's beds and the hull outside, and screens don't stutter when the ship is short of power.

**If something goes wrong**

- **"The game isn't answering"** means the game was closed while the Godot window was open. Close the Godot window and start it again.
- **If the Godot window is ever ended by force** while it was running the game itself, the game is left running with no window. `start-game.bat` will then say it seems to be running already: opening `start-godot.bat` again and closing its window the usual way stops it, and so does restarting the computer.
- If a line in amber appears above the meter saying something was drawn plain, a picture wasn't found in the `licensed` folder: tell Claude what it says.

**The art** is read from the `licensed` folder each time, as the game reads it, and is never copied into the Godot project.

**To be rid of it**, delete the `godot-engine` folder. Godot also keeps a small folder of its own, `%APPDATA%\AlmagestExplorersGodot`, with a log of what it did, which can be deleted too.

`godot/` is the Godot project, `src/godot-view.js` is what the game tells it about each deck, and `server.js` answers it under `/api/godot/`.

## Carrying on from another computer

The game's code and everything in `world/` are on GitHub, in a private repository. To carry on somewhere else, install [Node.js](https://nodejs.org) and [Git](https://git-scm.com), copy the game down with `git clone` and the address of your repository, and run these two in its folder:

```bash
npm install
```

```bash
npm start
```

Then go to http://localhost:3210. Two things stay on the computer they're on, on purpose:

- **Your saved games:** a new copy starts with none. To bring them along, copy `data/saves` and `data/campaigns` to the same place on the new computer.
- **`backups/` and `dist/`:** the old copies of the game and the installer. Git keeps the game's history from now on, and `npm run build-app` makes a new installer.

`CLAUDE.md` and `docs/notes/` tell whoever works on the game next, including Claude, what you've decided so far and why.
