A sci-fi RPG with a story written in advance, where the game's own code runs the rules, the ship and the world.

## How to play

Double-click **Almagest Explorer's** on your desktop or in the Start menu. The game opens in its own window, and it saves itself after every action. Close the window when you're done.

- **F11** switches to full screen and back.
- **Ctrl** with **+** or **-** makes everything bigger or smaller, and **Ctrl** with **0** puts it back.

You can still play in your browser if you prefer:

1. Double-click **`start-game.bat`**. A black window opens, and so does the game in your browser.
2. Keep the black window open while you play. Close it when you're done.

If the browser doesn't open, go to http://localhost:3210.

Both ways use the same saves. Only one copy of the game can run at a time, so they can't overwrite each other: whichever you open second joins the one that's already running.

Everything can be done by clicking. A new game's story begins by itself. In the views where the story is written out, the prompt under it also takes a choice typed in, part of one, or its number. Over the isometric room there's no typing at all.

The story is written in advance, in files you can read and change: see [The story](#the-story).

## Menus

The buttons down the right edge open the menus, grouped under **You**, **The story**, **The ship**, **Gear** and **Game**. A menu slides out over the story and always takes up the same space: three quarters of the screen. To close one, press **Close** or Esc, click the story behind it, or click its button again. On a narrow window, press **Menu** instead, and the same groups run along the top. Inside a menu, the parts you don't need every time fold away behind a line like **[ + Ship's log ]**: click it to open them, and they stay open while the game's running. Rest the mouse on a button to see what its menu holds.

**Your own registry printout** isn't a menu: click your name at the top left of the screen, or press **C**. It slides down inside the map with who you are, your level, attributes, skills, condition, your body, gear, work and sleep, and a star by your name means there are attribute points to spend on it. Tear it off (or C, Esc) to put it away.

In the views with a prompt under the story, each menu also has a command you can type:

| Command | Menu | What's in it |
|---|---|---|
| `/skills` | Skills | Every skill, your odds at each difficulty, your progress, and practice for each check window |
| `/traits` | Traits | Traits, convictions, and everything currently affecting you |
| `/perks` | Perks | What you've learned, what you can learn now, and what's locked |
| `/beliefs` | Beliefs | Where you stand on each axis, and your standing with each faction |
| `/dossier` | Your past | Your personal record, career and life history |
| `/people` | People | Everyone you've met or heard of |
| `/quests` | Quests | Your quests, what each needs next, what happened in them, and the journal. It's the tracker in the room's corner, grown: **J** opens it too |
| `/leads` | Leads | Stories you could start here, what people are saying, and the stories you're following |
| `/codex` | What you know | Places, factions, ships and ideas you've come across, with a search box |
| `/inv` | Inventory | What you're carrying and wearing, on a grid, in a window over the room. **I** opens it too |
| `/ship` | Ship | The Meridian: her stores, rations, orders, systems and crew, with the way to the star map. If you aren't the captain, how she stands |
| `/map` | Star map | The chart of known space, with Set course, or Book passage if you aren't the captain |
| `/market` | Market | Buying and selling, where there's a market |

`/close` closes the open menu, and `/help` shows this list in the game.

## Your inventory

Press **I**, or **Inventory** under **Gear**, and your inventory opens over the room, the way a thing's window or a skill check does: nothing behind it is dimmed, the clock runs on, a thin line runs from it to you, and you can pull it aside with the mouse and it slides back when you let go. **I** again, **Close** or Esc puts it away. While it's open the keys are its own, so you don't walk. A fight, a skill check, a talk or a computer closes it.

- **On you,** round a figure of yourself: the **Weapon** in your hand, the **Armor** you wear, a **Sidearm** on your hip, which can be any weapon of 2 squares or fewer, and a **Belt** with four places for supplies. The figure is your body: each part is brighter for an old mark, blue if it's artificial, yellow if it's hurt and red if it's badly hurt, and resting the mouse on a part says what's there. How you walk, and your blood if you've lost some, is in the corner of its box. See [Bodies](#bodies).
- **Pockets** hold eight things one square big, and the **Pack** is 8 squares by 5. Everything takes squares by its size: a medkit takes one, a rifle four in a row, plate armor three by three. Supplies stack on one square, up to five medkits or ten ration packs.
- **To move something,** drag it where it goes. Where it would go lights up green if it fits there, and red if it doesn't. Dropped on a stack of its own kind, it joins the stack. Drag a weapon onto your hand or hip, or armor onto you, to put it on, and whatever was there goes back in your bag.
- **Double-click** something to put it on or take it off, to use it if it's a supply, or to take it from something you've opened.
- **Right-click** something for the rest, numbered so a number key picks one: **Put on** or **Take off**, **Use**, **To the belt** or **Off the belt**, **Split the stack**, **Put it down** (or **Put in** whatever's open), and **Look**. Rest the mouse on anything to see what it is, its size and weight, and what it does.
- **The load** is how much you're carrying, against what you can carry: 12 kg, and 3 more for each point of Might. Carry more and you tire twice as fast. Something with no room left is carried in your hands, under the grids, until you make room or put it down, and you can't buy anything that won't fit.
- **Putting something down** leaves it in a pile on the floor where you stand, called **Things you left**, and it's still there if you come back for it. Story items can't be put down.
- **Opening something:** a pile on the floor, or something in a room that holds things, has **Open** in its window and in its right-click menu. What's in it opens in your inventory beside your pack, on a grid of its own, and you drag things between the two, or press **Take everything**. Leaving the room shuts it, and it keeps whatever you left in it.
- **What the people you beat leave:** after a fight you win, what they had on them is left on the floor where you stand, in a pile called **What they left**: now and then a ration pack, a medkit, an energy cell or a stim, and sometimes the weapon they fought with. A machine leaves only what its file says. Walk over and open it.
- **Only rearranging stays out of the story.** Putting something on, taking it off, using it, taking it and putting it down each leave a line in the story, the same as before. Moving things around the bag, splitting a stack or moving supplies to the belt leaves nothing.

## Quests

The quest you're following is in the top left corner of the room: its name, the last thing you did for it, and what it needs now, with where. **J**, **Quests** under **The story**, or a click on it opens the quest log: the same box slides wider and taller, with the quest's steps staying on the left, and your other quests and what happened in them unfolding on the right. **J**, Esc or **Close** slides it back.

- **The story goes on with the person it needs.** When a quest needs you to talk to someone, the tracker says who and where, such as **Talk to Commander Ilya Voss** and **She's at the Bridge.** Walk over and click them, in the room or in **What's here**: the scene plays there, with their words on the pane by their head and your answers hanging off it. While whoever the next part needs is in the same room, it goes straight on. When it needs someone elsewhere, it waits for you there.
- **Doing what it asks:** when a quest needs you somewhere, such as at Kessara, or needs the Star map, its step says so, and the story goes on when you do it.
- **On hold:** walk out of the room in the middle of a scene and that quest is on hold, in amber, with where they are. Nothing in it moves on until you're back with them. Talk to them again, and they say their last words again, with your answers there as before. The clock and the world go on meanwhile.
- **Failed:** if someone a quest is waiting on dies, the quest fails, in red, and can't go on. That will make some stories too easy to lose, and it's to be balanced later. Someone missing or captured holds the quest up instead, until they're back.
- **Several at once:** each quest keeps its place. Taking up a new story leaves the one you were in waiting, and talking to whoever a quest is waiting on picks it up again, whichever quest you're following.
- **The log** groups your quests as **Following**, **Going on**, **On hold**, **Failed** and **Finished**. Clicking one only changes which one's shown. **Follow this one** makes it the quest the tracker follows; until you pick one, it's the main quest. **What happened** has what that quest's scenes said and what you did in them, latest first, with the journal and the **Facts on record** folded under it.
- **Nothing stays along the bottom of the room:** see [The story over the room](#the-story-over-the-room).
- **In a scene file,** a part waits for whoever speaks last in it. A `WITH:` line says someone else, or `WITH: nobody` for a part that plays wherever you are, and `world/scenes/_how-to-write-a-scene.txt` explains it. A story's first part brings its people over, and so does a part for someone who lives at another place or is behind a door the story hasn't opened, since they can't be walked to.

## Fights

In the isometric view, a fight happens in the room you're in, in real time, and it stops whenever you like. A simulated fight, and a fight in any other view, opens in its own window instead, played in rounds.

### A fight in the room

Everyone in the fight stands somewhere in the room: you where you were, someone you went for where they stood, and anyone else coming in by the way into the room furthest from you. Your followers stand beside you. Each fighter has a health bar and, under it, a bar that fills as they get ready to act again. Nobody can act again until that bar is full.

- **It starts stopped.** Nothing moves until you press **Space** or click **Go on**. Press Space again, or click **Stop**, to stop it whenever you like, as often as you like. You can give orders while it's stopped, and they're carried out once it goes on.
- **To shoot or hit someone,** click them in the room, or on their line in the room's panel on the right. You keep going for them whenever you're ready, until they're down or you choose someone else. If you can't reach them from where you are, you walk up to them.
- **To move,** click the floor. You walk there, and you can't act while you're walking.
- **Your orders** are along the bottom, numbered as in the fight window: talking, the belt, your abilities, your sidearm, giving up, **Run for it**. Press a number or click one. Anything that's aimed at someone asks you to click who. What it does and your chances are in its tooltip. It's carried out the moment you're ready.
- **Stop when I'm ready to act**, a box beside the button, stops the fight by itself each time your bar fills, so you can choose your next order calmly. It stays ticked or not for the next fight.
- **Cover** is standing behind something. There's no Take cover order in the room. Someone with a console, a crate or anything else low between them and the shooter is harder to hit, by 3. When you hover over an enemy, the room shows whether you can shoot them from where you stand, and whether they're behind cover. Walls stop shots altogether.
- **Up close or from afar.** Someone with a pistol or a rifle shoots across the room. Someone with a blade, a club or claws has to walk up to you first. Enemies who shoot look for a spot where they can hit you and you can't easily hit them, so moving matters.
- **People who were due to come running partway through** come in by the door when their time comes, about six seconds for each round they'd have waited.
- When it's over, a line says how it went, with **Continue the story** and **Look around first**. You stay where you were standing at the end.

If you open a fight in the room from another view, the fight window says so and has a **Show the room** button.

### The fight window

A fight in the window has your side facing the enemy's, with each fighter drawn in console art beside their health and numbers, and your actions run down the sides of the window with your odds next to them.

The window is only as big as the fight needs: small for a duel, and bigger as followers join and the log fills. If your screen runs out of room, the actions move underneath the fighters and the log shrinks, so the buttons stay in view.

- **To act,** press a number or click an action. Each round then plays out one blow at a time, and you can click to skip ahead. If there are ten things to do, 0 is the tenth.
- **To aim,** click an enemy or use the left and right arrow keys. Your odds change to match that enemy's defenses.
- **Menus** still open during a fight, and slide over its window.
- **Supplies come off your belt.** Only what's on your belt shows as an action in a fight, and each use takes one off it, so put what you want to use there beforehand. Nothing else in the inventory can be changed in the middle of a fight.
- **Your sidearm:** with a weapon on your hip, **Draw your ...** switches to it for the rest of the fight, and **Back to your ...** switches back. Either takes your turn.
- **Difficulty:** **Fights** in the Settings menu goes from Easy to Hard, and applies from your next fight. Your followers stay the same, but enemies change. On Hard, someone who can't fight will usually lose to anything above a minor threat, unless they bring help or run.

**Talking instead of fighting.** Under **Another way** in the fight window, and among the orders along the bottom in a fight in the room, are other things you can do with your turn. In the room, "your turn" is the moment your bar is full, and talking only reaches people within about twelve tiles. Each shows your chance of it working against whoever you're aiming at, and the chance changes as the fight goes on. Hover over one to see the sum.

| Option | What it uses | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Talk them down** | Persuasion, and Presence or whatever suits who you're talking to | They stop fighting and walk away. Their faction likes you a little more |
| **Threaten them**, or **Scare it off** for an animal | Intimidation, and Presence or Might | They back off, and their faction likes you less. How strong you are counts for more here |
| **Pay them off** | Commerce and Presence | They take the money and go, and it's worth half the XP. You only pay if they accept. The price is on the button |
| **Lie to them** | Deception, and Presence or Intellect | They believe you and go. If it fails, they won't believe anything else you say |
| **Shut it down** | Hacking and Intellect | For machines. It stops, but it isn't destroyed |
| **Back away slowly** | Survival, and Endurance or Perception | For animals. It loses interest |

Four things decide how hard it is, and a line under the fighters tells you how each stands for whoever you're aiming at:

- **How much they hate you.** It comes from how their faction feels about you and what you both believe, or from how they feel about you if they're someone you know. It can run from "They like you" to "They want you dead".
- **Why they're fighting.** Someone who's hungry is far easier to talk down than a true believer. Money works on a hired hand and not on someone who's sure they're right, and a forged order is the way past a soldier. Every kind of enemy has its usual reason, and a written scene can give them a different one, in which case you'll read it in that line.
- **How strong you are.** The game works out how long each side would take to beat the other, from everyone's real numbers, so it changes with every hit. Nobody backs down when they're winning.
- **The stat that helps.** Your skill and your best stat for it are added to the roll. What helps with talking depends on who it is: you can show a hired hand the sums with Intellect, a Korrun respects Might, and someone desperate needs reading with Perception.

It takes your whole turn, and the enemies still get theirs. Your followers hold their fire while you do it. If it fails, they get angrier, so it's harder the next time. Once one of them has gone, the others are a little less sure of themselves, and more so if that was the one in charge. Anyone who walks away isn't beaten: you get the XP, but none of their money, and they're still out there, so the story decides what they do next. Machines can't be talked to or frightened, though a free synthetic can be reasoned with. The Choir isn't listening, and it's very hard to hack. The very worst enemies, the same ones you can't run from, can only be stopped by a fight.

**Give up** ends the fight on the spot, as long as there's someone there to give up to. You're not hurt any more than you already are, and they leave it there, unless a written scene started the fight and says what happens next. It costs you some stress, and it's never the end of the game.

**Followers:** either side can have followers if the story allows it, up to four fighters on a side, counting you. Some may come running partway through. Your followers fight by themselves, going after whatever you're aiming at, and they take a share of the XP. A follower who's knocked out stops fighting, and the story decides what became of them. If you fall, the fight is lost, even if your followers are still standing.

Fights run entirely on this computer, from the stats in the game's files. A fight starts when a written scene says so, or when you attack someone from the right-click menu. When it ends, the story gets one line saying how it went. If a written scene was waiting on the fight, **Continue the story** goes on from how it ended. If nothing was, the fight is simply over, and the window takes you back to the room.

To try a fight without it counting, use **Run in simulator** in **Enemies met**, aboard the Meridian, or **With followers** to have your crew and more enemies join in. Nothing that happens in the simulator carries over.

## Skill checks

When a choice has a check on it, or a written scene calls for one, a window opens over the story, and each skill has its own. It floats over the room like the game's other windows, with nothing behind it dimmed, so you can see the room go on around you. Pull it aside with the mouse to see what's under it, and it slides back when you let go. A thin line runs from it to what the check is about, in the window's colour: the part you're working, the door you're forcing or hacking, the console you're getting into, the person whose pocket you're picking, or whoever or whatever in the room the check names, such as the person you're treating. If the window would hide that thing, the room slides aside until it shows beside the window, and slides back when the window closes. Practice is about nothing in the room, so it has no line. Hacking is a console session against a server, where you hunt for the password in a memory dump before the trace finds you, and what you do there is the result. Medicine is a patient monitor. Piloting is a run through debris on a proximity radar. Persuasion is a conversation where you read the other person and pick your approach.

| Skills | What the window is |
|---|---|
| Hacking | Find every password in the memory dump before the trace completes. No roll: getting in is the result |
| Science, Lore | Work out a hidden sequence, from how close each attempt gets |
| Engineering | Flip relays to get a whole panel live, when each one also flips its neighbors |
| Medicine, Survival, Composure | Keep three readings in the safe zone while events push them around |
| Piloting, Athletics | Pick your way down a corridor of obstacles, one move at a time |
| Stealth, Void Ops | Cross a map without stepping into anything's line of sight |
| Gunnery, Firearms | Aim where the target is going to be |
| Navigation | Plot the course with the least drift across a star chart |
| Tactics, Melee | Read what the other side is about to do, and answer it |
| Persuasion, Leadership, Streetwise | Bring someone around before they lose patience. With someone who has a file, what lands depends on what they really believe |
| Deception | Keep your cover story alive under questioning, by watching for tells |
| Intimidation, Manifestation | Push harder for a stronger result, and stop before it breaks |
| Commerce | Find their real price without insulting them |
| Awareness | Spot what's out of place in the picture |
| Attunement | Find where on the dial something comes through |

- **Your odds:** the bar at the bottom shows your chance of success, and it moves as you play. Your character's skill sets where it starts, and playing well can add up to 30%. When you're ready, the button beside the command line makes the final roll, at the odds showing. You can press it at any time. Hacking is the exception: see below.
- **Your training matters:** a better-trained character gets better tools in the window. A trained hacker's tools rule out wrong passwords, a good pilot can see further ahead, and a seasoned talker can tell what will land. Some perks and gear make the tools better still.
- **Hacking decides itself.** There's no odds bar and no button: find the password and you're in, let the trace complete and you're caught, or **Disconnect** to give up quietly. What you're breaking into sets the whole session, and the title bar names it: a *door panel* (easy), a *crew terminal* (moderate), a *ship's main system* (hard), a *Ministry or Navy server* (very hard), or *the Beacon or the Choir* (nearly impossible). The class sets how long the password is, how big the dump is, how many wrong tries the trace allows, how many words look nearly right, and how many passwords stand between you and the inside: a ship's main system has two, the account and then root, and the worst have three. Your training adds tries and tools. An **advisor** slides in from the right with hints that grow with your training, from how likeness works to which word to try next and how many tries are left; its tab slides it away and back. If the trace completes on a ship's main system or worse, a fact goes on the record that the stories can read. All the numbers and the hints' words are in `world/checks.json`.
- **It's never a race:** nothing in the window moves until you do, and everything can be clicked as well as typed. The room's clock runs on behind every check, the story's own as well as those on something in the room, such as a junction box or a locked door, so the world doesn't wait for you. If you do type, part of a word is enough, and a typo won't cost you anything. Number keys pick commands, and the arrow keys work wherever there's a direction to choose.
- **When it's over:** if a written scene is waiting on the result, **Continue the story** goes on from it. Otherwise **Close** takes you back to the room, and the result is in the story.
- **To try them:** every skill in the **Skills** menu has a **Practice** button, and nothing in practice counts. For Hacking it's a row of buttons, one for each class of computer.
- **To skip them:** in **Settings**, set **Skill checks** to **Just a roll**, and checks go straight to the roll at your character's own odds, hacking included.

## Computers

Getting into a computer is the start, not the end. When a hack gets in, when you **Use** a console that's yours, or when you've learned a computer's password, the screen becomes the computer's own system: a console window floating over the room the same way as the check window, with nothing dimmed, a line to the console or the door you're at, and the clock running on, which you can pull aside and which slides back. It has what's on it down the left, what you've opened in the middle, the advisor's hints on the right, and a command line at the bottom for anyone who'd rather type. Everything can be clicked.

- **What's on one:** **Files** to read, **Mail** between the people of the place, **Logs** the game writes from what it knows (whether the Meridian is at the berths, today's prices, who's here, what's been heard), **Programs** that do real things, and a **Network** of other computers it can reach.
- **Programs do real things:** a door opens on the map, a debt is cleared, a fact goes on the record for the stories to read, a password to another computer is learned. Each runs once. What you've read goes into **What you know**.
- **The trace runs on inside** a hacked computer: every program costs a point of it, and on a Ministry or Navy server or worse so does every first read. The meter is in the taskbar. If it completes, you're thrown out, and on a ship's main system or worse the mark goes on the record. Your own computers aboard the Meridian have no trace.
- **The network:** another computer on the list is a hack of its own class, unless it's yours or you know its password. The advisor says what's reachable and how hard it is.
- **When you log off,** one `[COMPUTER]` line in the story says what you read and ran, at least a couple of minutes have gone by for each thing you did, counting the time the clock ran while you were on it. A computer remembers what you've read and run for next time.
- **Written or made up:** the Meridian's plot table and reactor gauges, Haven's berth board and the harbor master's machine, the Foundry's sublevel door panel and the Ministry's archive on Asterion are written out in `world/computers/`, as drafts for the owner to rewrite. Any other console, terminal, repeater, server, keypad, panel or power board in a room, and any door that asks for a hack, is a computer the game makes up from what it knows of the place, unless the map file already says what using it does. `_how-to-write-a-computer.txt` in that folder explains the files, and the **Story board** shows them under **Computers**. A scene can hand out a password with `* password`, open a door with `* door`, or sit the player down with `* computer`.

## Systems

Every place has its own systems: power, air, lights, doors, cameras, an alarm and comms, and on a ship engines and navigation, on a station docking clamps. None of them reaches another place, and every part is a real thing in a room: a junction box on the wall, a vent in every room, a camera with a red light, a speaker, a relay. They're drawn where they are, each its own small drawing, and listed under **Systems** in **What's here**, the pane over the room. Click one and you walk over to it.

- **Working them by hand:** a part's window, beside it in the room, says what it is and its state, and the **Actions** window glued to its side says what you can do to it. Some things are free, like a light switch or a junction box's lever. Others open the check window: cutting cables takes Engineering, covering a camera's lens takes Stealth, and forcing a locked door takes Athletics.
- **Power:** a junction box powers its section. A room without power goes dark, and its cameras, vents, speakers, screens and door panels stop. A computer in a room without power won't come on, and one you're using goes dark and logs you off when its room's power is cut, from it or from anywhere else.
- **The dark:** a dark room is drawn dark, with only the red emergency lamps at its doors. Sneaking there is easier, by 2, and keeping watch is harder, by 2.
- **Air:** when the air plant stops, or a room's vent is closed or sealed, or the room loses power, its air goes bad over about twenty minutes. A haze hangs in the room, people head for good air, and you feel it: stress while it's thin, then harm once it's bad. It never takes you below 1 health, but you can't rest in it. Air comes back in about ten minutes once it's moving again.
- **Cameras and the alarm:** a camera watching you makes sneaking harder, by 2. Tamper with anything where a camera can see, and the alarm rings. The room washes red, the guards head for it if comms are up, the doors the place's file names lock, and whoever holds the place thinks less of you, by 2. It also goes on the record that you were seen, for the stories to react to. The alarm stops after half an hour, or from a computer that runs it.
- **Doors:** a locked door stops anyone walking through it, both ways. Its window offers **Force the door** or **Hack the door panel**, which opens the panel as a computer that runs that door. A forced door won't lock again until it's fixed.
- **Computers run them:** a computer that runs a place's systems has a **Systems** list on its screen, each part with its state and buttons to switch it, and a camera's screen shows who it sees. On a hacked computer each switch costs a point of trace, and cutting the power to a computer's own room turns its screen off with everything else.
- **The place puts things right:** its engineers fix what's broken, in a few hours, and you can see one walk over to do it. Its people switch back what you switched off, after a while. What the story changes stays changed.
- **The Meridian is yours:** as captain you run all her systems from the **Ship** menu, with no hacking, and nothing you do aboard her sets off the alarm. With her engines down she can't jump. With her navigation down every jump is plotted by hand, a fuel more and half as long again. A station with its docking clamps locked won't let her leave.
- **The story:** the written story can ask about the systems and change them, with lines like `? alarm ringing at haven` or `* power off in The Docks`.
- **Where they're written:** `world/systems/` has a file for the Meridian, Haven Station and Tessen Colony, as drafts for the owner to rewrite. Every other place gets systems made from its rooms and things, the way `world/systems/_defaults.json` says, and the alien places get none. `_how-to-write-systems.txt` there explains it all. The **Story board** shows any place's systems under **Systems**, its own or the defaults, and saving one there makes it the place's own file.

## Who you are

You don't have to be the captain. The **Your place** step of creating a character has 22 lives to start from: **Captain of the Meridian**, which plays the way it always has, or one of 21 others, aboard the ships or out in the world. You could be an engine-room hand on the Meridian, a dock hand at Haven Station, a miner at Cinder Reach, a card dealer on the Gilded Wheel, or a scavenger at the Pale Gate. **Surprise me** can land you in any of them. Games you've already started stay as the captain.

The life you pick decides where you start, who you know, where you stand with each faction, and your first quest. After that the world goes on over your head, and what you do about it is up to you. If you aren't the captain:

- **Someone else has the chair.** It's Captain Reddick, who's careful, Captain Marchetti, who's bold, or Captain Lund, who's somewhere between, and it's a different one from game to game. They're on the Meridian's Bridge like anyone else, and you can go and talk to them.
- **They really run the ship.** They move her when she needs something, and buy it at the dock out of her own money, every day, on your computer. It happens whether you're aboard or on the far side of the map.
- **The Ship menu tells you what you'd know.** Aboard the Meridian, or on the same dock as her, you see how she stands, what's been decided and her log. Anywhere else, you only get where she was last heard of. You can't give her orders, her systems aren't yours to run, and **Wait** just lets the time go by. The story only tells you about the ship while you're with her, or if it's big news.
- **Getting around costs money.** The **Star map** shows a fare and a time instead of fuel, and **Book passage** takes you there by yourself, on whatever's going that way. The further and the more dangerous it is, the more it costs, and it's slower than a jump. A few places have no passage at all, because nobody goes there for ordinary reasons, so you'd need the story to get you there. Going aboard the Meridian costs a fare too, unless she's docked where you are.
- **You can work.** Where your job is, you'll find it at the bottom of your registry printout (click your name, or press C), and under **You can** in **What's here**. A shift takes eight hours, wears you out, and pays a few credits, a little more as your skill improves.
- **You have to sleep somewhere.** It's free where you live and aboard the Meridian. Anywhere else, you can rent a bunk for 6 credits, or sleep rough for nothing, which doesn't bring back your health or energy, and leaves you no calmer. Both are in the same two places as your job, and at the bottom of your registry printout.
- **Leads are written for you.** Stories reach you the way they'd reach someone who isn't in charge. The 12 that only work for the captain, such as a court martial or the Commandant's request, stay closed, and the main story goes on around you.
- **It can change.** If the story gets there, a written scene can hand you the chair, or take it away from you. The orders and the **Ship** menu's buttons then become yours, or stop being yours, and the ship goes on from wherever she's got to.

## Bodies

Everyone in the game has a body, made up when a new game starts: the people with files, everyone with only a name, and you. A body has six parts (the head, the chest, both arms and both legs), and each part can carry old marks from a life, like a scar, an old break, old burns or two fingers gone, and wounds of now, like a cut, a broken bone or a burn. Start another game and the same people have different marks, though what someone's own file says is always there: Chief Marrow is always missing two fingers from his left hand, and Rennick always limps.

- **Where they come from.** How many old marks someone has goes by their age, and rough work, like the engine room, the docks or the marines, brings them twice as often and makes the marks that go with it likelier: burns in the engine room, a bad knee on the docks, scars on a guard. Synthetics carry their own kind, like scuffed casing or a replaced joint, and don't bleed.
- **Your own body** comes from your life story. The injuries in it leave their marks, like the burns over half your body from losing your ship in an ambush, or the lungs that never quite recovered from a lab accident, and so do the traits One-Eyed, Old Wound and the cybernetic arm and eye. The wounds you have now sit on a part: a broken arm is on one of your arms, a concussion on your head, and blood loss lowers your blood.
- **Where to see them.** **Look** at someone (right-click them) and you read how they walk if it isn't normally, and the worst of what can be seen on them. Their **Registry** printout has the chart of their body: a figure with each part shaded by the worst on it, how they walk, their blood, and every mark and wound, worst first. Your own is on your printout (**C**) and on the figure in your inventory (**I**).
- **Vik and Hana**, on the medbay's cots, are there for a reason now: Vik has a broken leg and can't walk, and Hana has a deep burn.
- **Nothing works differently yet.** This is the first step of the health plan. Wounds don't bleed or heal yet, a limp doesn't slow anyone down, and nobody is carried to the medbay. Those come in the next steps, one at a time.
- **Where they're written.** `world/health/wounds.txt` has every kind of wound and old mark, where on the body it can be, what it looks like and its rules, and how often people gather old marks. `world/health/meridian.txt` has who starts the game hurt aboard the Meridian. A species' file has a **Body** part with how often a hit lands on each part and what its people do differently, and a person's file can have a **Body** part with what's always true of them, which the game never changes. `world/health/_how-to-write-health.txt` explains all of it. `npm run check-story` checks it too.

## The ship

The Meridian is a frigate built for three hundred, carrying nine hundred, and she's on her own: the fleet she once led is a thing of the past. The captain feeds, fuels and moves her, and she can run out of things. This section is how that works if the captain's you. If it isn't, someone else is doing all of this: see [Who you are](#who-you-are). All of it runs on this computer, and what happens shows in the story as a line or two.

**Stores.** Food, medicine and parts are what's in the rooms' stores, described below, counted together: food is every ration crate and basket of produce aboard, counted as crates. Fuel is the tank, for jumps. Nine hundred people eat about nine crates a day at the kettle, the ship burns a little under half a unit of fuel a day keeping station, the medbay takes a crate of medicine each time it has used one up, more often the sicker people are, and the engineers a crate of parts about once a week. While a store is empty nothing's used from it and nothing's owed, so the first crates to come aboard after a shortage last their whole time. The shuttle stores are topped up every six hours, so the food reads **holding** on the Ship menu instead of a count of days, and settles at twenty-odd crates: the shuttle stores full, the pantry kept at about four by Brandt, and the produce eaten as fast as the garden grows it. Nobody counts the days of a supply that's holding: the council won't cut the rations for it, and whoever has the chair won't go shopping for it. She starts with about two weeks of fuel, six weeks of medicine and twelve of parts, unless you do something about it. You'll get a warning in the story when something's about five days from running out, and then:

| Runs out | What it does |
|---|---|
| Food | Morale and health fall fast, and you go hungry yourself, which costs you Endurance |
| Fuel | She can't jump, and the lower decks go cold |
| Medicine | Health falls, people aboard start dying, and your own wounds heal half as fast |
| Parts | Nothing gets mended, and the hull wears a little every day |

**In the rooms.** Her stocks are also kept somewhere you can walk to: the shuttle stores in the lighter bay, the fuel rack in engineering, the pantry in the galley, the garden bins, the medicine cabinet in the medbay, the parts lockers in stores, and the soup kettle in the mess. Walk up to one and press **Open**, in its window or its right-click menu, and its grid opens beside your pack, the way a pile of loot does: take a crate out, put it back, and the Ship menu's **In the rooms** section follows the count. They start full. Where each one is and what it holds is written in `world/logistics/meridian.txt`, which the **Story board** shows under **Logistics**, and `npm run check-story` reads it too.

**The machines and the sittings.** The same file says what the ship's machines do, and they run on the clock, at the same beat as everyone's days. While a cook's in the galley and the room has power, the galley turns a basket of produce or a ration crate from the pantry into two hundred sittings in the soup kettle every hour; while a gardener's in the garden, the beds give a basket of produce into the garden bins every two hours; the generator is a machine too, and has its own paragraph below. The Ship menu's **Machines** lines say what each is doing, or why it's waiting: no power, waiting for a cook, its store full, or nothing left to cook. Everyone aboard eats from the kettle at four sittings a day, two in the morning and two in the evening, four hundred and fifty people at each, and the kettle says how much is left in it when you look. Whoever finds it empty goes without: the people you can see eating are "finding the kettle empty" instead, you're told when it runs dry, and at midnight the ship's morale feels the share who went hungry, through the same rule as food running out, with a line in the ship's log. Nothing happens while the clock is paused. The daily count above still runs on its own for now, and a later step makes the two one count.

**The errands.** When a store runs short, someone goes for more: the quartermaster (Brandt, while she holds the post) fetches ration crates from the shuttle stores in the lighter bay when the pantry is under four, two at a time; a cook fetches produce from the garden bins when the pantry is low on it; a tech keeps the fuel rack in engineering topped up with cells; and a tech carries parts crates from the shuttle stores to the parts lockers when the lockers are down to one. Whoever does the job and is awake and free goes, so moving people on the watch bill changes who carries. You'll see them leave what they were doing, walk there, stand at the store a minute, and walk back with the load, their line saying what they're carrying all the way; then they go back to their day, and again while the store's still short. If there's nothing to fetch, they stand at the store and wait, and their line says what for. The Ship menu's **Errands** lines show where each has got to. Take the pantry's crates yourself and watch Brandt go down for more. Nobody carries in their sleep, so a store that runs short at night waits for the morning.

**The generator and the power.** The generator in engineering burns a fuel cell every eight hours, taken from the fuel rack, which a tech keeps up from the shuttle stores, and it gives the hundred power that everything aboard draws: the air plant, the doors, comms, navigation, the cameras, the lights room by room, and the computers. The same file says what each draws and the order they're let go in when there isn't enough: cameras first, then comms, lights, doors, navigation, and the air last. At rest the ship draws eighty, so there's some to spare. Under way the engines take their thirty, and for the jump the cameras and comms are let go. If the rack runs dry when the generator needs its next cell, it stops, and nothing aboard has power: every room goes dark, the air stops, the cameras see nothing, the galley stops cooking, and the ship can't jump, exactly as when a junction box is cut, until someone puts a cell in the rack. The Ship menu's **Power** section, the **Machines** lines, the reactor gauges' readouts in engineering and any computer's Power screen say what the generator gives against what's drawn and what's been let go, and a dead part says why. To try it: empty the fuel rack and the shuttle stores' cells and wait for the generator to stop; then bring a cell to the rack and watch the lights come back. Between refills there are eighteen cells aboard, about six days' worth.

**The refills, from nothing.** Every six hours, on the hour from midnight, the shuttle stores in the lighter bay are topped up with ration crates and fuel cells, from nowhere: that's the one place anything still comes from nothing, standing in for trade off the ship until that's built. Each refill is written in the manifest, which you read by looking at the shuttle stores (it's on the clip) or in the reactor gauges' logs in engineering, and the Ship menu's **Refills** lines say the last one. Empty the pantry and watch Brandt bring the next refill up. Medicine and parts aren't refilled: those you buy, or find.

**The repairs.** When something aboard has been damaged (a junction box's cables cut, an injector pulled out of the drive, a door forced, anything broken), an engineer fixes it within two hours, and the repair uses up a crate of parts. In the last hour you'll see the engineer walk to the parts lockers, or to the shuttle stores if the lockers are empty, their line saying they're fetching a crate of parts, and then to what they're fixing; the story says when it's done and where the crate came from. With no crate of parts aboard, the repair waits: the story says so once, the part's look says Marrow can't fix it until there's a crate in the stores, and the Ship menu's **Repairs** lines list what's waiting. Within the hour after a crate comes aboard, it's fixed. A rag over a camera's lens, a seal on a vent, a jammed intake or a scrambled alignment are put right with nothing, and so is anything you fix yourself. The line *a repair takes* at the top of the logistics file says all of this, and you can change it. To try it: cut the cables of the junction box in engineering, and watch Marrow go for a crate.

**The chain of command.** The **Command** menu, under The ship, shows every post aboard as a card: who holds it, where they're usually found, who chose them, their numbers in the skills that count for the post, the jobs they head, and since when. You're the captain at the top if you started as one, and otherwise whoever has the chair is. Under the captain come Commander Voss, the first officer, who keeps the watch bill, and the heads of the ship's departments: Quartermaster Brandt, Chief Engineer Marrow, Lieutenant Tarrow on security, Dr. Aduro and Deck Chief Hask. Under Voss come the bridge's comms, helm and sensor officers, and Speaker Halm is chosen by the civilians. Click the card of one of the story's people to read their registry printout over the room. Everything that used to name someone for their post now asks who holds it: the officers' council's rations decisions name the quartermaster and the speaker, the alarm brings whoever's chief of security, the chief engineer fixes what's broken, the quartermaster carries the ration crates, and the captain's chair is the captain's post. Posts change hands at the officers' reviews, and a mutiny can take the ship, both below. The posts, who chooses each, the jobs, who holds what and who does which job when a game begins are in `world/command/meridian.txt`, and a game keeps its own from the start, so a change there reaches new games.

**The watch bill.** Under the cards, the Command menu has the watch bill, kept by Commander Voss: every job aboard (cooks, mess hands, gardeners, techs, nurses, deckhands, ensigns and guards), the room it's done in, how many it wants, who's doing it, and how many it's short, with the spare hands at the bottom, six civilians who can be put to work. Everyone on it lives their job's day: put a cook in the garden and she walks to the garden and tends the beds, at her own shift. What's done follows: the galley only cooks with someone whose job is cook in it, errands go to whoever does the job they name and is free, the techs are the engineers who fix what's broken, and the guards come with the chief of security when the alarm rings. As captain you move people with two clicks: click someone, and **Move here** comes up on every other job; click the one they're going to. If someone's lost from their job, beaten and being patched up, or dead, Voss puts a spare hand on in their place and says so, and sends the spare hand back once they're back. The guards start with nobody, so they're two short until someone's put there. If you started as crew aboard, the engine hand, the orderly or the marine, you're on the watch bill too, among the techs, the nurses or the guards, and if you're ever moved, the shift you work moves with you. To try it: take both cooks off the galley before dinner, and watch the sitting go hungry; then put them back.

**How well they do.** Each post's card has a duty bar for whoever holds it: the average of their marks over the last week, each a day's mark out of 100, worked out at midnight from what the game already counts, with the last day's marks under it. The quartermaster is marked on sittings missed and stores run dry, the chief engineer on power short, repairs waiting and how worn the hull is, the chief of security on alarms, tampering seen and thefts, the doctor on medicine running out and deaths aboard, the deck chief on errands left waiting at an empty store, the first officer on how much of the watch bill is staffed, and the captain on morale, shortages and deaths aboard. Someone new in a post starts with a clean record. Skill shows in the work too: each point of the best worker's skill above 5 makes the work 8% quicker, and each point below that much slower. Marrow's engineering 8 takes a two-hour repair down to about an hour and forty minutes, and two spare hands put in the galley cook about a quarter slower than the cooks. Someone without a file of their own is a 5 at their own job and a 2 at anything else. A bad record can cost someone their post, below. To try it: take both cooks off the galley, wait two days, and watch Brandt's bar fall.

**Rising and falling.** Every seven days the officers review their people. Someone whose duty has been under 60 for five days of the last week is warned straight away by whoever chooses for their post, and relieved at the next review if it's gone on: the best of those the post is picked from takes it, and the one relieved takes the job the new holder left. You hear it in the story: "Commander Voss has relieved Quartermaster Brandt after six days of empty kettles, and given the post to Gardener Benno. Brandt is a gardener now, and takes it quietly." The new holder lives the post's day, sleeping in its cabin and working at its station, and the ship's own lines name them ("Food is down to about five days. Quartermaster Benno has started checking the count twice."). If whoever took over has the same trouble, Voss decides it isn't the post's doing, says so, and stops relieving people over it until it's over. As captain, the reviews come to you as Voss's recommendations, under **Waiting on you** at the top of the Command menu: **Do it**, **Keep** whoever holds the post, or **Leave it to Voss**. If you don't answer by the next review, she acts in your name. **Replace...** under a card gives a post you or Voss choose for to anyone it's picked from, in two clicks, and **Hold the reviews** stops anyone being warned, relieved or given a post until you let them go on. Someone you choose or keep on isn't reviewed for ten days. If you're crew, whoever has the chair decides without asking you. People remember being relieved, warned, passed over, told no, given a post or given one back, and by whom: it shows on their card, the watch bill says who was relieved, and for about a month it's the first thing they say when you talk to them. The ambitious push: someone with two points more skill for a post than whoever holds it, and the ambition for it, asks for the post, and one who's told no and has the temper works against their boss, taking ten off each of their days. Each officer's file has their ambition, temper and loyalty to the captain to begin with, and everyone else has the command file's. To try it: take both cooks off the galley and wait a week, and Voss warns Brandt and then recommends relieving her. Or relieve someone yourself with **Replace...**, then go and talk to them.

**Loyalty and mutiny.** Each officer's card has a loyalty bar: how loyal they are to the captain, from their file's own number, what they remember of the captain, and what's moved it lately, with the reasons under the bar ("Down for the rations, going hungry"). Half or reduced rations pull it down a little every day and generous ones lift it, a day of empty kettles pulls it down, morale under 30 pulls it down, a jump somewhere risky costs it, and so do the sides you take in the world, as far as each officer's beliefs disagree with the faction's. Each settles back once whatever moved it stops. The council has a confidence of its own, which follows morale, less for poor rations, empty kettles and shortages, and it's the speaker's duty bar. The top of the Command menu shows the officers' loyalty on average, the council's confidence, and how things stand aboard. When morale is under 30 and the officers' loyalty under 35, a mutiny brews, a day at a time. After a day there's grumbling: people at the benches are "grumbling about the captain", and the discontented say so when you talk to them. After four days, orders go astray: the council won't put a cut in the rations through for you, and a first officer who's lost faith won't move anyone on the watch bill. After seven, the officers come to you together, in a written scene that plays when you carry on the story. You can give them what they ask, talk them round, have the chief of security arrest whoever's leading them while security's still loyal, step down, or throw them out yourself, which is a fight like any other, with talking, threatening, paying and lying in it. Send them away, or leave them waiting, and they take the ship: whoever led them has the chair, you spend three days in the brig, and then you're crew, one of the spare hands, with shifts to work. If someone else has the chair and you're aboard, whoever's leading it asks you in, and you can join, stay out, or warn the captain; and if the officers once took the ship from you, they may offer it back when it's the new captain's turn. Whoever's arrested spends a week in the brig and comes out a spare hand. To try it: hold the council on the Ship menu, put the ship on half rations, take the cooks off the galley, relieve Voss, and wait about three weeks.

**The story knows.** A written scene can ask who holds a post (`? holds quartermaster odessa`), what someone does on the watch bill (`? job Yusuf cook`), how loyal someone is (`? loyalty marrow < 40`) and how far a mutiny has got (`? mutiny brewing`), and it can change things, each with a colon and the reason: `* appoint quartermaster tobin`, `* relieve chief_engineer`, `* assign Yusuf cook`, `* loyalty marrow -10`, and `* remember marrow tarrow -15` for a grudge one person holds against another. A scene can name whoever holds a post in curly brackets, like {quartermaster}, and the game fills in their name. When a post changes hands, someone's put in the brig, or a mutiny ends one way or another, it goes on the record, under Facts on record in the Quests menu, so later stories can react to it. The Story board has a **Command** page: each post as a box with who holds it when a game begins and an arrow from whoever chooses it, each job with who does it and an arrow from the post that heads it, and the spare hands, all changed with pickers and saved into `world/command/meridian.txt`. To try it: talk to Chief Hask and ask who keeps the stores, then give the quartermaster's post to someone else with **Replace...** and ask him again.

**The hull.** With parts aboard the engineers mend it a little every day, up to its best. Without them it wears. The story can take hull too.

**Morale and health.** Each settles toward a level set by your rations, and the Ship menu shows you where. Low health kills people. Morale is a line the story hears.

**Your decisions.** All of these are on the Ship menu, and each shows its real cost and benefit before you choose:

- **Rations:** Generous, Standard, Reduced or Half. Less food used, against lower morale and health.
- **Wait** a day or three.
- **Buy and sell** supplies for the ship with your own credits, at docks that deal in them: food by the five crates, medicine and parts by the crate, fuel by the unit. What you buy goes into the stores that keep it (food into the shuttle stores, then the pantry), so there has to be room, and what you sell comes out of them. What they cost changes from day to day: see [The wider world](#the-wider-world).

**The officers and the council.** If the food's run low and stayed low, they don't wait to be told. Quartermaster Brandt cuts rations when the count gets low, Speaker Halm puts everyone on half rations when it's nearly gone, Halm talks the council into generous rations for a while when morale's very low and the stores can stand it, and Brandt puts them back once people are happier, or once the stores can carry standard rations again. Each decision comes up in the story and in the **Officers and council** section of the Ship menu.

They give you a day or two first, they make one decision a day, and an order of your own stands for a few days before they'd change it. You can overrule anything they do, and they put back only what they changed themselves. If someone's been lost, the council does it instead. If you'd rather they didn't do any of this, press **Wait for my word**, and they won't, whatever happens.

**The star map.** Click any star to see the fuel and time it takes to get there, how dangerous it is, who controls it, and what it sells to the ship, then press **Set course**. A jump moves the Meridian and takes 1 to 3 fuel, depending on how far it is. The dock she's at is a short shuttle ride away, and so is she from it. The Sundial, the farm ship, and Anselm's Mercy, the hospital ship, are places of their own on the chart now, a jump away like anywhere else. The story line says where you've gone, and if there's a written arrival there, or a scene waiting for the Meridian to get there, **Continue the story** plays it when you're ready.

**What's left of the fleet.** The stories, the places and the people were written when the Meridian led a fleet of forty-one, and most of them still speak of it. The game reads a story's `fleet:` lines as the ship's own (morale, food, medicine, parts and fuel) and lets the rest go, and `npm run check-story` ends with a list of everything that still speaks of the fleet, to rework in time. Eleven stories that stand on a ship of the fleet are set aside in `world/stories/_set-aside.txt`: they stay in their files, and out of the game, until their line is taken out of that list.

## Walking around

**Story view** under **Reading** in the Settings menu switches between **Isometric**, where you see the room you're in from an angle, **Walk-around**, which is the same thing in console art, and **Classic**, where the story has the whole window. Isometric is the default. Isometric and Walk-around work everywhere in the world, and fights, skill checks and menus are the same in all of them.

In Isometric and Walk-around, the main window shows the room you're in, with you standing in it. Every part of every place is its own level, with its own shape, colors and things in it. The Meridian's Bridge has its viewport and sensor pit, the bar at Haven has its weapons rack and its stain, the Long Furnaces at Korrath roar along both walls, the Gilded Wheel is one enormous wheel, and the 18:40 tram is still waiting on Founders Walk in Asterion. You arrive at each place by its way in, such as its docks. In Isometric the story comes up over the room: see [The story over the room](#the-story-over-the-room). In Walk-around it stays in a panel under the room, showing the latest scene, and **Show the whole story** opens all of it. Typing an action works the same way in all of them.

**What's here** floats over the right side of the room, in a pane with corners like the speech panes. Its tag has the room's name and deck. Under it are **About this room**, which opens a few words about the room, the button for the place's plan, the **People** here with what each is doing, the room's **Systems** as small tags (teal when they work, amber when they're off or broken, red when an alarm's ringing), anything wrong with the room, and the **Ways out**, with **You can** under them when you have work or a bunk. The things in the room aren't listed: they're the `?` marks in it. Click the tag to fold the pane down to one line, such as `4 people · 7 systems · 1 way out`, and again to open it, and it stays the way you left it. While it's open, the room keeps you clear of it, and the story's panes over the room keep to its left. On a phone-sized window it sits under the room instead.

- **To walk,** hold **W**, **A**, **S** or **D**, or the arrow keys, or click the floor. You walk freely, not a square at a time: walls stop you, you slide along them, and walking straight at a doorway eases you into it. A click takes the shortest way to the very spot you clicked, and pressing a key on the way takes over from it. Walking into a way out that's open takes you through.
- **The keys walk.** A number key picks one of the story's choices, and **Space** pauses the clock. In Walk-around, the typing line under the room only has the keyboard when you click it, or press Enter, and **Esc** gives the keys back to walking.
- **To go to another room,** click a way out, such as `v The Lower Forward Stair`, on the level or under **Ways out** in **What's here**. Or press the plan button at the top of **What's here**, which has its own name in each place, such as **Deck plan**, **Station plan** or **Colony map**. It shows the whole place and who's in each room, and you click where you want to go. On the Meridian each deck is one continuous level with its rooms drawn on it, so you walk from room to room through their doorways, the room's name and who's in it changing as you cross, and only the stairs between decks are ways out; the deck plan has a tab to a deck, and in Walk-around the deck scrolls to keep you in sight.
- **Some ways in are closed,** and they show an `x` instead of an arrow: a guard, a locked door, a picket line. Click one to walk up to it and read why. **Ask to go through** follows that door's own rule: some open when asked, some need something you've earned, and some open a skill check whose result decides. If the story takes you through, that door stays open from then on, even after you've left and come back. Whatever's behind a closed door is closed too, on the map as well. There are 15 of these in the world, and none on the Meridian.
- **To talk to someone,** click them, on the level or under **People** in **What's here**. You walk up to them, and a talk window opens.
- **To look at something,** click its `?` on the level. You walk up to it, and a window comes up beside it on a line, in the game's console look: its name and state in the bar and what it looks like under it, with an **Actions** window glued to its side holding what you can do. Its colour says what it is: gold for things, teal for the place's parts, amber for a part that's off or dead or a door that's shut, and red for a locked door or a ringing alarm. Nothing behind it is dimmed, and the room goes on around you. Pull the window aside with the mouse to see what's under it, and it slides back when you let go. Walking anywhere, or **Close**, or Escape, puts it away. **Use** gives the thing's own answer in the window, or picks it up, and anything that hasn't been written yet says so plainly. A closed or locked door answers in the same kind of window.
- **Right-click** anyone, anything, a door or the floor, on the level or in **What's here**, and a menu slides open where you clicked, in the same console look and in the colour of what it's for, or the person's own colour. Click a choice or press its number. A click anywhere else, Escape, or walking puts it away. What needs you up close walks you over first, and **Look** looks from where you stand.
  - **Someone:** **Talk**, **Look**, **Registry**, **Pickpocket** and **Attack**. **Look** opens their window beside them, with how they look, how they walk if it isn't normally and the worst of the marks on their body, and what they're doing, and the same choices in its **Actions**. **Registry** feeds their record out of the printer in the map's corner, the same amber printout as your own (your name at the top left, or C): who they are, their post, attributes, skills, how they'd fight, how hard their pocket is, the chart of their body, and what they're doing. Someone with a file prints what it says, never their secrets. Anyone else gets a thin record made up from their name, the same every time, with their job taken from their name, and the fight and pocket it shows are the ones the rules really use. **Your own record** on it prints yours, and **Registry** on them again, **Tear off**, **C** or **Esc** puts it away.
  - **Something:** **Look** and **Use**. A part of the place's systems has **Look** and what can be done to it, such as **Pull the lever down** or **Cut the cables**. Something that holds things, or a pile on the floor, has **Look** and **Open**, which opens it beside your pack in the inventory.
  - **A door:** **Go through**. A closed one has **Look** and **Ask to go through**, and a locked one **Look**, **Force the door** and **Hack the door panel**.
  - **The floor:** **Walk here**.
- **Picking a pocket** is a Stealth check in the check window. It's as hard as the person's Awareness in their file, so Commander Voss, with 4, is hard. Someone without a file is easy, or moderate if their name says they watch for a living, like a guard or a hunter, and anyone asleep is a step easier. A clean lift pays a few credits, and nobody knows. If they catch your hand, they think less of you, whoever holds the place takes 2 off your standing (on the Meridian, as her captain, it's 2 off morale aboard instead), it goes on the record for the stories, and a camera on the room rings the alarm. Either way, they keep a hand on their pocket for a day.
- **Attacking someone** asks first, then walks you over and starts a fight: in the isometric view it's played in the room, with them where they stood, and in the other views in the fight window. Someone with a file fights the way their file says. Anyone else fights as a Dock Thug under their own name, or as a Customs Marine if they're a guard. It costs 5 standing with whoever holds the place (5 morale on your own ship), turns anyone with a file against you, goes on the record, and a camera on the room rings the alarm. If you win, they're off getting patched up for 12 hours. All of these numbers are in `world/deeds.json`.
- **The clock runs on** while a thing's window is open, through every skill check (working a part of the place's systems, forcing or hacking a door, hacking a computer you've walked up to, picking a pocket, and the story's own checks too), while you're using a computer, and while a menu or the map of the place is open. So the world doesn't wait while you try, and the air can go bad or the alarm stop while you're at it. Talks and fights stop it, and so does **Space**.
- **When the story moves you,** the map follows, and people the story has with you stand beside you.
- **When the story goes somewhere that isn't on the map,** such as somewhere a scene names that no map has, that place gets a room of its own, drawn to suit: ducts look like ducts, a brig has bars, and a chapel has pews. The scene says what kind of room it is and what's in it that matters, and the room is kept with your campaign, so it stays the same if you come back. It's listed on the map of that place from then on.

**Isometric** is what the game looks like, seen from an angle the way Fallout is. It's the same rooms, people, clock and clicks as Walk-around, and everything above applies, except that the room is drawn instead of written out in characters.

- **To walk,** hold **W** to walk up the screen, **S** down it, **A** to the left and **D** to the right, or the arrow keys the same way. Two at once walk along the room's own edges, so **W** and **D** together go up and to the right. Or click the floor, or anyone, a `?` or the name of a way out, and you walk over to it.
- **The camera** shows the whole room if there's room for it. If not, it stays with you, and **What's here** lists the people, systems and ways out that are off the edge; things off the edge come into view as you walk toward them. While **What's here** is open, the camera keeps you in the part of the room it doesn't cover, and folding it gives the camera the whole width back. The mouse wheel zooms in and out. Zoomed all the way out, the room is drawn at 82% of its usual size, so the ship stays big enough to read.
- **Walls at the front of a room** are cut down, so that they don't hide anyone.
- **The look** owes something to old isometric games like UnderRail: dark rooms, pools of colored light, gritty floors and walls, and clutter. It's only the style, though. None of their art is in here.
- **The game draws all of it by itself** from each level's file, with no art from anywhere else and no graphics engine, so anything you change in `world/maps/` shows up here too. It works out its own metal, concrete, rock, wood and rust. A mark's shape comes from what it is: `#` is a wall, `=` is a console or a bench, `[]` is a crate, `|` is a post, `~` is water, `%` is rock, scrap or crops depending on the place, and anything shut inside an outline stands up as one solid thing.
- **Lamps and fire:** lamps on the back walls throw pools of light on the floor, and a row of `*` on a plan is something burning, such as a furnace or a reactor. A `*` on its own is a star.

### The story over the room

In Isometric, the room gets the whole window and the story comes up over it, in the same console windows as the rest of the game.

- **A scene comes up a piece at a time.** Click anywhere, on the room or on the story, or press Enter, to go on to the next piece. **Back** goes back. Nothing makes you read it all: you can pick a choice at any point.
- **What someone says** comes up on a pane by their head, in their color: four corners, a tag with their name, and a thin line down to their head. It slides out from the head, and the words write themselves out a letter at a time. A click finishes the line at once, and the next click goes on. If they're far away, the camera moves to keep them in view, and a long speech is cut into pieces that fit.
- **Pull the pane aside** with the mouse to see what's under it. It slides back when you let go.
- **What happens** comes up over the top of the room while you read it, and goes once you've gone on. The last of it goes by itself a few seconds after you've read it, since it's all kept in the quest log. So does anyone with no head in the room to put it over, such as a voice over comms or someone who isn't on the map. Nothing of the story stays along the bottom.
- **What you can say** hangs off the pane of whoever the story is waiting on, once a scene has been read, the way it does in a talk: click one, or press its number. When nobody's being answered, it hangs off your own head. While a quest is on hold there's nothing to answer, and the tracker says who to go back to.
- **What's happening:** news, supplies and what your choices did come up as short lines at the top left and go away by themselves. Click one to dismiss it. They all stay in the whole story. A line waits for the piece of the story it goes with, so it doesn't give anything away. The clock runs on while you read, so press **Space** if you'd like the world to wait.
- **Esc puts the story away,** and **The story is waiting for you**, at the top of the room, brings it back, and so does clicking whoever it's waiting on. A new scene brings it back by itself. Until you've read to the end of a scene, a click on the room goes on with the story and doesn't walk anywhere, so to walk off in the middle of one, press Esc, or walk with the keys.
- **The whole story** is in the quest log, quest by quest, under **What happened**, and all of it together is in Classic, under **Reading** in the Settings menu.
- **With less room,** such as in the smallest window, it all goes under the tracker: what happens comes up below it, and the panes keep out of its way. It's one thing at a time: narration goes away while someone's talking, and the choices wait until you've read to the end.
- **In a talk,** what the person says comes up on a pane by their head the same way, and the things you can say hang off it once their words are all there. See below.

Under **Reading** in the Settings menu:

- **Story over the room:** **Popups** is all of the above. **One box** keeps everything in the one window at the top of the room, with nothing over anyone's head. **Under the room** puts the story back in a panel under the room, the way it is in Walk-around.
- **People's words:** how fast they write out: **Slow**, **Normal**, **Fast** or **All at once**.
- **Popup font:** **Terminal** matches the rest of the game, and **Book** is the one the story uses in Classic.
- **What each place is made of** and how it's lit is in `world/looks.json`: a cold blue Bridge, an orange furnace, red carpet at the Wheel. Change a word or a color there, and restart the game. It can also put one big thing in a room in place of what's on its plan, which is how the Great Wheel gets to be round.
- **It doesn't change your save,** and the choice is kept with your other reading settings.

**Talking to someone.** In Isometric, with the story over the room, a talk comes up by the person's head: what they say on a pane in their color, with how they feel about you on its tag, and **What do you say?** beside it, on whichever side has the room, once their words are all there. It has the things you can say, to click or pick by their number, and **What was said** folded under them. Your own line comes up over your own head while they answer. Click their pane to finish a line at once, and pull it aside to see what's under it. In Walk-around and Classic, and with the story under the room, the talk has its own window instead, showing the person, how they feel about you, what's been said, and the same things to say.

- **What they say:** someone with a written conversation of their own answers in their own words, and what you say leads on. Everyone else has stock lines, made from who they are and what they're doing. **Ask what they've heard** passes on what's going around.
- **Time:** the clock stops during a talk, so talking can never use up a day aboard. A conversation can't pass time or move you, and sleeping and the Ship menu's orders wait until you've closed the window.
- **Checks and fights:** if something you say leads to a skill check or a fight, its own window takes over, and the talk ends there.
- **Afterwards:** the story keeps one line about the talk, with **What was said** folded under it.

## People's lives

Everywhere in the world, people have a day of their own. In Isometric and Walk-around you can watch them get up, go to work, eat, walk around and go to bed, and they carry on whether you're watching or not. Aboard the Meridian that's the 13 crew you know, each with their own habits, and 24 others. In the other 23 places it's the 44 people you can meet there and 231 locals, such as dockers at Haven, kelp farmers at Maris Deep, the debtors in the Gilded Wheel's cellars and the monks who sit in shifts at Ilun. Each place keeps its own hours: the Remnant stands for the anthem at six, nearly everyone at the Wheel stops to watch it spin at eight in the evening, and at Vantor Prime they're home before the last chime. All of this runs on your computer.

- **The clock** runs by itself while you explore, from the moment the game opens. It's under the level, with buttons for **Pause**, **Normal**, **Fast** and **Faster**, and **Space** pauses it, and goes on again at the speed it had. A day in the game takes about two and a half hours at **Normal**, 24 minutes at **Fast**, and 5 minutes at **Faster**. This is time like any other: you get tired and your wounds heal as it goes by, and each midnight is another day of the ship's food and fuel.
- **It runs until you pause it.** It goes on through the menus, the map of the place, reading the story, a thing's window, skill checks and computers, and when you've stepped away. It stops by itself only for fights and talks, which take their own time, while the story's being written, while there's something typed in the typing line, while the Story board is open, and while the game's window is minimized. In the Classic view it never runs by itself, and only the story moves it.
- **Who's in the room** is listed under **People** in **What's here**, with what each of them is doing. Click anyone to walk over and talk, even someone who's just a dim `o`. Someone asleep has to be woken up first. Someone who's on their way somewhere shows only their letter, and you can walk right past them.
- **The Hot Bunks** is the room where most of the Meridian sleeps, through a door on the Names Bulkhead. The map of each place shows how many people are in each room.
- **People go home.** At night a lot of people leave by a door and can't be found until morning, so a place can be nearly empty at three o'clock. You can wait, or press **Faster**.
- **The story knows about it.** A written scene waits for the person it needs, wherever their day has taken them. If the story brings someone over to you, they stay for a while, then go back to their day, from wherever they were standing.
- **Only where you are is walked step by step.** Everywhere else, people are simply where their day says they are whenever you turn up, which keeps a whole world of them quick.

## The wider world

Every place has its own supplies, prices and factions, and they change at each midnight, whether you're there or not. This runs on your computer too, and a whole year of it takes a fraction of a second.

- **Supplies:** food, fuel, medicine and parts each have a level at each place, shown as plenty, normal, short, scarce or almost gone. They drift up and down by themselves over days and weeks.
- **Places depend on each other.** Haven gets its fuel from Cinder Reach and the Deepyards, for example, so a strike at Cinder Reach means dearer fuel at Haven a few days later.
- **Prices follow the supplies.** The less there is of something, the more it costs, up to about three times the usual price, and when there's plenty it's cheaper. That goes for what the ship buys in the Ship menu and for what's made from it in the Market: a medkit costs more where medicine's short, and so does what you sell there.
- **Each lot you buy or sell moves the price,** so buying up everything gets expensive, and it can run out. Selling to a place that's short pays well, and it helps them. The ship still needs what you sell, though.
- **Who holds a place** is worked out from how much of a say each side has there, which you can see on the star map. It shifts a little each day. Shortages get blamed on whoever's in charge, and things that happen move it further. A side that gets clearly ahead takes over, and you'll hear about it wherever you are. Some places can't change hands, such as Vantor Prime and Foundry Seven.
- **Whoever holds a place sets your prices there.** If they hate you, everything costs a quarter more, and if they like you, a little less.
- **Things happen,** each place with its own: raiders at Tessen, a fight on the picket at Cinder Reach, a bloom at Maris Deep, high season at the Gilded Wheel. Trouble makes a place one step more dangerous while it lasts.
- **News** is at the bottom of the **Star map** menu, for the places you know of. You're only told in the story about what's happening where you or the Meridian are, or anything big. People also pass the latest on when you ask what they've heard.
- **The Star map** shows all of this for each place, and which supplies are short. The list of factions in the **Beliefs** menu says what each one holds.
- **The story can change it.** When the story changes things, it changes them for real, so if you help the strikers take the Stack, they hold it from then on.
- **The rest:** the Choir's places, the Pale Gate, the Sundial, Anselm's Mercy and the Meridian herself have no daily supplies of their own. The Meridian runs on her own stores, on the Ship menu.

## Stories don't wait

The world isn't waiting for you. Each story has what happens if nobody steps in, and it happens at midnight on the day it comes round, on your computer. Leave the strike at Cinder Reach alone, for example, and the hired men try the Gate, the strikers throw them back, and the fuel stops coming out.

- **It really changes things.** A story that moves on can change what a place has and who holds it, and it can cost the Meridian morale, supplies or fuel.
- **Nothing's ever closed.** A lead that's moved on says how things stand now, and it's marked **it's moved on** in the **Leads** menu. If it's been written out, you can still take it up from there.
- **Once you've taken a story up, it's in your hands,** and its clock stops.
- **What you did has consequences, on a timer.** A story's clock can say what follows from how a story ended, or from a fact on the record, and how many days later: a week after you raid Quill's depot, Kessara's docks close to the Meridian. Each happens once, in the news and under **Moved on without you**, and it can leave facts of its own on the record.
- **A stage can depend on the record.** A stage with a condition happens only if the condition holds when its day comes, and is skipped otherwise. Two stages on the same day with opposite conditions are a fork, so what the world does on its own fits what you did: the council without you votes to hear Corvane out if the Meridian runs on his fuel. `npm run check-story` checks the clock's conditions along with the rest.
- **Bigger stories start moving later,** and a story that follows another one opens once that one's finished or has run its course.
- **The main story moves too.** Its pressure builds with events of its own, and if nobody moves it on, it moves on to the next act by itself, as far as the fourth. The last one needs someone to take the Meridian there.
- **You won't be swamped.** Only a few of these come up in the story at once. All of it is under **Moved on without you** in the **Leads** menu, and in the **News** on the **Star map**.
- **Every game's world goes at its own pace,** so you won't see the same things on the same days twice.

## The record

The game keeps a record of what you did: how each story ended, and the lasting facts that left, such as a door closed to the Meridian or a debt owed. It's in the **Quests** menu: a finished story's card carries its ending's name and its last line, and **Facts on record** lists the facts. Every story's file in `world/stories` names its endings (under *Where it can end up*), can name the facts the story leaves (*Facts it can leave*, a name and a line of plain words each) and can say what on the record changes the story (*What on the record changes it*, each a condition written like a scene's, such as `ended main_two_weeks_of_fuel raid_depot` or `flag kessara_closed`, and a line on what changes). The written story ends a story with `* ending its_name: How it ended` and puts facts on the record with `* flag` lines, and its scenes can ask `? ended a_story its_ending`. A story's reactions are notes for whoever writes its scene: the checker reads their conditions, and the story clock's own forks and consequences read the record too. `npm run check-story` checks the notes along with the scenes: an ending without a name, a reaction the game can't read, or one waiting on a fact nothing leaves.

## Leads and the wider world

The **Leads** menu shows what you've heard about wherever you are: a few stories you could start, and what people are saying. **Look into this** starts a story that's been written out. One that hasn't says **Not written yet**: it moves on by itself, and the news says how it's going. Leads change as you travel and as time passes, and finishing one story can open another. They don't wait for you, either: see [Stories don't wait](#stories-dont-wait).

There's a lot more out there than the places you start out knowing about, and you'll hear about them as you go. Every place you know of is on the **Star map**.

## Your files

The world is kept in files you can open with any text editor. Be warned that most of them contain spoilers, including everyone's secrets and how the whole story ends.

- `world/scenes/` has the written story, one plain text file for each scene: its parts, what each says, the choices, the checks and fights they lead to, the conditions on them, and what they change in the game. `talk_<id>.txt` is what that person says in a talk, and `arrive_<place>.txt` plays when the player arrives somewhere. `_how-to-write-a-scene.txt` there explains it all, including a thing's `used` and a door's `ask` in the map files, and the stock lines at the end of `rooms.json`. These are read again whenever the story needs them, so there's no restart. `npm run check-story` lists every mistake with its line, and `npm test` checks them too.
- `world/characters/` has one plain-text file for each important person, whether you've met them or not: who they are, how they look, speak and think, their numbers, and how they fight. `_how-to-write-a-person.txt` there explains every line.
- `world/enemies/` has one plain-text file for each type of enemy, with its numbers, why it usually fights (`motive`), who for (`faction`), and what it says in a fight. `_how-to-write-an-enemy.txt` there explains every line. Against you, threat levels 2 and up fight harder than their files say, by the amounts in `FIGHT_DIFFICULTIES` in `src/combat.js`, depending on the difficulty you've chosen.
- `world/places/` has one plain-text file for each place: what it's like, its districts, who's there, what could happen and what people say. `_how-to-write-a-place.txt` there explains every line.
- `world/species/` has one plain-text file for each kind of people a character can be, with what the character creator gives them, and their **Body** part. `_how-to-write-a-species.txt` there explains every line.
- `world/health/` has the bodies: `wounds.txt` has every kind of wound and old mark, and a file for a place (`meridian.txt`) has who starts the game hurt there. `_how-to-write-health.txt` explains every line. See [Bodies](#bodies).
- `world/stories/` has one plain-text file for each story that can come up: its lead, what's really going on, how it might go, its endings by name, the facts it leaves, what on the record changes it, and what happens if nobody steps in. *For anyone who isn't the captain* is how it reaches someone who isn't the captain, and a story without one stays the captain's own. `_how-to-write-a-story.txt` in that folder explains every line, and the **Story board** in the game shows and changes the same files as boxes and arrows.
- `world/arc.json` is the main story: its acts, its clues and its endings.
- `world/ship.json` has the Meridian: who's aboard and what they use up, what she starts with, the ration levels, what each shortage does, and everything she says in the story. `council` is what the officers and the council will do by themselves, and when. `chair` is how someone else in the captain's chair runs her, with each captain's own habits.
- `world/starmap.json` has the star map: where each place is, how dangerous it is, who controls it, and what the Meridian can buy there, at its usual price. `fare` and `passage` are what it costs to go there on someone else's ship.
- `world/starts.json` has every life you can start from: where it starts, the job and what it pays, who you know, where you stand with each faction, your first quest, and notes on who they are. Instructions are at the top.
- `world/daily.json` has what changes at each place from day to day: where it gets its supplies, how much of a say each side has, how prices follow supplies, and everything that can happen there. Instructions are at the top.
- `world/story-clock.json` has what happens to each story if nobody steps in, day by day, and the main story's own events. Instructions are at the top. It's full of spoilers.
- `world/maps/` has one file for each place, with every room drawn in characters, which you can edit like a picture. Move a letter on a level to move a person or a thing. The file also has each room's colors, what each thing says when you look at it, what happens when you use it, what's in it if it can be opened (`holds`), what you can first say to each person, and which doors are closed. Instructions are at the top of `meridian.json`. A place with no file here just stays in the classic view.
- `world/maps/meridian/` has the Meridian's three decks, drawn in Tiled, one file to a deck with its rooms drawn on it as rectangles, with the same things in them as a room in characters, and `world/maps/tiles/` has the stand-in tiles they're drawn with. A deck or room drawn in Tiled counts instead of one in the place's file. See [Drawing the ship in Tiled](#drawing-the-ship-in-tiled). They're read again whenever one is saved, with no restart.
- `world/life/` has one file for each place, with everyone's day: where they sleep, eat and work, and what they do at what time. Instructions are at the top of `meridian.json`. A place with no file here keeps its people at their posts, with no clock.
- `world/looks.json` has what each place and room is made of, and how it's lit, in the Isometric view. Instructions are at the top.
- `world/rooms.json` has the kinds of room the game can draw for anywhere that isn't on a map, such as ducts, a brig or a chapel, with the things you'd usually find in each. It also has what anyone can be asked in a talk, and how people look when you come over.
- `world/items/`, `world/factions/` and `world/perks/` have one plain-text file for each item, each faction and each perk, with a how-to in each folder. An item's `size`, `weight` and `stack` lines say how many squares it takes in the inventory, how heavy it is, and how many go on one square.
- `world/inventory.json` has the inventory's numbers: the size of the pockets, the pack, the belt, something that holds things and a pile on the floor, how much you can carry and how much faster too much tires you, each kind of item's usual size and weight, and what the people you beat might leave. A `loot` line in an enemy's or a person's file says what they leave instead. It's read when the game starts, so a change there needs a restart.
- `world/computers/` has one plain-text file for each computer the player can get into: its class, where it is, what's on it, what its programs do, and what it reaches. `_how-to-write-a-computer.txt` there explains every line, and `_flavor.json` has the words for the computers the game makes up. These are read again when the board saves one, with no restart.
- `world/systems/` has a file for each place that has systems of its own: who guards it and keeps it running, and each system with its parts and the rooms they're in. `_defaults.json` has what every other place gets, the numbers they all start from, and each kind of part's look and work by hand. `_how-to-write-systems.txt` explains every line. These are read again when the board saves one, with no restart.
- `world/logistics/` has the Meridian's logistics: where each of her stores is and what it holds, what each machine takes and makes and how often, when the sittings are, who carries what from where when a store runs short, what each system draws from the generator and the order they're let go in, and what's refilled from nothing, how often and how far. `_how-to-write-logistics.txt` there explains every line. It's read again when the board saves it, with no restart.
- `world/command/` has the Meridian's chain of command: each post, the room it's kept in, who chooses whoever holds it, the jobs it heads and the skills that count for it; the jobs and how many each wants; and who holds each post when a game begins. A council decision in `world/ship.json`, a guard or engineer in `world/systems/`, or an errand's carrier in `world/logistics/` can name a post (the quartermaster) instead of a person. `_how-to-write-command.txt` there explains it.
- `world/deeds.json` has the numbers for picking pockets and attacking people from the right-click menu: how hard a pocket is, what's in it, what being caught or attacking costs, who fights as what, and how long someone beaten is away. It's read when the game starts, so a change there needs a restart.
- `world/checks.json` has everything said in the skill check windows, and which game each skill uses. For hacking it also has the classes of computer with their numbers, what training adds, and the advisor's hints by level. It's read when the game starts, so a change there needs a restart.
- `world/combat-text.json` has everything that gets said in a fight.
- `world/parley.json` has everything about talking in a fight: every reason to fight and how much harder each makes each option, the options themselves, and what gets said. Instructions are at the top.
- `app/` has the app that the game runs in. See [The app](#the-app).
- `world/art.json` has the console art for fighters. Each figure is drawn facing right, and the game turns enemies around. A character's or enemy's `art` setting picks which figure they use.
- `data/campaigns/` has a folder for each campaign. People from your character's past get their files here, and so did anyone an older game's AI game master added. They're kept for good, and their stats are generated by the game. Rooms the story has invented are in its `rooms` folder, and you can give one its own drawing: Section Fourteen's file shows how. `captain.json` is a readable copy of your character's stats, and changing it does nothing.

Saved copies of the same campaign share a folder, and a new game starts with an empty one. Restart the game after changing a file.

## Reading the story

- **People:** everyone who speaks gets their own name label and color, with a colored bar beside their words. Speech over comms has a dashed bar and looks like a transmission, machines look like machines, and the Choir sounds like nothing else.
- **Lore:** underlined words are people, places and ideas the game can explain. Hover over one for a summary, including how they feel about you or your standing with them. Click it to open a person's file in **People**, or anything else in the **Codex**, which fills up as you come across things. On a phone, tap the word.
- **Consequences:** a boxed **Consequence** in the story marks something that will come back later.
- **Results:** colored lines show what your choice actually did: checks, items, money, standing and beliefs. Green is good news and red is bad.
- **Choices:** tags before a choice show what it involves. `[PERSUASION 60%]` is your chance on a skill check, `[MERCY]` is the belief it expresses (red if it goes against your convictions), `[VOSS +]` or `[TARROW -]` means someone will approve or disapprove, and `[! costs 200 credits]` is a known cost or risk. Hover over a tag for details.

To change the story's font and size, use **Reading** in the Settings menu. **Book** is easiest on the eyes, and **Terminal** keeps the whole game in the console style. **Story font** is for Classic, the whole story and the panel under the room, and **Popup font** is for [the story over the room](#the-story-over-the-room). **Text size** goes for both. **Story view** is there too: see [Walking around](#walking-around).

**Display** in the Settings menu sets how big everything is, so the game fits your screen:

- **Interface size:** **Automatic** makes everything bigger in a bigger window, including the room, so it looks the same on any screen. At the size the app first opens (1400 by 900) and smaller, everything's drawn at its normal size. **Small**, **Normal**, **Large** and **Largest** set it yourself. A size can't go past what still fits in the window, so **Largest** in a small window is no bigger than **Normal**. Your choice is remembered.
- **Full screen:** press **Go full screen**, or F11 in the app. You can also pull the edges of the window to make it any size, and the screens rearrange themselves to fit.

## Creating your character

Press **New game** and pick **Build from scratch**, **Surprise me**, or a ready-made character you can still change. Building from scratch takes you through these steps:

1. **Identity:** name, pronouns and appearance.
2. **Your place:** the captain, or one of 21 other lives. See [Who you are](#who-you-are).
3. **Species, homeworld and upbringing:** these set your starting attributes, skills, money and reputation.
4. **Attributes:** spend points on eight abilities. These decide your odds in the next step.
5. **Life simulation:** live your life in four-year terms, choosing a career each time. Each term rolls for survival and promotion, and gives you a decision to make. The choices shape your skills, beliefs, friends and enemies. Stop whenever you like. More terms mean more skills, but slower learning later, and aging starts to cost you after term 4. Your life ends at the Fall of the Concord.
6. **Skills:** spend points across 25 skills, and tag 3 that you'll improve faster.
7. **Traits:** choose strengths, paid for with points that flaws give back.
8. **Ideology:** set your beliefs on five axes. Strong beliefs become convictions, with real benefits and costs.
9. **Drive and bonds:** what you want most, and who you'd do anything for.
10. **Gear:** spend your savings. Whatever's left stays as credits.

Your life history is on your registry printout (press **C**). Your traits and beliefs change your numbers, and the written story can ask about them.

## How the simulation works

- **Checks:** the story picks a skill and a difficulty, then you play out the check in its own window. The game rolls a d20 and adds your attribute, skill, bonuses and what you earned in the window. You see the full breakdown each time.
- **Learning:** skills improve as you use them, as well as from the points you get when you level up.
- **Stress and fatigue:** frightening events and hard choices build stress, and time builds fatigue. Both give penalties when they get high, and enough stress causes a breakdown. Sleep to recover.
- **Injuries:** these have real penalties, and heal with time or treatment.
- **Beliefs:** your choices move your beliefs. Acting against a strong belief causes stress, and sharing a belief with someone helps your social checks with them.
- **The ship:** a day's numbers are all worked out in one place, and the Ship menu shows them, so what you read is what happens.

Saved games from before this system still load. They get a basic character profile, and you need to start a new game to use the creator. When you start a new game, your last one is kept as **Previous game** on the title screen.

## The story

The story is written in advance, in plain text files in `world/scenes/`, and the game plays it by itself. The choices are the ones on offer: click one, press its number, or type part of it. What the story can't answer, it says so, and nothing changes. For now that's the openings for three lives (the captain, an engine hand on the Meridian, and a dock hand at Haven Station), the Meridian's arrival at Haven, the whole of the main plot's first act with every way through it written out, and one story aboard the Meridian. The first act is **The Warm Room** at Haven, **Two Weeks of Fuel** at Kessara Drift and Cinder Reach, **Visiting Hours** on Anselm's Mercy, and the council meeting, **Where We're Going**, which ends the act once the fuel's found and the council has voted. The Meridian's story is **The Downstairs Crew**. The **Leads** menu offers them where they happen, written ones first, and never leaves a written one off. Anyone else gets a short opening while theirs is written, and a smaller side of each story. Each scene file says at the top whether it's the AI's draft or the owner's own writing. In the Leads menu, a story that hasn't been written out yet says so instead of offering **Look into this**, and it plays out by itself, by its clock. Walking, people's lives, the wider world, fights, skill checks, the ship and the menus all work as usual.

- **People:** walk up to anyone and they speak first. The Meridian's crew each have a written conversation of their own (`world/scenes/talk_<id>.txt`), with things to say that lead on, questions that go away once asked, and answers that change with what the story has done. Everyone else has stock lines, made from who they are, what they're doing and where you are, plus what they've heard.
- **Things and doors:** **Use** on a thing gives its written result or picks it up, and **Ask to go through** at a closed door follows that door's rule: some open when asked, some need something you've earned, and some open a skill check whose result decides. All 15 doors have a rule, and the bridge's things have results, with a plain answer anywhere that hasn't been written yet.
- **Writing it yourself:** open a file in `world/scenes/` with Notepad, change it and save. The game reads it again the next time the story gets there, with no restart. `world/scenes/_how-to-write-a-scene.txt` explains the format: parts, choices, checks, fights, what a part changes, and conditions such as `? has data_chip` or `? skill persuasion >= 3` that decide which choices are offered and where the story goes. A choice can lead to a check whose pass and fail go different ways, a part can remember a flag for any later scene, a part can wait for the Meridian to arrive somewhere, and a story can fill her tank, change her stores or move the main story on to its next act.
- **Finding mistakes:** run `npm run check-story` in the game's folder. It lists every problem with the line it's on, in plain words, and changes nothing. A file the game can't read is explained in the game too.

**The AI game master is gone.** Until 10 November 2026 the game could also have an AI game master, which wrote whatever wasn't written and answered whatever you typed. It was taken out, and the game runs on its own systems now. A game it was running opens as a written game: a line in the story says so, its last choices stop being offered, and a written scene that was under way carries on. Everything else in the save, the journal, the quests, the people you know and the record, stays as it was. Nothing goes online, and nothing costs anything to play.

## The story board

**Story board**, under **The story** on the rail, shows any of the 128 stories, 24 places, 61 people, 6 species, 34 enemies, 64 items, 12 factions, 53 perks, 6 computers, the systems of every place and the ship's logistics as boxes and arrows, and lets you change it by clicking. Pick what to look at and which one at the top. On **The story**, the boxes are the lead, what's really going on, the beats, the ways through, the checks and fights, the rewards, and the endings by name. Click an ending and the facts it leaves, what follows days later, and the other stories that react to it unfold beside it. What on the record changes this story sits on the left, and the clock's stages along the bottom. Click any box and its words open on the right, with **Keep** and **Undo**; names are made from the words, conditions are picked from lists, and the buttons add an ending, a fact, a consequence, a stage of the clock, or a reaction. A stage's or a consequence's changes at a place and aboard the ship are picked there too. Drag a box to move it, and the board remembers where you left it next time; **Tidy up** puts everything back in its columns. Drop a fact or a consequence on an ending to link them. Drag the dark background to look around, and use the **−**, **+** and **Fit** buttons for the size.

**The written scene** shows a story's scene file, when it has one: each box a part, each arrow a choice, a check's two ways, or a rule. Click a part and its lines open as they are in the file, with the words on its arrows; click an arrow to change where a choice goes, drop its words on another part to send it there, or drop a part on another part to give it a choice that leads there. **As the file** shows the same story written the way its file holds it.

**A place** shows what the player sees, the feel of it, its districts, what can happen there, and the people and stories here, each a box you can open. **A person** shows how the game sees them, their looks, personality, voice, goals and secrets, their numbers and how they fight, with boxes with arrows for every number and nothing to type, and where they belong: their place, their species, their stories, and **The written lines** tab when they have a talk written out. **A species** shows what the player reads, the notes on how they play, their numbers and effects, and the people who are one. **An enemy** shows what the player sees, what they say in a fight, one box of lines per moment, who they fight for and where they turn up, with their numbers in the box on the left. **An item** shows what the player reads, its effects, and where it's sold or found, with its type, price and numbers picked from lists. **A faction** shows what the player reads, what they believe, and their people, places, enemies and stories. **A perk** shows what the player reads, what it changes, with every number in a box with arrows, and the perks it needs or leads to. **A computer** shows what the player sees when they sit down, its files, letters, logs and programs, each a box with buttons to add more, and the computers it reaches over its network, with its class, place and login picked in the box on the left. **The systems** of a place show who keeps it running, with its guards and engineers picked from its people, each system with its parts, where you add a part by picking it and a room from lists, each computer with arrows to what it runs, and the rooms with what's in each. **The logistics** of the ship show each store with what it holds and the room it's in, and the machines, errands, loads and refills as boxes, each with arrows to the stores its words name; a store's line is written with its room and what it holds picked from lists, a machine's, an errand's, a load's or a refill's line in the words the folder's how-to explains, with the checker's complaint on the box when it can't be read.

**Save** writes the file in `world/stories`, `world/scenes`, `world/characters`, `world/places`, `world/species`, `world/enemies`, `world/items`, `world/factions`, `world/perks`, `world/computers`, `world/systems` or `world/logistics`, through the same reader the game uses, so a file that can't be read is never written, and the game uses the new version from the next turn. The checker's problems are marked in red on the boxes and listed on the right. Nothing is saved until you press Save, and Close asks again if something isn't. A changed person, item or faction reaches the codex when the game next starts.

## Drawing the ship in Tiled

The Meridian is drawn in **Tiled**, a free map editor, one file to a deck, and you can change the decks there or add rooms to them. Each deck is one continuous map with its rooms drawn on it, so you walk from the bridge to the names bulkhead and on down the deck without anything loading or jumping, and the stairs take you between decks. The three decks are laid out after the Anvil Carrack's arrangement, command on top, the civilian deck under it and engineering along the keel, as a starting point to redraw. Tiled shows a deck from the same angle as the Isometric view, built of plain stand-in blocks: grey walls, amber doors, teal consoles, brown crates, a green figure for each person, a gold diamond for each thing you can look at, a teal arrow for each stair, and a flag where you stand when the story puts you in a room. The game still draws its own picture. The blocks are only there so you can see what's where.

- **Getting Tiled:** it's free from [mapeditor.org](https://www.mapeditor.org). Its itch.io page asks for a price, and **No thanks, just take me to the downloads** gets it for nothing.
- **Opening the ship:** in Tiled, choose **File > Open File or Project** and open `world/maps/almagest-explorers.tiled-project`. The ship's decks are listed on the left, `deck1.tmj`, `deck2.tmj` and `deck3.tmj`. Double-click one.
- **A tile is a square of floor** the way the Isometric view draws it. The game's own squares are half as wide, so some tiles have something on one half only, such as a thin wall or a narrow door. A map can be 100 tiles across and 60 down.
- **The layers:** **Floor**; **Walls and things**, for walls, doors, consoles, crates and anything else in the way, where an amber door is a doorway between two rooms; **People and things**; **Words**, for words on the walls and the names painted on the floor; and **Rooms**, a rectangle round each room.
- **Drawing:** click the layer, pick a tile from **Stand-ins**, choose the **Stamp Brush**, and click or drag. The **Eraser** takes tiles away. Clicking a tile in the tileset shows what it is, under **what**.
- **The rooms:** each is a rectangle on the **Rooms** layer with its **Class** set to **Room**, drawn round the room, walls included. Its **Name** is what the room's called, and has to be its own; **id** is what the game's files call it, such as `bridge`, which the stories, everyone's days and the ship's systems go by; **desc** is what you read about it, and **aliases** its other names. Who's in a room, what's in it, what the story is waiting on there and whether its lights and air are on are all worked out from the rectangles, so dragging a person into another rectangle moves them rooms. A square in no rectangle belongs to the deck itself, which is fine for a passage.
- **People, things and stairs:** drag them with **Select Objects**, and each stands on the square under its feet. To add one, pick its marker in the tileset, choose **Insert Tile**, and click where it stands. Its properties say who or what it is: a person's **person** is the name of their file in `world/characters`; a thing has its **Name**, what you read when you **look**, and what you do when you **use** it; a way out's **to** is the deck it leads to, such as `deck2`, and you come out beside its way back. A flag inside a room is where the story puts you in it.
- **The deck itself:** **Map > Map Properties** has its name, what you read about it, and its colours.
- **Saving:** **Ctrl+S**, or **File > Save**. The game notices within a second or two, even while you're playing, and draws the deck again the next time the clock moves or you take a step. If a wall lands where you're standing, you're moved to the nearest floor.
- **A new room** is a rectangle on a deck: carve its walls and floor, put a door in, draw the rectangle round it on the **Rooms** layer and name it. **A new deck:** open a deck, choose **File > Save As**, save it in the same folder under the new deck's name, change it, and give another deck a way out to it.
- **If something's wrong,** such as a person with no **person**, a way out to a deck that isn't there, or two rooms with the same name, the game leaves that one thing out and draws the rest, and `npm run check-story` lists it in plain words.
- **Still in `meridian.json`:** the ship's name and the room it starts in. The deck plan, from the button in **What's here**, is made from the decks, one tab to a deck.
- **The other places** still have their rooms in their own files, drawn in characters, one room to a level, and a Tiled file with no **Rooms** layer is one room the same way. Any of them can move into Tiled exactly as they are, with `node tools/to-tiled.mjs` and the place's name, and be joined into decks from there.

`world/maps/_how-to-draw-rooms-in-tiled.txt` explains all of it, step by step.

## The app

The app is a window around the same game. It starts the same local server that `start-game.bat` does and shows the same page, so nothing about the game itself is different.

- **Changing the game:** on this computer, the app runs the game from this folder, so a change here shows up the next time you close the app and open it again. There's nothing to rebuild.
- **Changing the app itself:** everything in `app/` is built into the app, so a change there needs a new build. Run these two, then run `dist/Almagest-Explorers-Setup.exe`, which updates the app in place and leaves your saves alone:

```bash
npm install
```

```bash
npm run build-app
```

- **Giving it to someone else:** send them `dist/Almagest-Explorers-Setup.exe`. It holds a copy of the game as it was when it was built, without your saves. On a computer that doesn't have this folder, the app runs that copy, and keeps its saves in `%APPDATA%\Almagest Explorer's\data`. Windows warns about installers from unknown publishers unless you pay for a signing certificate, and each player needs their own API key.
- **Moving this folder:** the app asks where it went the next time it opens, because your saves are in it.
- **What the app keeps for itself:** `%APPDATA%\Almagest Explorer's\` has `game.log`, which is what the black window would have shown, and `app-state.json`, which says where the window was and where this folder is. The game was called The Last Meridian until version 0.5.0, and an app installed under that name kept these in `%APPDATA%\The Last Meridian\`, with the saves of anyone without this folder: the first run under the new name copies them across, and leaves the old folder as it was.
- **Uninstalling it:** use **Installed apps** in Windows Settings. That only removes the app, and this folder and your saves stay where they are.

`app/main.js` is the app, `app/choices.js` has the decisions it makes when it starts, `app/build.js` makes the installer, and `app/icon.ico` is its icon.

## Carrying on from another computer

The game's code and everything in `world/` are on GitHub, in a private repository. To carry on somewhere else, install [Node.js](https://nodejs.org) and [Git](https://git-scm.com), copy the game down with `git clone` and the address of your repository, and run these two in its folder:

```bash
npm install
```

```bash
npm start
```

Then go to http://localhost:3210. Two things stay on the computer they're on, on purpose:

- **Your saved games:** a new copy starts with none. To bring them along, copy `data/saves` and `data/campaigns` to the same place on the new computer.
- **`backups/` and `dist/`:** the old copies of the game and the installer. Git keeps the game's history from now on, and `npm run build-app` makes a new installer.
